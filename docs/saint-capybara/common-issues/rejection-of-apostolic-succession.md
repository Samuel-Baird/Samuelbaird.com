# Rejection of apostolic succession

## General response

LDS position: authority passed by ordination/succession is real (this is
why LDS priesthood ordination itself matters — see
`lds-priesthood-authority.md`), but the specific historical chain
claimed by Rome/Orthodoxy broke, requiring literal angelic restoration of
priesthood keys to Joseph Smith (D&C 13, 27:12-13; JS-H 1:68-72 for
Aaronic; Peter/James/John for Melchizedek).

## Catholic response

Engage the primary historical claim directly rather than hand-waving it.
Hans von Campenhausen's *Ecclesiastical Authority and Spiritual Power in
the Church of the First Three Centuries* is the standard non-LDS academic
treatment and documents that monepiscopacy (one bishop per city,
successor of a single apostolic line) itself developed gradually — see
his chapter ["Tradition and Succession in the Second
Century"](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/campenhausen-ecclesiastical-authority-and-58d4/161).
Even the *New Catholic Encyclopedia*'s own article on "Apostolic
Succession" concedes real historical-critical difficulties in the early
evidence (["there are three historical mistakes in the
letter..."](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/volume-1-fa64/603)) — citing
the Church's own reference encyclopedia conceding complexity is stronger
on screen than an outside critical source making the same point. Keep
the "early ≠ later form" discipline: some form of ordered ministry is
early; the fully worked-out doctrine of unbroken monepiscopal succession
as a guarantee of doctrinal authority is a later theological
interpretation of that early data, argued for in *Who Runs the Church?
4 Views on Church Government* (Zondervan Counterpoint) — a genuinely
contested question even among non-LDS scholars, not settled fact.

## Protestant response

Most Protestants already reject Roman/Orthodox apostolic succession as
the guarantor of doctrinal authority — use that common ground. Then push
further: if formal succession doesn't guarantee authority (the shared
Protestant premise), what *does* transmit authority to teach and to
perform binding ordinances? "Scripture alone" answers the doctrine
question but doesn't obviously answer the *authority-to-act* question
(who can validly baptize, ordain, administer the Lord's Supper) — this is
where LDS priesthood restoration offers an answer many low-church
Protestant traditions have historically struggled to give a clear one to.

## Exmo/atheist response

The evidentiary thinness the critic points to cuts against every
institution's succession claim equally, not LDS claims specifically:
the succession claimed by any single institution (Catholic, Orthodox,
or LDS) rests on documentary and archaeological evidence that gets
thinner the further back it goes, especially in the first two
centuries, which makes this a shared condition of the whole field
rather than an LDS-specific vulnerability — cite McDonald, *The Formation of the Biblical
Canon* vol. 2, on how thin and contested even canon-related apostolic
attribution is by the ["mid-second century after the eyewitnesses...were
gone"](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/mcdonald-the-formation-of-the-biblical-canon-29cb/35).
The honest framing: every tradition's authority claim, including LDS
restoration, ultimately rests on trust in specific claimed events (an
angelic visitation, in the LDS case) that isn't independently verifiable
by ordinary historical method — the question is which claim's
surrounding evidence (context, testimony, internal consistency, fruits)
makes it more reasonable to trust, not which claim alone has "proof."
