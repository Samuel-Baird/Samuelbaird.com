# Handling of sexual abuse cases, Abuse Help Line, clergy-penitent privilege

## General response

The Church operates a confidential help line bishops call for guidance
when abuse is disclosed; critics have documented specific cases (notably
the Bisbee, Arizona case) where local leaders reportedly failed to report
abuse to civil authorities, in some instances citing clergy-penitent
privilege exemptions in reporting law. State the specific documented
concern precisely rather than a vague "the Church covers up abuse"
framing, which overclaims beyond what any single case establishes.

### Mandatory-reporting resources (added 2026-09-18, user-supplied; used in `lds-mandatory-reporters`)

- **FAIR, "Mandatory reporting of abuse"** —
  https://www.fairlatterdaysaints.org/answers/Mandatory_reporting_of_abuse .
  Argues bishops act under clergy confessional privilege, that
  confidentiality encourages perpetrators to seek help, and that
  counseling plus membership restriction protects others. Cites General
  Handbook 38.6.2 and 32.2, and Cunningham, Raz and Walch (2022).
  **Caution:** its claim that reporting laws "may actually contribute to
  an increase in abuse" is weakly supported. The research we found (Penn
  LDI; Child Trends) shows universal reporting laws don't improve
  detection, not that they raise abuse, and none of it tests confession
  exemptions. Don't repeat that claim on screen; use the narrower
  confidentiality argument.
- **General Handbook (verified verbatim 2026-09-18):** 38.6.2 says "If
  members become aware of instances of abuse, they report it to civil
  authorities and counsel with the bishop." 38.6.2.7: "Church leaders and
  members should fulfill all legal obligations to report abuse to civil
  authorities," and "in many locations, any person who learns of abuse is
  required to report it." 38.6.2.1 sends bishops to the help line to
  "comply with legal requirements for reporting abuse." Gospel Topics /
  "Preventing and Responding to Abuse": leaders "should never disregard a
  report of abuse or counsel a member not to report criminal activity."
  **Don't dispute critics on this:** a critic who says the Handbook tells
  members to report is right. Critics sometimes cite "38.16.6" (no such
  section exists; the real one is 38.6.2) or the old "Handbook of
  Instruction" name; that is a garbled cite, not a false claim.
- **Utah (H.B. 432, 2024, enrolled text):** clergy are exempt only for a
  confession "made directly" by the perpetrator where clergy are bound to
  confidentiality; a cleric who learns of abuse "from any source other
  than confession of the perpetrator" must report.
- **Law:** Child Welfare Information Gateway, "Clergy as Mandatory
  Reporters" (2023): ~16 states require any person to report (Utah,
  Arizona's statute reaches "any person"); ~29 list clergy; privilege
  varies (West Virginia, New Hampshire deny it; Utah grants it).
- **Arizona Supreme Court, Doe v. Corporation of the President
  (CV-25-0213-PR, July 30, 2026):** unanimous; courts defer to a church's
  own definition of confession. Strongest critic point: the institution
  defines what it must report. Concede that as a legal debate, rebut the
  "protect predators" motive framing (Church says the bishop urged
  reporting).
- **Do not overclaim:** Church counsel's "more abuse surfaces where
  privilege exists" (Deseret News, 2025-08-08) is unverified by
  independent research.

## Catholic and Protestant response

Both traditions (Catholic institutions especially, given the scale of
documented clergy abuse scandals since the 1980s-2000s) have far more
extensive documented institutional failure in this specific area — this
context is relevant, not as a deflection ("others are worse") but because
it shows the underlying problem (institutional incentive to protect
reputation over victims, ambiguity in confidentiality/privilege law) is
structural across hierarchical religious institutions generally, and the
policy fixes worth advocating for (mandatory reporting regardless of
privilege claims, independent victim advocacy channels) are the same
across traditions.

## Exmo/atheist response

Engage the specific documented case and policy question directly and
without minimizing:

- Mormonr and FAIR both address the Bisbee, Arizona case directly rather
  than avoiding it — even FAIR (an apologetic organization) states the
  Church's "compliance with reporting obligations" applies "even in
  cases when the law provides clergy-penitent privilege or restricts"
  reporting in some jurisdictions —
  [source](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/failure-to-report-sexual-abuse-bisbee-arizona-3120/35)
  — meaning the Church's own defenders acknowledge the reporting-
  obligation question is real and specific, not a fabricated critical
  narrative.
- The help line's actual function is described directly: "when a leader
  calls the help line, the conversation is" [subject to legal privilege
  in most U.S. states, which critics argue can function to shield the
  institution from reporting duties rather than only to protect the
  confessor](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/failure-to-report-sexual-abuse-bisbee-arizona-3120/51) —
  state this mechanism plainly, since understanding exactly how the
  privilege functions is necessary to evaluate the criticism fairly.

**Certainty level:** that specific documented failures occurred (Bisbee
and other reported cases) is `fact`; whether these represent a systemic
pattern versus isolated local failures is `disputed` and deserves
direct, careful engagement rather than a confident claim in either
direction — this is a topic where overclaiming in the Church's defense is
especially costly to credibility.
