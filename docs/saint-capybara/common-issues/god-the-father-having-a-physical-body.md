# God the Father having a physical body

## General response

Core doctrine: "The Father has a body of flesh and bones as tangible as
man's; the Son also" (D&C 130:22), quoted constantly in modern teaching:

- Dallin H. Oaks, ["Apostasy and
  Restoration"](https://pulpit.restorationcommons.org/talks/1995-04-oaks-apostasy-and-restoration/#p14)
  (1995)
- Ronald A. Rasband, ["Let the Holy Spirit
  Guide"](https://pulpit.restorationcommons.org/talks/2017-04-rasband-let-the-holy-spirit-guide/#p8)
  (2017)
- David A. Bednar, ["The Times of Restitution of All
  Things"](https://pulpit.restorationcommons.org/talks/2025-04-bednar-the-times-of-restitution-of-all-things/#p11)
  (2025) — shows this is not an archaic teaching quietly dropped; it's
  still being taught verbatim.
- George F. Richards, Conference Address (1948), same language —
  continuity across a century of General Conference teaching.

Grounding text: Joseph Smith's First Vision account (JS-H 1:17) and
Stephen's vision (Acts 7:55-56) — both describe seeing a divine Father
figure, which the LDS reading takes at face value rather than as
metaphor.

## Catholic response

Catholic theology holds God is pure spirit / immaterial actus purus
(classical theism, drawing on Aristotelian metaphysics via Aquinas).
Don't just assert LDS is right and Aquinas is wrong — engage the actual
philosophical argument for divine immateriality (simplicity, act/
potency) rather than ignoring it. High-level sources for that framework:
Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I q.3; standard summaries in any Catholic
catechism treatment of divine simplicity.

LDS counter-materials: Blake Ostler's *Exploring Mormon Thought* vol. 3,
*Of God and Gods*, engages classical theism directly and argues the
Thomistic model creates its own coherence problems (e.g., relating an
absolutely simple, timeless being to a temporal creation). Use this to
challenge the *necessity* of classical theism's premises, not just assert
a counter-doctrine.

## Protestant response

Ask the sola scriptura question directly: where does the Bible state God
is immaterial, rather than describe him in bodily/anthropomorphic terms
throughout (Genesis 1:26-27 "image," Exodus 33:11 "face to face," Exodus
24:9-11 elders "saw the God of Israel")? The doctrine of divine
incorporeality is a philosophical import (largely Middle Platonist/
Aristotelian), formalized in later confessions (e.g., Westminster
Confession 2.1's "without body, parts, or passions") — not itself a
biblical phrase.

- FAIR, ["Mormonism and the belief in the corporeality of
  God"](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/mormonism-and-the-belief-in-the-corporeality-of-edc4/1)
  — collects the relevant biblical anthropomorphism texts and traces the
  post-biblical development of incorporeality as doctrine.

## Exmo/atheist response

This audience's version is usually less "is this heretical" and more
"this looks like 19th-century American folk religion/anthropomorphism,
not divine revelation." Take that framing seriously rather than only
rebutting the theological question:

- The historical point is true and is where the answer starts: this
  teaching developed and sharpened over Joseph Smith's career (compare
  early Book of Mormon / 1835 D&C language on the Godhead to the 1843-44
  King Follett-era teaching), and per the research-protocol's
  fact/inference distinction that fact carries the critic's conclusion
  only if development of doctrine is itself disqualifying, which it
  isn't (every tradition's theology developed, including Nicene
  Trinitarianism). Spend the beat on that inference, not on the fact.
- Terryl Givens, *Wrestling the Angel* (Oxford), gives the fullest serious
  historical-theological treatment of how this doctrine emerged and its
  intellectual context — useful for showing it wasn't ad hoc borrowing
  but a considered (if late-developing) theological position.
