# "Different Jesus" / different Christology

## General response

The claim usually bundles several distinct sub-claims (Jesus as a
literal, begotten, embodied Son of the Father rather than one being of
the Trinity; Jesus as "spirit brother" of Lucifer via premortal spirit
creation; a Christology tied to the LDS Godhead view) — separate them
before answering, since conflating them makes the response vaguer than
it needs to be. Gary J. Coleman, ["Mom, Are We
Christians?"](https://pulpit.restorationcommons.org/talks/2007-04-coleman-mom-are-we-christians/#p4)
(2007) is the standard General Conference direct response to this exact
charge.

**Added 2026-09-12 — a philosophy-of-language tool that undercuts the
claim at a more fundamental level than any of the sub-claims above.** The
"different Jesus" argument silently assumes *descriptivism*: that two
people are only talking about the same thing if their descriptions of it
match closely enough. That assumption is exactly what philosophers of
language (most influentially Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity*, and
Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth arguments) argue is false. Reference — who or
what a word or name actually picks out — is a different question from
whether what you believe about that thing is true. Someone can hold
false or unusual beliefs about a person and still be clearly referring to
that person: two people can argue for years about the details of
Einstein's biography, or Columbus's voyages, and neither one stops
talking about Einstein or Columbus by being wrong about them. Reference
tracks a causal-historical chain back to the actual person/thing, not a
checklist of matching properties.

Applied here: Latter-day Saints and traditional Christians disagreeing
about Jesus's *nature* (one substance of a Trinity vs. a distinct,
embodied Son) does not by itself establish that the two groups are
picking out two different referents. It establishes that they disagree
about the nature of the one historical person both groups are referring
to — the Jesus of Nazareth crucified under Pontius Pilate. The "different
Jesus" argument needs an additional, undefended premise (that reference
requires matching theological description) to get from "you believe
different things about him" to "you don't mean the same him." Putnam's
point about linguistic competence is worth naming too, as the honest
limit of this move: if a group's use of "Jesus" diverged enough from
what any historical claim about a first-century Jewish teacher
crucified under Pilate could mean, *that* would be a real referent
shift — but a disagreement over his metaphysical nature, while sharing
every historical claim about who, when, and how he died, isn't that
kind of divergence. Don't overclaim this into "therefore all religious
language refers to the same God regardless of belief" — the move only
works because the historical/biographical anchor (a first-century
person named Jesus, crucified under Pilate, believed by both groups to
have risen) is genuinely shared; it would not rescue a much more radical
redefinition.

This is a general-audience tool — it works for Catholics, Protestants,
and atheists/ex-Mormons alike (an ex-Mormon audience in particular tends
to have philosophical background where naming Kripke/Putnam by name adds
credibility rather than jargon) — so it belongs at the top of the
response before branching into the audience-specific material below,
which is about defending the LDS *description* of Jesus's nature once
"same referent, different description" has separated that question from
whether it's "even Jesus" at all.

## Catholic response

Catholic Christology (Chalcedonian: one person, two natures, of one
substance with the Father) is the historical target of this objection.
Route this into `lds-rejection-of-the-nicene-trinity.md`'s "early belief
vs. later creedal form" argument rather than re-deriving it — the
Christological question and the Trinity question are the same
underlying dispute about how much later conciliar precision is required
for the label "Christian."

## Protestant response

Ask what minimal Christological content a sola scriptura Protestant
actually requires from the text itself (divine Sonship, virgin birth,
atoning death, bodily resurrection, exclusive mediatorship — 1 Timothy
2:5) versus what requires the added technical vocabulary of Chalcedon.
LDS affirms every one of the biblically-stated claims; the dispute is
almost entirely over post-biblical metaphysical vocabulary, which is the
same fault line as the Trinity objection.

## Exmo/atheist response

For this audience the "different Jesus" language is usually lifted
directly from evangelical anti-Mormon rhetoric (2 Corinthians 11:4, "if
he that cometh preacheth another Jesus") and repurposed as a
"Mormonism isn't even real Christianity" argument, without independent
force of its own. Name that lineage plainly, then redirect to whatever
underlying historical or epistemic objection (Joseph Smith's authority,
continuing revelation) is actually motivating the claim — see the
playbook's audience note that ex-Mormon objections are usually
trust-claims in disguise.
