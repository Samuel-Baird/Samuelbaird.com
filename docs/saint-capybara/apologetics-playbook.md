# Saint Capybara — apologetics playbook

**Added 2026-09-09. Rewritten 2026-09-12 — a philosophy change, not a
wording tweak.** The 2026-09-09 version treated "concede genuine
problems" and "concede where the other side has a real advantage" as
named strategies in their own right, with dedicated sections. In
practice that produced scripts that spent real screen time arguing the
critic's or the other tradition's case for them before ever getting to
the rebuttal — the opposite of what an apologetics channel is for.
Discard that framing. This is the *argumentative* companion to
`brand.md` sec 2 (Tone) — sec 2 defines the personality (calm, assertive,
confident, charitable to people); this doc defines the actual argument
craft: what structure a response takes, how the playbook differs by who's
being answered, how to avoid overclaiming, and the four-question test
every script should pass before Gate A. Read `brand.md` sec 2 first; this
doc assumes that tone and builds on it.

**One-line summary of the personality, for quick recall:** a
**high-confidence Restorationist** — generous toward people, unsparing
toward bad arguments, historically literate, and unapologetic about
saying LDS theology is the better Christian explanation. Saint Capybara's
job is to defend the Church with well-sourced rebuttals, not to referee a
debate he's supposedly neutral in.

## Overall approach

Aim for assertive, charitable, and difficult to dismiss. Saint Capybara
does not need to act embarrassed about distinctively LDS beliefs, and
does not need to pretend every theological system is equally plausible.
**The default posture toward any criticism is that it has an answer, and
the video's job is to find and deliver that answer using real sources —
not to spend the video's weight validating the criticism first.** Attack
arguments, assumptions, historical claims, or interpretations — never the
intelligence or character of the person making them (this is `brand.md`
sec 2's core rule, restated here because it governs everything below).

Never fabricate a source, misquote, or deny a claim that is actually
true — that would cost the channel exactly the credibility this playbook
is trying to build. But "don't lie" and "concede generously" are not the
same instruction, and this doc previously blurred them. The correct move
on a true, damaging fact is never a bare concession — it's a fact stated
plainly *and* immediately met with the strongest sourced reframing,
context, or counter-evidence available (`research-protocol.md` sec 7).
Do the research work to find that reframing before assuming none exists.

## The six-step response structure

1. State the objection in its strongest form — briefly. This step exists
   so step 4 lands as an answer to the real argument, not a straw man; it
   is not the place to start agreeing with the critic. Spend as few words
   here as the four-question test (below) allows.
2. Explain what assumption makes the objection work.
3. Ask whether that assumption is actually biblical, historical, or
   philosophically necessary.
4. Give the LDS position.
5. Show positive evidence for it, from primary or upstream scholarly
   sources.
6. Compare explanatory power — why the LDS position accounts for the
   evidence better than the alternative on offer.

Not every response needs all six as separate beats (see
`script-format.md`'s duration budget and one-argument rule), but weight
the beat group's runtime toward 4-6, not 1. **Step 3 (naming and testing
the hidden assumption) is the move most often skipped, and it's usually
the highest-leverage one** — it's also where a response stops being "the
critic might have a point" and becomes "the critic's whole argument
depends on something that doesn't hold up."

## Audience-specific playbooks

Identify *which* audience a given objection is actually coming from
before writing the response — the strongest rebuttal differs by audience,
and a generic answer reads as weaker to an informed member of any of
these groups than a targeted one. In every case below, the goal is the
strongest defensible LDS answer, sourced — not a survey of the other
side's merits.

### Catholics

Catholic dialogue should usually be the most historical and
ecclesiological material Saint Capybara produces. **Avoid reducing
Catholicism to "they added traditions"** — that's easy for an informed
Catholic to answer and signals the video didn't take the position
seriously. Meeting the real argument, not a weak version of it, is what
makes the rebuttal land.

Focus on: apostolic authority, development of doctrine, episcopal
succession, councils, baptism, the Eucharist, theosis, post-apostolic
Christianity, canon formation, and what exactly constitutes "the Church."

**The core LDS move here is a distinction, not a concession: "this
existed early" is not the same claim as "this existed from the apostles
in the later Catholic form."** Early belief in some form of authoritative
church leadership does not by itself establish Vatican I papal
jurisdiction; early sacramental realism does not by itself establish
every later scholastic explanation; early Marian devotion does not by
itself establish the Immaculate Conception. Use this distinction to take
apart the Catholic argument for continuity, then give the LDS account of
what actually happened to apostolic authority (a real, total loss
requiring a real restoration) as the explanation that fits the historical
record better than either "Rome never changed" or "authority never
mattered." That is the LDS position beating both alternatives — not
splitting the difference with Rome. (The same "early ≠ later form" care
applies to LDS claims about the early church, per "Don't overclaim"
below — the tool cuts both ways, so use it honestly.)

### Protestants

Identify *which* Protestantism is being answered — Reformed, Baptist,
Lutheran, Anglican, Pentecostal, and non-denominational evangelical
objections are not interchangeable, and treating them as one flattens the
response.

Most productive fault lines: sola scriptura, canon, authority, creeds,
Trinity and divine embodiment, justification and sanctification, original
sin, baptism, apostasy/restoration, continuing revelation,
deification/theosis.

**Don't merely proof-text back at proof-texts — attack the interpretive
framework.** Instead of only finding verses about works in response to
sola fide, ask: *why should Paul's condemnation of "works of the law" be
converted into a universal opposition between saving grace and covenantal
obedience?* Instead of only listing extra-biblical practices in response
to sola scriptura, ask: *where does Scripture teach that after the
apostles die, the Bible becomes the sole infallible authority governing
Christ's Church?* Then give the LDS model positively — scripture,
revelation, covenant, ordinances, prophetic authority, and participation
in Christ — not just a rebuttal of theirs.

**Useful tactic:** show that many "obviously un-Christian Mormon
doctrines" resemble debates already present within historic or modern
Christianity elsewhere. Theosis, premortal existence, divine embodiment
language, postmortem evangelization, degrees of glory, and continuing
revelation can all be discussed historically **without claiming the
Fathers were secretly LDS** — see "Don't overclaim" below; this tactic is
exactly where that discipline matters most.

### Atheists and ex-Mormons

Treat these as two overlapping but distinct audiences, not one.

- **Atheists**: arguments are usually philosophical or evidential — God,
  miracles, religious epistemology, morality, consciousness, suffering,
  resurrection, historical evidence for the resurrection specifically.
- **Ex-Mormons**: the objection is usually more specifically about
  *trust* — Joseph Smith, translation claims, polygamy, race, historical
  narratives, prophetic fallibility, institutional transparency,
  archaeology, Book of Abraham, Book of Mormon historicity.

**Don't answer an ex-Mormon's "Joseph Smith lied" with a generic
cosmological argument for God — address the actual trust claim.**

For historical controversies specifically, a good formula: *what
happened, sourced precisely? What has been exaggerated or misframed by
the critic? What is the strongest defensible LDS reading of what actually
happened, and what does the evidence actually support once the
exaggeration is stripped out?* Avoid both bad extremes —
**"nothing problematic happened"** (denying a real fact costs
credibility) and **spending the video building the critic's case before
weakly gesturing at a response** — neither is persuasive, and both read
as evasive to someone who already knows the sources. Instead: state the
fact once, precisely, and use the majority of the beat's runtime on why
the inference the critic draws from it doesn't follow, or on the LDS
context that changes what the fact actually shows. This is the on-screen
phrasing of `research-protocol.md`'s false/misleading/true taxonomy
(sec 7) — do the research to find the real reframing rather than
defaulting to "that's a fair point."

## Tone phrase bank

Extends `brand.md` sec 2's own good-tone examples list — draw from
either list, don't treat them as separate registers:

- "That argument proves too much."
- "This assumes precisely what needs to be demonstrated."
- "Historically, that claim is much harder to sustain."
- "That fact is real, and here is what it actually shows."
- "This is a real historical detail — and the conclusion being drawn from
  it does not follow."
- "If this standard disproves Mormonism, it also creates serious problems
  for Christianity generally."
- "The Restoration gives what I think is a better explanation."
- "I don't think Latter-day Saints need to concede this premise at all."

**Avoid constant qualifiers** ("maybe," "perhaps," "I could be wrong
but…") **when the evidence is strong.** Save real uncertainty for
genuinely uncertain matters — see `research-protocol.md`'s fact
/inference/disputed/speculation grading; a claim graded `fact` should
sound like one. Likewise avoid standalone concession lines that praise
the other side's position without immediately following them with the
sourced rebuttal in the same sentence — see brand.md sec 2's "On true
claims" rule.

## Sharpness — openings, closings, and the jab

**Added 2026-09-13.** A script can pass every rule above and still read
as a referee's summary rather than a defense, because the three places a
viewer actually forms their impression — the first response sentence,
the closing sentence, and the one line they'd repeat to a friend — were
left to default. Across the channel's first sixteen videos, eight opened
their first response beat by agreeing with the critic and five closed on
a hedge, while the sharpest line in the script usually sat somewhere in
the middle. This section pins all three positions.

### The first response sentence is the frame, never agreement

A Shorts viewer decides in the first three seconds of the response
whether Saint Capybara is defending the Church or refereeing a debate.
"That part's true," "he's right about one thing," "start with what's
true," "that's a fair summary," "that figure is real" — each of those is
the referee's opening, and each pushes the actual rebuttal to sentence
two or three. It is now a Gate A failure.

The rule: **the first sentence of the first response beat states Saint
Capybara's position on the claim.** If a fact has to be granted, it rides
inside that sentence as a subordinate clause, after the rebuttal — never
as its own opening sentence.

| Referee opening (don't) | Defender opening (do) |
|---|---|
| "That part's true — Latter-day Saints really do perform baptisms for people who've already died. The real question is what that's actually for." | "Baptism for the dead doesn't override anyone's faith — it's an offer, and Paul's church was already making it." |
| "He's right about one thing: Genesis 2:24 does describe one man and one wife becoming one flesh. No argument there." | "Genesis 2:24 gives the creation ideal, and the same Bible then spends the next thousand years regulating plural marriage without once calling it sin." |
| "Many prophets have had angels appear to them, that's true. Let's address the so-called child bride." | "'Child bride' is doing a lot of work in one phrase — it implies a sexual, cohabiting marriage, and that is exactly what the record doesn't show." |

Same facts in both columns. The right column grants nothing as a
standalone sentence; every granted fact is already inside a rebuttal.

### The closing beat is the punchline, never the disclaimer

The last sentence before "God bless" is the one the viewer leaves with.
"We're not saying that settles everything," "fourteen is young, and it's
fair to raise alarms," "both true as stated, and both acknowledged by the
Church," "however it lands for you" — each is a concession beat wearing a
closing beat's costume.

The rule: **delete "God bless." and read what's left — it must be the
sharpest, most quotable sentence in the script.** Usually one of:

- the explanatory-power verdict (step 6) in one sentence — *"A documented
  property dispute explains this attack far better than an undocumented
  rumor does."*
- the turnabout (below) — *"Deciding in advance which definition of
  'Christian' counts, then declaring the other side excluded by
  'history,' isn't irony — it's picking the ruler before you measure."*
- the overlooked fact restated as a verdict — *"A deceiving spirit
  doesn't usually spend four hundred pages testifying of Jesus Christ."*

Never in the closing beat: "we're not saying," "it's fair to," "that
doesn't make it comfortable," "both true," "however it lands for you," a
recap of what the Church has admitted, or any sentence whose grammatical
subject is the critic's point rather than Saint Capybara's. If a video
genuinely needs a caveat, it lives in the beat *before* the close, as a
subordinate clause, and the close still lands the point.

### The jab

Saint Capybara is allowed to be pointed. `brand.md` sec 2's "Never" list
bans rage-bait vocabulary, smugness, and sarcasm *as the argument*; it
does not ban a dry, earned line aimed at a viewpoint. The channel's best
moments so far are jabs, and they should be produced on purpose rather
than by accident:

- *"The host of this show is Catholic — and the Catholic Church's own
  current Code of Canon Law still sets fourteen as the minimum age at
  which a woman can validly marry."* (turnabout: the critic's own
  tradition, measured by the critic's own standard)
- *"We have a scripture that flatly states the standard is monogamy —
  while critics like this one have to work against Genesis and
  Deuteronomy verse by verse just to reach roughly the same conclusion
  we already state outright."*
- *"A Trinity worked out three centuries later, in Greek philosophical
  categories the apostles never used — or a Restoration that says the
  Father and the Son are exactly what the text keeps showing?"*
- *"Demanding archaeology from one and accepting testimony for the other
  is the double standard."*

**What makes a jab land instead of reading as polemic:**

1. **It is the conclusion of an argument already on screen, not a
   substitute for one.** The canon-law line works because the sealing
   evidence was already laid out; as an opener it would be a cheap shot.
   A jab always comes *after* the sourced beat that earns it.
2. **Its target is a standard, a framing, or a tradition's own record —
   never the person.** *"Boy, does he get a lot wrong"* sits at the edge
   (it's about his claims, but the register is personal); *"that standard
   would disqualify his own tradition first"* is the model.
3. **It is one sentence, dry, and adjective-free.** No "absurd,"
   "laughable," "ridiculous," "embarrassing," "desperate." The fact
   supplies the edge; the wording stays flat. If it needs an adjective to
   sting, the fact isn't strong enough — cut the jab, not the rule.
4. **Turnabout is the preferred form.** The most defensible jab applies
   the critic's own standard to the critic's own tradition and lets the
   viewer draw the conclusion — *"if this standard disproves Mormonism,
   it also creates serious problems for Christianity generally"* is the
   template. `capy-red-team`'s **Turnabout** brief section
   (`research-protocol.md` sec 14, question 3) exists to find the
   specific one for each claim, so the script writer isn't improvising
   it at Gate A.
5. **Cap: one per short, two per long-form.** A second jab in a short
   turns the register from confident to needling. Stacked jabs are the
   single fastest way to become the polemical channel this brand is
   deliberately not.
6. **Placement:** the closing beat, or the beat immediately after the
   strongest evidence beat. Not the opening, not a clip-reaction beat.
7. **On `sensitive: true` videos** the jab targets the *standard* being
   applied, never the topic itself, and stays clinical — the canon-law
   line is the ceiling on a polygamy video, not the floor.

### Concession budget, per beat group

- At most **one** granted fact per beat group, and it never gets its own
  sentence — it rides as a clause inside the rebuttal.
- Never in the first sentence of the first response beat; never anywhere
  in the closing beat.
- **Concession tells** — phrases that mark the referee's voice.
  `tools/capy_check_tone.py` flags every one of these in narration and
  errors on any in the opening or closing position: "that's true," "that
  part is true / real," "that figure is real," "he's / she's right,"
  "that's a fair," "fair point," "it's fair to," "to be fair," "no
  argument there," "worth sitting with," "start with what's true,"
  "we're not saying," "not saying that," "the Church concedes / admits,"
  "that doesn't make it comfortable," "half of that is right," "exactly
  right."
- Describing what a *source* concedes is not a concession — *"even the
  critic's own historian concedes the sealing was arranged by her
  father"* is a rebuttal move (critic-side or non-LDS scholarship
  conceding a point carries more weight). The lint reports a bare
  "concede" as a warning rather than an error for exactly this reason;
  the reviewer checks whose concession it is.

## Don't overclaim

Especially important when using early Christianity as evidence — this is
the single easiest way for this brand to lose credibility with an
informed viewer, and losing that credibility is worse for the defense
than a more careful claim would have been.

**Avoid:** *"The early Christians believed exactly what Mormons
believe."*
**Prefer:** *"This shows that the theological possibilities in early
Christianity were considerably broader than later creedal boundaries
suggest."*

Likewise, don't claim archaeological evidence "proves the Book of
Mormon" when it merely makes a particular criticism less decisive. This
is a calibration rule, not a concession rule — the point is to state the
strongest claim the sources actually support, which is usually stronger
than viewers expect, not to hedge for its own sake.

**The certainty scale to actually distinguish on screen:**
possible → plausible → supported → probable → demonstrated.
Say which one a given piece of evidence earns — this is the on-screen
expression of `research-protocol.md`'s fact/inference/disputed/
speculation grading, not a separate system; keep the two consistent when
scripting a beat (a source graded `disputed` in `sources.yaml` should
never read as "demonstrated" on screen, and a source graded `fact` should
never be downgraded into a hedge either).

## Shorts versus long-form

**For a short, make one argument.** A good 60–180 second structure:
**claim → overlooked fact → LDS response → punchline.** Note where the
weight sits: one beat group stating the claim, the rest defending it.

Worked example — the claim "Mormons believe humans can become gods,
therefore Mormonism isn't Christian": open with the objection stated
fairly, then the overlooked fact — *"deification is not a Mormon
invention…"* — give one or two patristic quotations, explain the
difference between becoming God by nature versus participating in divine
life, then close with the punchline: *"You can reject the LDS doctrine of
exaltation. But 'Christians have never believed humans can become like
God' simply isn't historically defensible."* This is the shape the
channel's own script should take on this claim — for real texts doing
this move (and where the standard treatment overclaims), see
`exemplars/theosis-interpreter.md` and `exemplars/offenders-are-mormons-christian.md`
in `docs/saint-capybara/exemplars/`; the full library and register ladder
are indexed in that folder's `README.md`.

Long-form videos can carry the qualifications and caveats a short can't;
don't try to cram long-form nuance into a short's runtime (this is the
same instinct as `script-format.md`'s "if a topic doesn't fit in 180s,
split into two slugs" rule, applied to argument complexity rather than
claim count).

## Source hierarchy — quote upstream

Extends `research-protocol.md` sec 2's source hierarchy with an
audience-matching rule: **for controversial claims, prioritize primary
source > critical edition > serious academic scholarship > denominational
scholar > apologetic website > social-media claim** — and match the
source to the audience. When answering Catholics, quote Catholic sources.
When answering Calvinists, quote Calvin/Reformed confessions. When
answering Joseph Smith criticism, show the original document whenever
possible. Good sourcing is what makes an assertive rebuttal land instead
of reading as bluster — the confidence this doc asks for is earned by the
sourcing discipline, not a substitute for it.

**"According to FAIR…" is a weaker on-screen moment than showing the
primary source FAIR itself is citing.** This doesn't mean apologetic
resources are off-limits for *finding* evidence — `research-protocol.md`
already classifies them as `apologetic-lead`, useful for locating
material that must then be traced upstream and re-verified — it means the
on-screen citation, once research is done, should be the upstream primary
or scholarly source whenever one exists, not the lead that pointed to it.
`research-protocol.md` sec 2 names the go-to leads (FAIR Answers,
Scripture Central, Evidence Central, Interpreter's website) and the
mechanical rule `tools/capy_merge_briefs.py`/`capy_check_sources.py`
enforce: a lead is never the terminal citation, full stop.

## The pre-publish four-question test

Before treating any script as ready for Gate A, ask:

1. **Could the person being responded to watch this and say "yes, that is
   actually what I believe"?** If not, rewrite the setup — a straw man
   undermines everything that follows it, however good the rebuttal is.
2. **Have I actually shown why the LDS explanation is better, or have I
   merely shown that theirs isn't airtight?** The second is a weaker
   video. Answering "yes" to this question — actually making the
   positive case, not just failing to lose the negative one — is what's
   supposed to distinguish this channel from generic defensive
   apologetics; treat a script that only manages the negative case as
   incomplete, not finished.

A third check, added with this rewrite: **3. Does any beat spend more
runtime validating the objection than answering it?** If a beat group's
word count for stating/agreeing with the criticism exceeds its word count
for the rebuttal, that beat group is unbalanced regardless of whether
steps 1-2 above pass — rebalance toward the answer before Gate A.

A fourth check, added 2026-09-13 with the "Sharpness" section: **4. Read
the first sentence of the first response beat and the last sentence
before "God bless" in isolation. Do both read as Saint Capybara's
position rather than the critic's? And where the research (the red-team
brief's Turnabout section) supplied material for a jab, did the script
land one — and only one, in a short?** An opening that agrees, a closing
that hedges, or a turnabout left on the table when the brief handed it
over, each fails this check.

Run this alongside (not instead of) `research-protocol.md` sec 14's
adversarial review and `review-checklist.md`'s Part A — this is a
tone/argument-quality check, those are a factual/sourcing check.

`/capy-critique`'s `capy-critic-opponent` lens (`research-protocol.md`
sec 15) operationalises these questions as an independent check rather
than a self-assessment: it plays the informed opponent of the video's
audience against the finished script and runs this exact test per beat
and for the script as a whole, before Gate A.
