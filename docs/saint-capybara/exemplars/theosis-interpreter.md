## The passage

Marshals real patristic support for theosis as a positive case that LDS exaltation
doctrine isn't a modern deviation: Athanasius's "God became man, so that man might
become God," plus Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Basil, and Gregory of Nyssa, arguing
belief that humans can become like God is "one of the oldest and most fundamental
teachings of Christianity." Turnabout: critics who call LDS theosis "not Christian"
are, on this reading, opposing a strand already present in the earliest post-New
Testament fathers, not a later Mormon invention.

## Take

- **The positive case is real and citable**: patristic theosis material genuinely
  exists and genuinely predates Joseph Smith; this is legitimate ammunition for "not a
  modern invention."

## Do NOT take — the exact overclaim risk `apologetics-playbook.md` warns about

- **The review never engages the actual doctrinal gap.** LDS theology adds a claim
  the fathers quoted here did not make: that God the Father was himself once a
  mortal man who progressed to godhood. Patristic theosis is participation in an
  eternally-uncreated God's nature by grace; it is not a claim that God was once a
  creature. Citing Athanasius for "humans can become like God" while staying silent
  on "and God was once like us" is exactly the move `apologetics-playbook.md`'s "Don't
  overclaim" section flags: *"The early Christians believed exactly what Mormons
  believe"* is the overclaim; *"the theological possibilities in early Christianity
  were broader than later creedal boundaries suggest"* is the defensible claim.
- **A script modeling this exemplar's positive case must also do the honest part
  this review skips** — name the distinction and say plainly which piece of LDS
  doctrine the patristic parallel does and doesn't cover.

## Beat translation

*"'God became man so that man might become God' — that's not a Mormon slogan, that's
Athanasius, the fourth century's fiercest defender of the Trinity. He's not saying
God was once a mortal man. We add that claim. But 'humans becoming like God' being
somehow un-Christian? That part's had a much longer shelf life than critics think."*
