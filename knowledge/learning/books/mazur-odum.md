# Mazur & Odum, *Learning and Behavior* (9th ed.) — chapter digest

Mazur, J. E., & Odum, A. L. (2023). *Learning and Behavior* (9th ed.). Routledge.

Orientation: a behavior-analytic textbook that builds from habituation and classical conditioning through operant conditioning, stimulus control, observational and motor-skill learning, and choice, always seeking general principles from controlled laboratory work (mostly with animals) and then showing applications in therapy, education and everyday life, with cognitive and neuroscience perspectives set beside the behavioral ones. All page numbers are printed pages. This digest is a paraphrased study aid, not a substitute for the book.

## Ch 1 — History, Background, and Basic Concepts (pp. 1–27)

**Core ideas**
- Learning is a change in an individual produced by experience; the field studies both the acquisition process and the lasting behavior that results, in all ages and species, not just classrooms (pp. 1–2).
- The research strategy is to study a few simple, well-controlled situations in depth and extract general principles expected to hold across species and settings; laboratory control is defended the way a vacuum chamber is for studying falling bodies, and each chapter pairs lab principles with applied examples (pp. 2–4).
- The Associationists (Aristotle; Locke; James and John Stuart Mill; Brown) held that thoughts link by contiguity, similarity and contrast; Brown added duration, vividness, frequency and recency. Mill's idea that simple ideas fuse into complex ones fits some learning (multiplication built from addition) but not all (a toddler knows "house" without its parts) (pp. 4–7).
- Ebbinghaus tested these ideas with nonsense syllables and a "savings" measure (reduction in repetitions needed to relearn). Practice past one perfect recitation (overlearning) improved savings after 24 hours; forgetting is steep soon after study and then levels off; relearning rearranged lists showed that items closer together in the original list were more strongly associated (pp. 7–11).
- Behaviorism (Watson) restricts psychology to observable stimuli and responses. Skinner objected to unobservable intervening variables as unnecessary and as false explanations (e.g., "lazy") that discourage looking for changeable environmental causes; Neal Miller countered that with several independent and dependent variables an intervening variable (thirst) shrinks a theory from nine relationships to six. The authors judge a theory by its predictions (pp. 11–15).
- Animal subjects offer control over prior experience, convenience, no subject effects and comparative simplicity; costs are that some uniquely human abilities cannot be studied and there are ethical concerns, handled by oversight committees and the Three Rs. Within-subject designs and replication underpin confidence in the findings (pp. 8, 11–13).
- Sensory receptors break the world into simple features, and cortical feature detectors (lines, angles) are partly innate yet shaped by experience: kittens reared among stripes of one orientation developed more detectors for it (pp. 18–20).
- Learning changes the brain through chemical strengthening of existing synapses (long-term potentiation), growth of new synapses and dendrites (enriched-environment rats, juggling learners) and growth of new neurons; whether complex ideas are stored diffusely (Lashley) or in localized regions (category-specific deficits after injury) is unresolved (pp. 20–23).

**Key terms**: process vs. product of learning, acquisition, Associationism, contiguity, similarity, contrast, Empiricism, Nativism, tabula rasa, complex idea, nonsense syllable, savings, overlearning, forgetting curve, behaviorism, introspection, intervening variable, subject effect, Three Rs, neuron, synapse, feature detector, long-term potentiation, neurogenesis, within-subject design.

**Notable studies / people**
- Aristotle (c. 350 B.C.) and Brown (1820): principles of association (pp. 5, 7).
- Ebbinghaus (1885): savings, overlearning, forgetting curve, rearranged-list test of contiguity (pp. 7–11); replicated by Murre & Dros (2015) (p. 8).
- Skinner (1938, 1950) vs. Neal Miller (1959) on intervening variables (pp. 13–15).
- Hubel & Wiesel (1963, 1965, 1979): feature detectors present in naive kittens (p. 19); Blakemore & Cooper (1970): rearing environment shifts detectors (p. 20).
- Bliss & Lomo (1973): long-term potentiation (p. 20); Rosenzweig and colleagues (1966, 1972): enriched rats had heavier cortex (p. 21); Spinelli et al. (1980): leg-flexion training increased dendritic branching (p. 21); Draganski et al. (2004): juggling (p. 21); Gould et al. (1999): learning-related neurogenesis (p. 22); Lashley (1950): small cortical lesions did not erase maze memory (pp. 22–23).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Build in overlearning: Ebbinghaus's savings after a day grew when he kept practicing beyond the first perfect recall, so plan extra practice past first success when durability matters (p. 9).
- Expect steep early forgetting that flattens, which argues for an early first review (the book shows only the curve shape; the spacing prescription is outside the book) (pp. 9–10).
- Explain a failing learner by changeable environmental arrangements rather than a trait label, as in the homework-before-video-games example (p. 14).

## Ch 2 — Innate Behavior Patterns and Habituation (pp. 28–53)

**Core ideas**
- Control-systems theory describes goal-directed behavior as a feedback loop: a comparator checks the actual input against a reference input and drives an action system, whose output feeds back and can be disrupted by disturbances; the book reuses this scheme throughout (pp. 29–30).
- Innate patterns range in complexity. Reflexes are stereotyped movements of a body part (the flexion reflex runs through sensory neurons, interneurons and motor neurons in the spinal cord); tropisms move the whole animal as kineses (activity level depends on the stimulus, direction is random, as in wood lice seeking humidity) or taxes (movement direction relates to the stimulus, as in a maggot leaving light or an ant using the sun) (pp. 30–32).
- Fixed action patterns are species-typical sequences that run to completion in a rigid order, shown unlearned by deprivation experiments (a hand-raised squirrel went through nut-burying motions on a bare cage floor), triggered by a sign stimulus; exaggerated "supernormal" stimuli can beat the natural one (oystercatcher and oversized egg) (pp. 33–34).
- Reaction chains are sequences in which each response produces the stimulus for the next; they stall if the needed stimulus is absent and skip early steps if a later stimulus is supplied (hermit-crab shell selection), making them more adaptable than fixed action patterns (pp. 34–35).
- Humans have few fixed patterns but may have inborn predispositions: language-related brain specializations, universal recognition of basic facial emotions, and a long list of cultural universals. Universality alone does not prove innateness, because shared environmental demands could explain it (pp. 35–38).
- Habituation, a decline in response to repeated harmless stimulation, filters out the irrelevant and occurs from protozoa to people with shared properties: decrements shrink as trials proceed, responses recover with time, relearning is faster, weaker stimuli habituate faster, practice beyond zero response strengthens retention ("below-zero" habituation), and habituation generalizes to similar stimuli (pp. 39–41).
- Infant researchers use habituation to test what babies treat as "the same"; faster early habituation correlates with later cognitive scores, and slower habituation with some disorders, but only correlationally (pp. 41–42).
- In the sea snail Aplysia, habituation is reduced transmitter release by sensory neurons (weaker calcium current) with no new structures; mammals show similar changes on the sensory side of reflex circuits, plus cortical, hippocampal and cerebellar habituation (pp. 42–45).
- Opponent-process theory: an emotional reaction is the a-process minus a slower b-process; with repetition the b-process grows, so the initial reaction weakens while the after-reaction strengthens and lasts longer. It is applied to shocked dogs, parachutists and drug tolerance and withdrawal, with mixed support and the criticism that the time scales differ enormously (pp. 45–49).

**Key terms**: control systems theory, comparator, reference input, actual input, action system, disturbance, reflex, interneuron, tropism, kinesis, taxis, fixed action pattern, sign stimulus, supernormal stimulus, deprivation experiment, reaction chain, human universals, habituation, orienting response, below-zero habituation, simple systems approach, plasticity, opponent-process theory, a-process, b-process, tolerance.

**Notable studies / people**
- Fraenkel & Gunn (1940): wood lice kinesis (p. 32); Eibl-Eibesfeldt (1975): isolated squirrel (p. 33); Reese (1963): hermit-crab reaction chain (p. 34).
- Pinker (2002), Ekman (1973), Brown (1991, 2013) on innate human tendencies (pp. 36–38).
- Thompson & Spencer (1966): principles of habituation (p. 40); Johnson & Aslin (1995): infants and a partly hidden rod (p. 41).
- Kandel and colleagues (Castellucci et al., 1970; Klein et al., 1980): Aplysia (pp. 42–44); Davis (1989): rat startle (p. 44).
- Solomon & Corbit (1974), Church et al. (1966): opponent-process theory and dogs' heart rate (pp. 45–48).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Repeated identical stimuli stop being attended to, weak or varied stimuli habituate quickly, and attention recovers after a gap, so vary the presentation of repeated explanations and warnings rather than relying on repetition alone (pp. 40–41).
- Habituate-then-test designs give a quick check of what learners treat as "same" or "different": repeat one example, then watch for reaction to a changed one, as with infants (p. 41).
- Opponent-process logic suggests repeated exposure to an aversive or exciting event shrinks the initial reaction but not necessarily the after-effect, so look at both phases when judging anxiety or motivation (pp. 47–48).

## Ch 3 — Basic Principles of Classical Conditioning (pp. 54–78)

**Core ideas**
- Pavlov noticed that dogs experienced with the testing procedure salivated before food arrived because stimuli that reliably preceded food had come to elicit the reflex. In the standard paradigm a neutral conditioned stimulus (CS) is paired with an unconditioned stimulus (US) that triggers an unlearned response; the response to the CS alone is the conditioned response (CR) (pp. 54–55).
- Common laboratory preparations: eyeblink conditioning (slow; impairment predicts later Alzheimer's), conditioned suppression (fast, sometimes after one pairing), the human skin conductance response (words and pictures can serve as CSs), and taste-aversion learning (flavor paired with illness; over half of surveyed students had one) (pp. 56–58).
- Stimulus substitution theory (the CS stands in for the US) fails because the CR is rarely a copy of the UR: it can be smaller, only part of the UR, include extra orienting behavior, or even be opposite (heart rate falling to a shock-paired CS). Sign-tracking theory says the CS becomes a signal that animals orient toward and approach (pp. 58–59).
- Rescorla's US-devaluation experiment (habituating rats to the noise US after conditioning weakened the CR to the light) favors learning of a CS–US (S–S) association over a direct CS–response link (p. 60).
- Extinction does not erase learning: spontaneous recovery after a rest, disinhibition by a novel distractor and faster reacquisition all show the original association survives. Accounts include new inhibitory learning, loss of attention, an ambiguous CS, and partial weakening (pp. 62–63).
- A CS can become inhibitory if it signals that the US will not occur; a true inhibitor suppresses responding to other excitatory CSs. Generalization yields a gradient of responding by similarity (brand-name generalization in advertising), and discrimination training sharpens responding to the reinforced stimulus (pp. 63–64).
- Timing matters: short-delay conditioning (CS onset shortly before the US) is best; simultaneous and backward arrangements are weak; trace and long-delay conditioning weaken as the interval grows. The working rule is predictiveness: a CS predicting the US becomes excitatory, one predicting its absence inhibitory. Animals also learn when the US will come (pp. 67–68).
- Contingency, not just pairing, counts: a CS is excitatory when the US is more likely with it than without, neutral when equally likely, inhibitory when less likely (p. 69). In second-order conditioning a CS gains power from an already effective CS; evaluative conditioning shifted liking of faces paired with positive or negative words even when the pairings were not remembered (pp. 69–70).
- Applications: emotional reactions to everyday cues are conditioned (pp. 70–71). Phobias can arise through conditioning (Little Albert), but susceptibility, prior positive experience and social transmission matter too. Systematic desensitization builds a fear hierarchy, trains relaxation, and moves up the hierarchy only once each item no longer evokes fear (reported 80–90% success; real exposure, humor and virtual reality are variants; graduated VR exposure outperformed usual treatment for soldiers with PTSD). The bell-and-pad method for bedwetting pairs bladder sensations with waking and works about 80% of the time (pp. 71–74).

**Key terms**: classical conditioning, US, UR, CS, CR, conditioning preparation, eyeblink conditioning, conditioned suppression, skin conductance response, taste-aversion learning, stimulus substitution, sign-tracking, S–S vs. S–R association, US devaluation, acquisition, asymptote, extinction, spontaneous recovery, disinhibition, rapid reacquisition, conditioned inhibition, compound CS, generalization gradient, discrimination, short-delay / simultaneous / trace / long-delay / backward conditioning, temporal coding, CS–US correlation, second-order conditioning, evaluative conditioning, systematic desensitization, fear hierarchy, virtual reality therapy, bell-and-pad method.

**Notable studies / people**
- Pavlov: salivary conditioning and most basic phenomena (pp. 54–55, 62–64); Zener (1937) and Black (1965): CR differs from UR (p. 58); Rescorla (1973): US devaluation (p. 60); Rescorla (1966, 1968): CS–US correlation (p. 69).
- Gerwig et al. (2010): eyeblink acquisition with and without cerebellar damage (p. 61); Liu (1971): generalization gradient (p. 64).
- Ader & Cohen (1975): conditioned immune suppression; Goebel et al. (2008): conditioned allergy relief (pp. 65–66).
- Watson & Rayner (1921): Little Albert (p. 71); Paul (1969), Kushner (1968), Ventis et al. (2001): desensitization (p. 72); McLay et al. (2011): VR for PTSD (p. 73); Mowrer & Mowrer (1938): bell-and-pad (p. 74).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Extinction does not erase learning, so correcting a wrong response or an anxiety needs continued practice of the new response; expect the old one to return after a delay or in a new context (pp. 62–63).
- For test or subject anxiety, a graduated hierarchy with calm practice and advancement only after the current step is comfortable follows the desensitization logic (p. 72).
- Cues that reliably predict what follows acquire power, while cues present equally with and without the event do not; and emotional associations form even without recall of the pairing, so classroom atmosphere can shape attitudes (pp. 67, 69–70).

## Ch 4 — Theories and Research on Classical Conditioning (pp. 79–105)

**Core ideas**
- Kamin's blocking experiment broke the frequency principle: a tone paired with shock the same number of times produced almost no fear if a light had already been conditioned as a predictor, because the tone added no new information. Pairing alone does not guarantee learning; the CS must be informative (p. 80).
- The Rescorla-Wagner model treats learning as driven by surprise on each trial: when the actual US exceeds the expected US, present CSs gain excitatory strength; when it falls short they gain inhibitory strength; when it matches nothing is learned. Larger discrepancies mean more learning, salient CSs learn faster, and expectations from several CSs add up (p. 81).
- The model explains negatively accelerated acquisition, blocking (no surprise), extinction, conditioned inhibition (a new CS added to an extinction compound goes below zero) and overshadowing (the salient CS takes most of the available strength) (pp. 81–84), and predicted the counterintuitive overexpectation effect: two separately trained CSs presented together with the single original US lose strength despite extra pairings (pp. 84–85).
- It cannot explain the CS preexposure effect (conditioning is slower after unpaired exposures to the CS). Attention theories (Mackintosh; Pearce & Hall) explain it by reduced attention to an uninformative CS; evidence is mixed, so both CS and US effectiveness may change (pp. 85–86).
- Comparator theories say responding depends on comparing a CS's strength with that of contextual stimuli, predicting hidden learning that appears when a competing stimulus or the context is extinguished; some experiments confirm this learning-versus-performance distinction, others do not (pp. 86–87).
- Neural work: in Aplysia conditioning increased sensory-neuron transmitter release (opposite to habituation) and also postsynaptic sensitivity and new synapses; in vertebrates CR and UR pathways differ (cerebellum for rabbit eyeblink CRs), many structures participate, and different phenomena and response systems depend on different areas (hippocampus for blocking in rabbits). No simple physiological explanation exists (pp. 89–92).
- Biological constraints: Garcia showed taste aversion forms with delays of hours and that rats link taste to illness but audiovisual cues to shock, contradicting equipotentiality; Seligman called these prepared and contraprepared associations, and species differ (quail use color). The author argues these are differences in time scale and speed, not different laws: contiguity still operates and blocking, extinction and overshadowing occur in taste aversion (pp. 93–97).
- Conditioned compensatory responses explain part of drug tolerance (Siegel): cues that regularly precede a drug evoke an opposing reaction, so tolerance is context-specific and the same dose in a new setting can be far more potent, helping to explain some unexpected overdoses. Cues also trigger craving; cue exposure treatment works best across several realistic contexts and repeated sessions because of spontaneous recovery (pp. 97–100).
- Conditioned opponent theory (Schull) says only the b-process is conditioned; Wagner's SOP predicts that a CR mimics the UR when the UR is monophasic (eyeblink) and opposes it when the UR is biphasic with a rebound (shock-induced heart rate) (pp. 100–101).

**Key terms**: frequency principle, blocking, Rescorla-Wagner model, salience, overshadowing, overexpectation effect, CS preexposure effect, attention theories, comparator theory, contextual stimuli, cerebellum, hippocampus, equipotentiality premise, prepared / contraprepared association, long-delay learning, compensatory CR, tolerance, cue exposure treatment, conditioned opponent theory, SOP, monophasic / biphasic UR.

**Notable studies / people**
- Kamin (1968): blocking (p. 80); Rescorla & Wagner (1972) (p. 80); Kremer (1978): overexpectation (p. 84); Lubow & Moore (1959): CS preexposure (p. 86).
- Carew, Hawkins & Kandel (1983): Aplysia conditioning (p. 89); Daum et al. (1993): cerebellar damage and eyeblink conditioning (p. 92); Solomon (1977): hippocampal lesions abolish blocking in rabbits (p. 91).
- Garcia, Ervin & Koelling (1966) and Garcia & Koelling (1966) (pp. 93–94); Wilcoxon et al. (1971): quail vs. rats (p. 95); Seligman (1970); Logue (1979) (pp. 95, 97).
- Siegel (1975, 2005): morphine tolerance; Rozin et al. (1984): coffee compensatory response; Siegel et al. (1982): overdoses in unfamiliar settings (pp. 98–99); Schull (1979), Wagner (1981) (pp. 100–101).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- A cue is learned only if it adds information: new cues added next to one the learner already uses (blocking, overshadowing) may go unlearned, so strip redundant cues and make the to-be-learned cue salient and informative (pp. 80–84).
- Prior learning is latent rather than gone after extinction or disuse; review in several contexts and sessions to guard against context-bound relapse, as cue-exposure advice does (pp. 62–63, 100).
- Performance depends on context: the same knowledge can fail in a new room (morphine and alcohol examples), supporting practice in varied settings resembling where the skill will be used (pp. 98–100).

## Ch 5 — Basic Principles of Operant Conditioning (pp. 106–134)

**Core ideas**
- Operant (nonreflexive) behavior is shaped by its consequences. Thorndike's cats escaping puzzle boxes improved gradually (160 s on the first trial to 7 s on the 24th), which he explained with the Law of Effect: a response followed by a satisfying outcome becomes more likely in that situation (modern term: reinforcement) (pp. 106–108). Guthrie and Horton's photographs showed each cat settling on its own movement style, supporting a "stop-action" view in which reinforcement strengthens whatever precise behavior is occurring at that moment (pp. 108–109).
- Accidental pairing can strengthen irrelevant behavior: Skinner's pigeons fed every 15 s developed rituals, and people with no control over loud tones developed key-pressing routines and believed they worked. Staddon and Simmelhag argued many such behaviors are innate interim and terminal behaviors; superstition may be a by-product of a system tuned to detect causes, and widely shared superstitions are usually socially transmitted (pp. 109–111).
- Shaping (successive approximation) first makes a signal such as the dispenser sound a conditioned reinforcer, then reinforces any step toward the target and raises the criterion gradually, exploiting natural variability in behavior; it can work even when the learner is unaware (pp. 112–114).
- Applied shaping: a pole-vaulter's arm extension was raised by moving a photobeam criterion upward, with a beep as conditioned reinforcer and about 90% success before each step; a boy with intellectual disabilities extended mask-wearing from 5 to 40 seconds; graded vouchers for reduced cocaine use beat demanding total abstinence from the start. A percentile schedule reinforces a response that beats a set share of the last several responses (e.g., 7 of the last 10), so the criterion rises automatically, and software can tailor difficulty on the same logic (pp. 114–115).
- Skinner's free-operant procedure lets a simple response recur freely so moment-to-moment rate can be studied; the unit of analysis is the three-term contingency of discriminative stimulus, response and reinforcer (pp. 117–118). Box 5.1 offers a test for classical versus operant: does the response cause the outcome, or is it an elicited reflex (p. 119).
- Operant learning parallels classical: gradual acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization and discrimination (pp. 119–120). Conditioned reinforcers need continued pairing with primary reinforcers; money is a generalized reinforcer. Whether they work by information or added value is unresolved, with marking and bridging as further roles (pp. 120–121).
- In a response chain, every intermediate cue both rewards the step before it (conditioned reinforcement) and signals the step after it (discriminative control). Backward chaining trains the last step first; removing a link extinguishes the earlier steps but not later ones, and early links are weakest, so interventions on unwanted chains should target early links (pp. 121–123). Applied teaching uses forward chaining (laundromat training), total task presentation with prompts, and prompt fading either most-to-least or least-to-most, always planning to remove the prompt (pp. 123–124).
- Biological limits: the Brelands' instinctive drift (pigs rooting coins, raccoons rubbing coins) showed food-related instinctive behavior displacing the reinforced response. Autoshaping (pigeons pecking a light that precedes free food, even when pecking cancels food) is now regarded mainly as classical conditioning, with the response form depending on the reinforcer and species behavior systems. The author concludes heredity and reinforcement both operate and the limits do not undo the reinforcement principle (pp. 124–130).

**Key terms**: Law of Effect, puzzle box, discrete trial, reinforcer, stop-action principle, superstitious behavior, interim and terminal behavior, adjunctive behavior, conditioned / primary / generalized reinforcer, shaping, percentile schedule, free operant, discriminative stimulus, three-term contingency, stimulus control, information vs. value hypothesis, marking, bridging, response chain, backward / forward chaining, total task method, prompt, most-to-least / least-to-most prompting, instinctive drift, autoshaping (sign-tracking), behavior-systems analysis.

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1898) (pp. 107–108); Guthrie & Horton (1946) (p. 108); Skinner (1948) (p. 110); Matute (1994, 1995) (p. 110); Staddon & Simmelhag (1971) (p. 111).
- Scott, Scott & Goldwater (1997): pole vault (pp. 114–115); Hagopian & Thompson (1999): mask wearing (p. 115); Preston et al. (2001): graduated cocaine reduction (p. 115); Platt (1973): percentile schedules (p. 115).
- Rachlin (1976), Schuster (1969): information view of conditioned reinforcement (pp. 120–121); McDonnell & McFarland (1988): forward chaining (p. 124).
- Breland & Breland (1961): instinctive drift (pp. 124–125); Brown & Jenkins (1968): autoshaping (pp. 125–126); Williams & Williams (1969) (p. 126); Jenkins & Moore (1973) (pp. 126–127); Wasserman (1973), Timberlake & Grant (1975) (pp. 127–128).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Shaping translates to scaffolded sequences: start where the learner can already succeed, raise the criterion in small steps after a high success rate (the pole vault used 90%), and give an immediate unambiguous success signal rather than delayed feedback (pp. 113–115).
- Percentile-style rules give adaptive practice by comparing today's work with the learner's own recent performance rather than a fixed bar, with software adjusting difficulty to each learner's rate of improvement (p. 115).
- For multi-step skills choose a chaining method deliberately: backward chaining ends every practice at the goal, forward chaining builds from the start, total-task teaching uses prompts; whichever is used, plan the fading of prompts (pp. 122–124).
- To discourage a bad habit chain, interrupt its earliest link (p. 123).

## Ch 6 — Reinforcement Schedules: Experimental Analyses and Applications (pp. 135–162)

**Core ideas**
- A reinforcement schedule is the rule for when responding pays off; most real behavior is only intermittently reinforced. Skinner's cumulative recorder shows moment-to-moment patterns: slope is rate and a flat line is no responding (pp. 135–136).
- The four simple schedules yield characteristic patterns. Fixed ratio gives stop-and-go responding, a postreinforcement pause that lengthens with the ratio, and ratio strain at very large ratios; variable ratio gives fast, steady responding with brief pauses (gambling, sports); fixed interval gives a pause then an accelerating "scallop" (waiting for a bus; students studying more as exams near; Congress passing bills late in a session); variable interval gives moderate, steady responding (checking e-mail) (pp. 136–143).
- The partial reinforcement effect: extinction is slower after intermittent than continuous reinforcement. The discrimination hypothesis says the change is harder to detect; Capaldi's generalization-decrement hypothesis, which fits better, says responding persists when extinction conditions resemble training, and intermittent training already included long unreinforced runs (pp. 143–144).
- DRL reinforces only responses after a minimum pause (useful for lowering a rate); DRH requires fast bursts; concurrent schedules offer choice (p. 144).
- Performance also depends on reinforcer amount and rate, delay, effort, and reinforcement history (FR-trained participants responded fast on a later FI, DRL-trained ones slowly, for 20+ sessions). Behavioral momentum: behavior richly reinforced in a setting resists disruption, which helps explain relapse after leaving a treatment setting (pp. 144–146).
- Humans often depart from animal patterns because verbal rules can override contingencies; Skinner's contrast is contingency-shaped versus rule-governed behavior, though reinforcers and histories also differ (pp. 146–147).
- Experimental analysis beats intuition: with multiple FR 10/FR 100 schedules the postreinforcement pause tracked the upcoming ratio, not the work just done or satiation, supporting the remaining-responses hypothesis over fatigue and satiation (pp. 147–149). VR beats VI response rates at equal reinforcement rates; molecular IRT theory (long pauses selectively reinforced on VI) and the molar response-reinforcer correlation theory compete, and experiments pitting them against each other favor the molecular view (pp. 149–151).
- Applied behavior analysis: functional communication training for children with autism identifies the function of problem behavior (attention, objects, escape), substitutes an easier communication response that now earns the same payoff, and fades prompts (pp. 151–152). Token economies exchange tokens for backup reinforcers (classrooms; the good behavior game; online vouchers for lower carbon monoxide in smokers) (pp. 152–153). Animal welfare uses shaping for voluntary husbandry procedures and enrichment (pp. 153–155). Organizational behavior management combines antecedent changes (training, task clarification, goals) with consequence changes (feedback, praise, rewards); trading stamps cut mine accidents by over two thirds and feedback raised safe instrument passing and standardized rounds (pp. 155–156). Behavioral couples therapy uses contingency contracts for behavior exchange plus communication training (pp. 156–157).

**Key terms**: reinforcement schedule, continuous reinforcement, cumulative recorder, fixed / variable ratio, fixed / variable interval, postreinforcement pause, ratio strain, scallop, partial reinforcement effect, discrimination hypothesis, generalization decrement, DRL, DRH, concurrent schedule, reinforcement history, behavioral momentum, contingency-shaped vs. rule-governed behavior, multiple schedule, remaining-responses hypothesis, molecular vs. molar theory, interresponse time, functional communication training, prompt fading, token, backup reinforcer, good behavior game, organizational behavior management, contingency contract, behavior exchange.

**Notable studies / people**
- Ferster & Skinner (1957) (p. 135); Mawhinney et al. (1971): study patterns under daily quizzes vs. 3-week exams (pp. 140–141); Critchfield et al. (2003): Congress (pp. 142–143).
- Humphreys (1939), Mowrer & Jones (1945), Capaldi (1966) (p. 143); Weiner (1964) (p. 145); Nevin (1974, 1992): momentum (p. 145); Mintz et al. (1967) (p. 148); Shimp (1968, 1973), Vaughan (1987) (pp. 149–151).
- Tiger, Hanley & Bruzek (2008): functional communication training (p. 152); Dallery, Glenn & Raiff (2007): online smoking vouchers (p. 153); Fox, Hopkins & Anger (1987): mine accidents (pp. 155–156); Jacobson & Follette (1985); Halweg & Markman (1988) (pp. 156–157).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Exam timing shapes study timing: a rare big exam produced little early studying and a late surge, while daily quizzes produced steadier day-to-day study, so frequent low-stakes checks support steady practice (pp. 140–141).
- Intermittent reinforcement builds persistence; use frequent, reliable feedback for new skills and thin it later, and avoid occasional give-ins for unwanted behavior (pp. 143–144).
- Pauses before a task track how much work lies ahead, not tiredness; showing a large task as smaller units may shorten the pause (inference from p. 148).
- Functional communication training's pattern (find the function, teach an easier replacement response that meets the same need, fade prompts) suggests how to teach help-seeking in class (pp. 151–152). Check whether learners' stated strategies match what actually works, since rules can override feedback (p. 146).

## Ch 7 — Avoidance and Punishment (pp. 163–190)

**Core ideas**
- Behavior can be changed four ways: positive reinforcement (add a pleasant stimulus, behavior rises), punishment (add an unpleasant one, behavior falls), negative reinforcement (remove or prevent an unpleasant one, behavior rises, as escape or avoidance) and negative punishment/omission (remove a pleasant one, behavior falls) (pp. 163–164).
- In shuttle-box experiments dogs first escaped shock, then learned to avoid it by jumping during the warning darkness, after which many were never shocked again; this raised the "avoidance paradox" of how a non-event can reinforce (pp. 164–165).
- Two-factor theory answers that fear is classically conditioned to the signal and avoidance is really escape from that fear-evoking cue. Problems: signs of fear fade as avoidance becomes skilled, and avoidance is very slow to extinguish (p. 166).
- One-factor theory says avoidance itself, or reduced shock frequency, reinforces the response (Sidman's signal-free task; Herrnstein and Hineline's task with no reliable signal or time cue). Cognitive theory says learners form expectations (response means no shock, no response means shock) that are never contradicted while they keep responding, explaining slow extinction (pp. 166–168).
- Bolles argued avoidance depends on species-specific defense reactions (freezing, fleeing, fighting): responses resembling them are learned quickly and arbitrary ones slowly, and arbitrary responses become easier when made compatible with natural defenses. No avoidance theory is settled, and each may be partly right (pp. 168–170).
- Maladaptive avoidance of relatively safe situations, including avoidance of internal experiences, runs across several disorders; Acceptance and Commitment Therapy works on reducing it through flexible, values-driven action (pp. 170–171).
- Learned helplessness: dogs given inescapable shock later failed to escape in the shuttle box, and students given unavoidable noise did worse on anagrams. Proposed protections are early mastery experiences ("immunization"; predictable courses with clear requirements and achievable early assignments) and learned optimism (disputing helpless thoughts, with mixed evidence) (pp. 171–173).
- Punishment is functionally the opposite of reinforcement and reduces the specific punished response, permanently if intense, although mild punishment can be habituated to (pp. 173–174). It works best when introduced at full intensity, delivered immediately, applied to every occurrence, aimed at low-motivation behavior and paired with an alternative way to get the reinforcer; a reprimand is a form of attention, and soft private reprimands halved disruption (pp. 174–177).
- Disadvantages of punishment: fear and anger (spanking is associated with more anxiety, aggression and noncompliance), suppression of other behavior (one harsh reply reduces all questions), need for constant monitoring, circumvention and escape, aggression, and staff reluctance; behavior analysts are required to prioritize reinforcement and to use punishment only when other means have been documented as ineffective. Contingent skin shock is widely condemned (pp. 177–180).
- Behavior decelerators besides punishment: response cost; time-out (shown in an ABAB design with a hoarding patient; a 3-minute playground time-out cut gym-class disruption by 95%); overcorrection (restitution plus repeated positive practice); extinction (ignore attention-maintained behavior, expecting an initial burst); escape extinction; response blocking; differential reinforcement of alternative behavior (DRA); and stimulus satiation. Teaching what to do instead fills the "behavioral vacuum" (pp. 180–185). Omission, too, works best when immediate and consistent (p. 178).

## Ch 8 — Theories and Research on Operant Conditioning (pp. 191–220)

**Core ideas**
- Thorndike held that the learner must perform and be reinforced for a response; Tolman held that learners form expectations and cognitive maps, which can be acquired without responding. Rats carried through a T-maze in a basket later ran as well as rats that had run it themselves, and carted rats benefited in an eight-choice water maze, so active responding is not essential (p. 192).
- Latent learning (Tolman and Honzik): rats given no food for ten days improved abruptly once food appeared, matching rats rewarded throughout. Reinforcement is not needed for learning but matters for performance, a distinction every learning theorist now keeps (pp. 193–194).
- Whether reinforcement can control visceral responses was tested by paralyzing rats with curare and reinforcing heart-rate changes with brain stimulation (Miller and DiCara); heart rate was shaped up and down, but replication was inconsistent (pp. 194–195).
- Biofeedback amplifies feedback about a bodily process so people can learn to change it. Forehead-muscle EMG clicks cut tension about 50% in the first session and headaches about 80% on average with maintained results; EMG feedback increased paralyzed-leg muscle activity in hemiplegia; effectiveness must be judged problem by problem. Neurofeedback of brain waves shows benefits for ADHD, epilepsy and some cognition (pp. 195–197).
- Defining a reinforcer as whatever increases behavior is circular unless it can be predicted in advance. Need-reduction theory fails (sex and non-nutritive sweeteners reinforce; a thiamine need does not), as does drive-reduction (increases in stimulation such as play, running wheels and roller coasters reinforce) (pp. 198–201).
- Premack's principle replaced trans-situationality with a relative rule: a more probable behavior (more baseline time) reinforces a less probable one, and the reverse punishes; a behavior such as door opening can be reinforcer or reinforceable depending on its rank. Rats confirmed both halves with reciprocal contingencies (pp. 201–205).
- Premack applied: let preferred activities follow desired ones (new foods before favorites; bell-signaled alternation of quiet listening and "run and scream" in nursery school; recreation-room time for calm class behavior in two boys with ADHD); activities are cheap and do not satiate as snacks do (p. 205).
- Response deprivation theory refines Premack: any behavior whose access the contingency restricts below baseline becomes a reinforcer, even a low-probability one. It predicted correctly almost 100% of the time in one applied comparison, making "reinforcer" non-circular (pp. 206–207).
- Functional analysis tests, case by case, what maintains a problem behavior (attention, tangibles, escape, automatic reinforcement) by varying consequences; a boy's self-injury proved to be escape and fell when he could switch tasks after completing one without injury. It can often be done in under an hour (pp. 207–209).
- Behavioral economics treats choices as allocation of limited resources: optimization (sunfish choosing prey by density), demand elasticity (rats' demand for fat fell faster than for food pellets; "stronger" reinforcer depends on the measure), and drug use as economic behavior sensitive to price, substitutes and competing reinforcers. Box 8.2 covers framing, decoy and sunk-cost effects, which people can learn to avoid (pp. 209–215).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Learning can occur without immediate payoff, so lack of visible performance is not proof of no learning; make a payoff available later and test, as with the latent-learning rats (pp. 193–194). Watching a worked path taught rats (the carted rats), which loosely supports worked examples before independent practice (p. 192, inference).
- Use Premack and response-deprivation logic for study schedules: tie a preferred activity, restricted below its usual level, to a required amount of the target activity (pp. 205–207).
- Immediate, amplified, continuous feedback about a normally hidden process can produce fast improvement (EMG biofeedback), a model for feedback tools for learners (pp. 195–196, extension is inference).
- Before intervening on a disruptive behavior, check what it gets the learner (attention, objects, escape, sensation); the same behavior needs different treatments depending on function (pp. 207–209).

## Ch 9 — Stimulus Control and Concept Learning (pp. 221–248)

**Core ideas**
- Generalization gradients can be measured with unreinforced probe trials or a brief extinction test, and also in humans (children judging whether a tone matched a 4-second standard gave a symmetrical gradient) (p. 222).
- Pavlov saw generalization gradients as an automatic property of the nervous system; Lashley and Wade said they depend on discrimination experience along the dimension. Pigeons given nondifferential training showed flat gradients, those with presence-absence training had peaked ones, and those with intradimensional S+/S- training (1,000 Hz versus 950 Hz) had very sharp ones; but animals reared under a single color still showed peaked color gradients. Both views are partly right, and for birds vision may be "prepared" for color but not pitch (pp. 223–225).
- Stimulus control may be absolute (each stimulus learned separately) or relational (a relation such as "lighter" is learned). Transposition (chickens choosing a new lighter card over the old reinforced gray) and peak shift (gradient peak moves away from the S-) point toward relational learning, though Spence's excitatory/inhibitory gradient theory also explains both. The intermediate-size problem (chimpanzees choose the middle square of any trio) and Lazareva's pigeon circle experiments favor relational responding, which was stronger with more training pairs (pp. 226–231).
- Behavioral contrast: when one component of a multiple schedule is changed to extinction, responding in the unchanged component rises (positive contrast), and the reverse for enriched schedules (negative contrast). Proposed causes (behavioral reallocation, reinforcer habituation/satiation, comparison of reinforcement rates, anticipation of the upcoming component) all seem to contribute; schedules should not be viewed in isolation (pp. 231–233).
- Errorless discrimination learning (Terrace): introduce the S- early, for very short periods, in a form unlikely to be responded to (a dark key), then fade it up gradually. Pigeons averaged about 25 S- responses versus over 3,000 with conventional training, with no setbacks or signs of aversiveness. It matches Skinner's view that curricula should keep errors rare, and was used with children with Down syndrome (shape cards with small, growing incorrect distractors) and adults with Alzheimer's, with the caution that educators should weigh pros and cons (pp. 234–235).
- Natural categories have fuzzy boundaries and graded typicality (robin 1.1, chicken 3.8, bat 5.8 on a 7-point scale); central instances are named more often and verified faster; theories include exemplar, prototype and feature accounts, with no agreement (pp. 235–236).
- Pigeons (and other species) learn natural concepts such as tree, people, water and fish and classify slides never seen before as well as old ones; they respond to pictures as representations of real objects; can categorize paintings by artist; show typicality effects; can switch between global and fine-detail cues; and orangutans learned categories at three levels of generality (pp. 237–240).
- Stimulus equivalence training (spoken word, written word, numeral) is used to teach reading and braille, and in one study equivalence-based training helped college students learn the concept of a statistical interaction (92% versus 57% in controls) (pp. 240–241).
- Applied stimulus control: studying at one fixed time and place used only for that subject, leaving after daydreaming, expanded gradually course by course, improved students' grades (alongside SQ3R); eating snacks never in front of the TV and "narrowing" TV time helped weight control; insomnia treatment uses the bed only for sleep and leaving it when unable to sleep, with success partly because incompatible behaviors are prevented rather than reinforced (pp. 241–244).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference; concept learning and errorless teaching matter most here)
- Concept teaching: show many varied examples (the pigeons classified novel tree slides after many examples) and non-examples, include atypical as well as typical members, and test with unseen instances rather than the training set, as the pigeon studies did, to verify the concept rather than memory of items (pp. 237, 244).
- Make contrasts sharp: discrimination develops when S+ and S- come from the same dimension and differ minimally (intradimensional training), and relational rules are learned better when multiple pairs all follow the same rule, so present several example pairs that share the principle (pp. 225, 231).
- Errorless learning: start with the correct item alone or with distractors that are faint or small, then fade distractors in; errors are reduced and attitudes toward learning improve, but weigh the trade-offs (pp. 234–235).
- Build stimulus control for studying: a dedicated place and time, only the target materials, leave when off-task, build duration gradually (pp. 242–243). Use equivalence-based matching practice across spoken, written and symbolic forms for reading, vocabulary, and notation (pp. 240–241).
- Transfer: because learners may respond to relations, teach the relational rule explicitly through varied pairs; because contrast effects exist, the value of a task depends on what surrounds it (pp. 231–233).

## Ch 10 — Comparative Cognition (pp. 249–278)

**Core ideas**
- Comparative cognition applies the concepts of human cognitive psychology to animals to find shared and unique processes. Working memory (replacing "short-term memory") holds information that is continually updated to guide the task at hand (pp. 249–250).
- Delayed matching to sample (DMTS) measures how long a sample is retained: accuracy falls steadily with delay (pigeons about 66% at 10 s). Children's DMTS accuracy rises with age (monkeys beat 4-year-olds, but 5-year-olds beat monkeys). Retroactive interference (events during the delay) and proactive interference (earlier trials) both impair performance, as in human list learning (pp. 250–253).
- Rats in the radial-arm maze track visited arms by room landmarks with near-perfect accuracy (about 15 of 17 in a 17-arm maze) and can remember for up to 4 hours, so how long working memory lasts depends on the task (pp. 253–254).
- Rehearsal is inferred rather than observed. Directed forgetting in pigeons (probe tests after a "forget" cue gave about 70% correct versus about 90% after a "remember" cue) suggests animals choose to maintain items; rabbits conditioned more slowly when a surprising event followed each trial soon after, interrupting associative rehearsal, with greatest harm when the interruption came close in time (pp. 254–257).
- Long-term memory is huge: pigeons discriminated hundreds, even 1,600, arbitrarily labeled slides; nutcrackers retrieve thousands of caches by landmarks; mice and rats show what-where-when episodic-like memory. Animal forgetting curves resemble Ebbinghaus's and interference (proactive and retroactive) affects long-term memory (pp. 257–258).
- Forgetting often reflects retrieval failure rather than loss: the context-shift effect (better recall in the learning context) holds for animals, and a reminder (brief reexposure to the white compartment) revived rats' "forgotten" avoidance learning three days after training (pp. 258–259).
- Chunking (Box 10.1) helps animals as it does people: pigeons learned five-item sequences faster when the list divided into chunks (three colors, then two shapes), pausing at chunk boundaries; rats in a 12-arm maze organized visits by food type, improving accuracy (pp. 259–261).
- Timing: the peak procedure shows response rates peaking at the usual reinforcement time (about 40 s or 20 s depending on the signal); duration discrimination follows Weber's law (roughly 25% difference needed); the timing system is stopwatch-like and transfers across modality (pp. 261–263). Counting is approximate (Mechner's run lengths) and follows Weber's law, though exact small-number counting appears in rats, a parrot (Alex, up to six objects) and a chimpanzee, and macaques generalized ordering to larger arrays (pp. 263–265).
- Animal language: Washoe (about 130 signs, inconsistent order), Sarah (symbol order and grammar-like rules, rarely initiated), Nim (Terrace judged chimp language mostly imitation and prompting), Kanzi (over 300 lexigrams, consistent order, understood spoken English), dolphins and sea lions responding to gesture sequences, parrot Alex (about 50 words), and dogs Rico and Chaser (names for hundreds of objects, inference by exclusion). Evidence exists for symbols, productivity, rudimentary grammar and communication, with less for displacement; none matches human language (pp. 265–268).
- Reasoning: object permanence through invisible displacement in dogs, cats, primates, birds; analogies (Sarah solved functional and perceptual analogies; baboons perceptual ones); transitive inference from rats to wasps; tool use and manufacture (the crow that bent wire into a hook); and evidence of metacognition (an uncertainty response used on hard memory trials), which is debated but growing (pp. 268–273).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Interference is a main cause of forgetting: avoid presenting similar new material right after or right before what must be remembered, and note that surprising events soon after learning disrupt consolidation, so leave a calm gap after a key learning episode (pp. 251–253, 256–257, 258).
- Forgetting is often retrieval failure, so cues work: practice and test in varied contexts, provide reminders and cue-based review rather than assuming the knowledge is gone (pp. 258–259).
- Chunk material before presenting it (group by type or by theme) and let learners generate their own groupings, which cuts working-memory load and errors (pp. 259–261).
- Directed forgetting suggests that telling learners which material will be tested changes whether they rehearse it, so signal what matters (pp. 255–256, my extension).

## Ch 11 — Observational Learning and Motor Skills (pp. 279–312)

**Core ideas**
- Much human learning comes from watching others and their consequences (vicarious experience), which traditional learning theory neglected; motor-skill learning is the other neglected area (p. 279). Imitation may be partly instinctive (infants a few weeks old copied some gestures; island monkeys spread potato washing), but toddlers trained on one set of gestures did not generalize to new ones, and an instinct account cannot say when imitation occurs (p. 280).
- Operant accounts: Miller and Dollard treated imitation as an operant response whose discriminative stimulus is a model's behavior (children rewarded for matching or opposing a leader quickly did so). Generalized imitation adds that a history of reinforced imitation leads people to copy new behaviors in similar situations without new reinforcement (children with disabilities copied unreinforced new acts) (pp. 282–283).
- Bandura's Bobo-doll study showed children copying a filmed adult's aggression, less if the model was punished, yet all groups imitated equally once rewarded for doing so. His four-factor theory: attention to the model; retention (often an abstract verbal summary, rehearsed); motor reproduction (the ability to execute the movements); and incentive/motivation. The first three govern learning and incentive governs performance. The generalized-imitation account can explain the same data through discrimination, generalization and the learning/performance distinction, so the dispute is partly about terminology (pp. 283–286).
- Mirror neurons fire when an animal makes or sees a movement and may support understanding others and observational learning (pp. 286–287). Media act as models: longitudinal and experimental work links violent TV to later aggression, heavy TV to lower reading scores, and educational shows to vocabulary gains; social media can spread helpful or harmful behavior; evidence on violent video games is mixed (pp. 287–290).
- Modeling in therapy: facilitating existing low-probability behaviors (assertiveness training combining modeling, role play and behavior rehearsal; graduated filmed modeling raised social interaction fivefold in withdrawn children); teaching new behaviors (speech for children with autism; behavioral skills training combining instruction, modeling, guided practice and feedback); and reducing fears (graduated modeling of child-dog contact reduced dog fear for a month). Video self-modeling shows an edited video of the learner's own correct, unassisted performance and improved skills in children with physical disabilities (pp. 291–293).
- Feedback in motor learning: Trowbridge and Cason showed Thorndike's "Right/Wrong" worked as information, not reinforcement; precise quantitative knowledge of results (KR: direction and size of the error) far outperformed qualitative feedback, and irrelevant feedback did nothing (pp. 294–296). Knowledge of performance (KP, information about the movement itself) can beat KR: throwers who watched a video replay and were told how to correct their form improved most, and stroke patients given joint-movement information improved where KR-only did not, though KP is not always better (p. 296).
- Mental practice (imagery rehearsal) helps athletes, surgeons and rehabilitation patients, more so for those with good imagery. Observation plus practice beats either alone for transfer (observers beat controls but not active practicers; observe-then-practice did best on transfer) (pp. 296–298). Transfer can be positive (mirror writing from dominant to nondominant hand; pianists adapting fingerings, suggesting learners acquire general movement patterns) or negative (usually brief but strong in unequal two-handed rhythms), and is hard to predict (p. 298).
- Adams's two-stage theory: in the verbal-motor stage improvement depends on external feedback and builds a perceptual trace (the feel of a good movement); in the motor stage the learner improves by practicing against that internal standard. Support: accuracy improves right after occasional KR and then decays, and early KR leaves lasting benefit, more after more KR trials (pp. 299–301).
- Schmidt's schema theory covers flexible skills: learners abstract a rule relating movement parameters to outcomes (putting effort to distance) and discard individual trials, so variable practice should help. Participants were as accurate at untrained distances as trained ones, and children who practiced throwing at 2 and 4 feet beat those who practiced only at 3 feet when tested at 3 feet (pp. 301–303).
- Contextual interference (Box 11.2): harder practice often improves long-term performance. Random practice (changing variation every trial) did worse during acquisition but better at 10-minute and 10-day tests than blocked practice, though not for tasks with many simultaneous demands (pp. 303–304). The chapter summary also states that distributed practice produces faster learning than massed practice, though the difference is not always large (p. 307).
- Movement sequences: a response chain account says feedback from each movement cues the next; Lashley argued for motor programs because rapid sequences (up to 16 finger movements per second) outrun feedback, skilled movement survives loss of sensation, anticipation and transposition errors (typing "hte") show planning ahead, and the time to start grows with the number of movements in the sequence. Programs are also implicated in walking on irregular surfaces and in fluent speech (pp. 304–306).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference; observational learning and motor skills matter most here)
- Design demonstrations around Bandura's four factors: direct attention to the critical features, give a short verbal summary the learner can rehearse ("first X, then Y"), confirm the learner has the component motor skills, and make the payoff for performing visible; a model's consequences affect whether learners perform what they learned (pp. 284–285).
- Feedback: give precise quantitative KR (direction and size of error) and, for complex movements, KP with a specific correction cue rather than outcome-only or praise-only feedback; the best group saw a replay and was told how to fix the next attempt (pp. 295–296).
- Fade feedback on purpose: early heavy feedback builds the perceptual trace, later practice relies on the learner's internal standard, and occasional KR refreshes it (pp. 299–301).
- Practice design: vary conditions instead of repeating one exact movement, consider random rather than blocked ordering for long-term retention while accepting worse in-practice performance (and check total task load), and spread practice over time (pp. 302–304, 307).
- Combine observation, imagery and physical practice, and use video self-modeling that shows only successful performance (pp. 293, 296–298). Expect slower, error-prone, feedback-dependent performance before sequences become automatic programs (pp. 304–306, inference).

## Ch 12 — Choice (pp. 313–341)

**Core ideas**
- All behavior involves choice, and choice in simple settings is highly orderly. Herrnstein's pigeons on concurrent VI schedules distributed their responses in the same proportion as the reinforcers each key delivered (the matching law). It has been applied to conversation time, classroom on-task versus off-task behavior, career-family conflicts, consumer purchases and football play-calling (pp. 313–315).
- Deviations from matching: undermatching (preferences closer to indifference, e.g., from rapid switching), overmatching (when switching is costly), and bias (e.g., a pull toward 3-point shots because they are worth more). The law extends to reinforcer quality and amount, which lets preferences between reinforcers be scaled (pp. 316–317).
- Reinforcement is relative: how much behavior goes to one option depends on the reinforcers available for competing options (a yo-yo on a dull day versus on Christmas; eating when bored), so effective classroom plans must consider competing sources of reinforcement for off-task behavior (pp. 314–318).
- Matching can be only a description or an explanation (animals are built to match). Optimization theory proposes choosing to maximize total reinforcement, and matching is optimal on ordinary concurrent VI pairs; but when the schedule arrangement makes matching costly, pigeons still matched and lost 29% or more of possible food, which favors matching over optimization, though results across studies are mixed (pp. 318–321).
- Momentary maximization says that at each instant the animal picks the alternative with the highest current payoff probability, predicting orderly moment-to-moment alternation (Box 12.1's nine-trial door game: 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, ...). Some experiments find such patterns and others do not, but short-term factors clearly affect choice; hybrid molar-molecular theories also exist (pp. 321–324).
- Self-control choices pit a small immediate reinforcer against a larger delayed one, and preferences reverse as time passes (planning to attend class Sunday night, then sleeping in). Delay discounting measures how fast value falls with delay (faster for children than adults, faster in smokers, people with addictions and pathological gamblers) (pp. 324–326).
- The Ainslie-Rachlin theory explains reversals: each reinforcer's value rises as it nears, and the small one can overtake the large one at the moment of choice, as a nearby small building looks larger than a distant large one. Pigeons chose the small reward when delays were 2 s and 6 s but the larger when 18 s was added to both; precommitment (arranging in advance so the impulsive option is unavailable) is the main remedy (pp. 326–329).
- Factors affecting self-control in animals and children: visible rewards and reward-linked cues increase impulsivity (pigeons waited on about 80% of trials when food was hidden but about 15% when visible; preschoolers in Mischel's studies behaved similarly), whereas distracting activity during the delay and thinking about something else help. Children improve with age, supportive parenting and scaffolding, adult models, and gradual lengthening of delays (fading) (pp. 329–331).
- Techniques to improve self-control: precommitment, adding an immediate reward for choosing the delayed option or a cost for the immediate one (with a partner to prevent cheating), verbal rules, imagery of the long-term goal, and distraction from the tempting cue; the book argues that self-control is more about strategy knowledge than willpower (pp. 331–332).
- Risk taking depends on need (juncos preferred the guaranteed seed when food was plentiful and the gamble when scarce); gambling's appeal may rest on the chance of immediate payoff (pp. 333–334). The tragedy of the commons applies the same short-term versus long-term conflict at the group level; Ostrom showed well-run commons exist, using clear boundaries, collective rule-making, monitoring, graduated sanctions and conflict resolution, which parallel self-control strategies (pp. 334–336).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Studying competes with other activities, so make the competing options less immediately attractive (phone away, tempting cues out of sight) and the study option more immediately rewarding (a snack or break after two solid hours with a partner checking), which is precommitment plus an added immediate reward (pp. 329–332).
- Decide study plans well in advance when delayed rewards (grades) are valued more highly, and set up commitments (study partner, scheduled session) so that the in-the-moment preference reversal does not decide (pp. 326–329).
- Teach patience gradually: start with short delays to reward and lengthen them (fading), model the delayed choice, and give learners something to do while waiting (pp. 330–331).
- Because reinforcement is relative, to raise on-task behavior either increase what on-task work earns or reduce what competing activities earn (pp. 314–315, 318).

## Cross-cutting takeaways for teaching and learning

Ranked roughly by usefulness for designing instruction and study (items are the digest's own synthesis, with the supporting pages).

1. Give precise, informative, immediate feedback. Quantitative knowledge of results (direction and size of error) beat "right/wrong" and irrelevant feedback, and for complex movements, knowledge of performance plus a specific correction cue did best (pp. 294–296); in biofeedback, immediate amplified feedback produced rapid change (pp. 195–196). In punishment and reinforcement alike, immediacy and consistency matter (pp. 113, 174–175, 178).
2. Fade feedback and prompts deliberately. Early external feedback builds an internal standard (the perceptual trace), after which learners can improve alone (pp. 299–301); prompt hierarchies, forward and backward chaining, and graduated modeling all end with the support removed (pp. 122–124, 292).
3. Vary practice and make it somewhat harder. Variable practice built flexible motor schemas (pp. 301–303), random ordering beat blocked ordering at later tests despite worse practice scores (pp. 303–304), and the summary credits distributed over massed practice (p. 307); when the context changes, learned performance can fail, so practice where the skill will be used (pp. 98–100, 258–259).
4. Shape by successive approximation with an adaptive criterion. Reinforce what the learner can already do, raise the bar in small steps, use a clear immediate signal of success, and tailor difficulty to the learner's own recent performance (percentile schedules, software) (pp. 112–115).
5. Teach concepts with many varied examples and test on unseen instances. Natural categories have typical and atypical members and fuzzy edges (pp. 235–236); novel-instance tests show real concept learning (p. 237); relational rules are learned better with several example pairs (p. 231); stimulus-equivalence matching practice teaches symbol-referent relations such as reading and statistics concepts (pp. 240–241).
6. Consider errorless learning for hard discriminations. Introducing the wrong option early but faintly and fading it up gave very few errors and better attitudes, though trade-offs should be weighed (pp. 234–235).
7. Use observational learning well. Attention, retention (a rehearsable verbal summary), motor ability and incentive all matter (pp. 284–285); observe-then-practice beat either alone on transfer (pp. 297–298); mental practice and video self-modeling of correct performance add value (pp. 293, 296–298).
8. Build stimulus control for studying and for avoiding competing behavior. A dedicated time and place used only for the target task, leaving when off-task, and narrowing tempting alternatives improved study and health habits (pp. 241–244).
9. Expect and plan for relative reinforcement. The value of any reward depends on the alternatives available (pp. 314–318); Premack and response-deprivation rules predict which activities will work as rewards, and activities rarely satiate as snacks do (pp. 201–207); frequent low-stakes quizzes produce steadier studying than rare large exams (pp. 140–141).
10. Prevent helplessness and manage self-control. Early success, predictability and controllability protect against learned helplessness (p. 172); precommitment, immediate rewards for the delayed choice, hidden temptations, distraction and gradually lengthened delays improve self-control, and the book frames this as strategy knowledge rather than willpower (pp. 326–332).
11. Learning is not the same as performance. Knowledge shows up when a payoff appears (latent learning, Bandura's incentive condition), so use later tests and incentives to reveal what was learned (pp. 193–194, 286).
12. Memory loss is often interference or retrieval failure, not erasure. Extinction leaves learning in place; avoiding similar material around key content, giving cues or reminders, chunking material, and overlearning (practice beyond first success) all help retention (pp. 9, 62–63, 251–261).
13. Treat problem behavior by function, then teach a replacement. Functional analysis identifies whether attention, objects, escape or sensation maintains a behavior (pp. 207–209); teaching an easier, equally effective alternative response (functional communication training, DRA) is better than only suppressing behavior (pp. 151–152, 184–185).
14. Prefer reinforcement to punishment in teaching. Punishment has narrow conditions for effectiveness and many side effects, and professional guidance prioritizes positive reinforcement (pp. 174–180).
15. Anxiety and avoidance respond to graded exposure. Fear hierarchies with calm practice and modeling reduce phobias, while avoidance brings short-term relief but long-term cost (pp. 71–72, 170–171, 292–293).
