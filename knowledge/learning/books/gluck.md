# Gluck, Mercado & Myers, *Learning and Memory: From Brain to Behavior* — chapter digest

Citation: Mark A. Gluck, Eduardo Mercado, and Catherine E. Myers, *Learning and Memory: From Brain to Behavior*, 3rd edition, Worth Publishers, 2016. Page cites below are printed page numbers.

Orientation: an introductory textbook that links the behavioral study of learning (conditioning, generalization, memory for facts and skills, working memory, emotion, social learning, lifespan change) to its brain mechanisms and clinical consequences, using experiments with animals and people and emphasizing formal models such as error-correction learning.

## Ch 1 — The Psychology of Learning and Memory (pp. 1–34)

**Core ideas**
- The opening case of the conductor Clive Wearing, who lost most past knowledge and the ability to hold new experiences beyond seconds yet kept emotional attachment, musical skill and some habit learning, shows that "memory" is not one thing: facts, skills, habits and emotions can be damaged separately (pp. 1–2).
- The chapter's "top ten tips" box gives practical memory advice: attend fully while learning (many "forgotten" items were never properly encoded); link new material to what you already know; attach images; repeat and practice (for facts and skills alike); use several senses (read aloud, write out); offload appointments to calendars or notes to free capacity; try recalling the original learning context when a fact will not surface; sleep enough, since sleep loss impairs both encoding/retrieval and memory consolidation; use rhyme or song for arbitrary strings; and relax or move on from a stuck retrieval, because the answer often returns later (p. 3).
- Aristotle's associationism explained memory as links between events or ideas formed by three conditions: closeness in time/space (contiguity), repeated co-occurrence (frequency) and resemblance (similarity). These remain the groundwork for modern learning theory (pp. 4–5).
- The nature-versus-nurture debate runs through the history: Plato and Descartes leaned nativist (knowledge largely inborn); Aristotle and Locke leaned empiricist (mind as a blank slate shaped by experience); Leibniz proposed a compromise of roughly three-quarters learned and one-quarter innate, which resembles most modern views (pp. 5–8). Locke's empiricism also carried an educational implication: because worth is not fixed at birth, good education should be open to all children (p. 8).
- William James modeled an episode in memory as many linked components (tastes, sights, smells, topics), with two events associated through their shared components, and expected such links to map onto physical brain connections (pp. 9–10).
- Darwin's natural selection (an inheritable, variable trait that raises reproductive success) lets behavior capacity evolve; what is inherited is the capacity to learn, not the learned content itself (pp. 10–12).
- Ebbinghaus launched experimental memory research with himself as sole subject, using meaningless three-letter syllables to avoid prior familiarity. His method had four stages (learning, delay, test, relearning); his savings measure (time saved on relearning) yielded a retention curve in which most loss occurs early, shorter lists are easier, and more initial practice improves later recall. His one-subject, unblinded design is why modern studies use many participants and blind/double-blind procedures (pp. 13–15).
- Behaviorism (Watson, Hull, Skinner) limited psychology to observable stimulus-response relations; Tolman's cognitive maps and latent learning (rats learning a maze layout without reward) challenged that, and cognitive approaches followed (Chomsky's critique of Skinner on language, Estes's stimulus sampling theory, Bower's one-trial insight learning, Miller's capacity limit of roughly seven items, Rumelhart's connectionist networks with distributed representations) (pp. 18–30).
- Bower showed that smooth group-average learning curves can hide abrupt all-or-none learning in each individual, so individual performance must be examined, not only averages (p. 27).

**Key terms**: learning, memory, associationism, contiguity, empiricism, nativism, dualism, reflex arc, natural selection, evolutionary psychology, retention curve, savings, independent/dependent variable, blind and double-blind design, classical conditioning, law of effect, instrumental/operant conditioning, behaviorism, radical behaviorism, cognitive map, latent learning, stimulus sampling theory, mathematical psychology, information theory, digit span, connectionist models, distributed representation

**Notable studies / people**
- Aristotle (4th c. BC): three principles of association (p. 4). Locke (1690): blank slate, simple ideas combine into complex ones (pp. 7–8). James (1890): network model of memory and habit (pp. 8–10).
- Ebbinghaus (1885): nonsense-syllable lists, savings-based retention curve with steep early forgetting (p. 14).
- Pavlov (1904 Nobel, 1927): conditioned salivation, extinction, generalization to similar stimuli (pp. 15–16). Thorndike (1911): puzzle boxes, law of effect (p. 17).
- Watson (1907, 1913): rats in mazes learned motor habits rather than relying on a single sensory cue (p. 19). Skinner: accidental discovery that intermittent reward sustains responding as well as or better than continuous reward (p. 21). Tolman (1932, 1948): detours and latent learning in mazes (pp. 22–23).
- Bower (1961): all-or-none insight learning (pp. 26–27). Miller (1956): the seven-plus-or-minus-two capacity limit, from digit-span and magnitude-judgment work (pp. 28–29). Rumelhart and McClelland (1986): connectionist models (p. 29).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Build study guides around the book's tips: encode with full attention, tie new items to existing knowledge, add images and multiple modalities (reading aloud, writing), and test yourself after delays; repetition is endorsed for both facts and skills (p. 3).
- Because forgetting is steepest soon after learning (p. 14), plan an early review rather than waiting; and because average class progress can mask abrupt individual "aha" learning (p. 27), assess students individually rather than relying on class averages.
- Keep information-load small: capacity limits near seven items (pp. 28–29) suggest chunking material into small sets per lesson step.

## Ch 2 — The Neuroscience of Learning and Memory (pp. 35–70)

**Core ideas**
- The chapter's box on speeding up forgetting inverts the memory tips: sleep loss, stress, trying to hold too many things in mind at once, impoverished sensory input and apathy all make encoding and retrieval worse (p. 36).
- The brain is better seen as a society of specialists than a single organ: the cortex (four lobes) handles perception, planning and many memory functions; the cerebellum coordinates movement-related learning; the basal ganglia support skilled movement; the hippocampus supports new factual and event memories; the amygdala adds emotional strength to memories (pp. 38–39).
- Bigger brains do not guarantee better learning (elephants exceed humans in brain size); relative cortex proportion and the organization of regions matter more. Even octopuses with distributed nervous tissue and nematodes with 302 neurons learn, which is why invertebrate studies have been valuable (pp. 39–41).
- Experience physically changes brains: rats kept in enriched cages with toys and companions learned mazes better and grew more and longer dendrites with more connections than rats in bare housing. London taxi drivers, who learn the city intensively, had larger hippocampi than comparison people, scaling with years of driving (pp. 44–45).
- Sensory input enters via the thalamus to primary sensory cortices (V1, A1, S1), and output exits via the motor cortex, with frontal planning plus basal ganglia and cerebellum fine-tuning movement; spinal reflexes (Bell-Magendie, Sherrington) were the first step toward understanding stimulus-response learning (pp. 46–48).
- Neurons communicate across synapses via neurotransmitters released from vesicles, bound by receptors, then cleared by breakdown or reuptake; neuromodulators such as acetylcholine alter how strongly whole regions respond, and their decline marks diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's (pp. 49–51).
- Methods differ in what they show: structural imaging (MRI, DTI) shows anatomy; fMRI and PET track blood-related activity changes but only indirectly and slowly; EEG and event-related potentials are fast but spatially coarse; single-cell recording catches individual neurons' firing (pp. 43, 52–56).
- Lesion and stimulation studies test function. Lashley failed to find a localized "engram" in rat cortex and favored equipotentiality, though the truth lies between strict localization and equal distribution; stimulation maps (the motor homunculus) show that body areas needing fine control get more cortex; TMS/tDCS allow noninvasive stimulation in people; drugs act by altering synaptic transmission (pp. 57–62).
- Synaptic plasticity is the main candidate mechanism for memory. Hebb's rule holds that neurons repeatedly active together strengthen their connection, which also explains pattern completion of a familiar stimulus; long-term potentiation (found by Lømo and Bliss) strengthens synapses for hours or longer, while long-term depression weakens unused ones (pp. 62–66).

**Key terms**: neuron, synapse, neurotransmitter, receptor, reuptake, neuromodulator, cerebral cortex, frontal/parietal/temporal/occipital lobes, cerebellum, basal ganglia, hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, structural vs. functional neuroimaging, MRI, DTI, fMRI, PET, EEG, event-related potential, single-cell recording, lesion, engram, equipotentiality, homunculus, TMS, tDCS, synaptic plasticity, Hebbian learning, LTP, LTD, enriched environment

**Notable studies / people**
- Rosenzweig and colleagues (1984): enriched housing improved rat maze learning and dendrite growth (p. 44).
- Maguire et al. (2000): taxi drivers' larger hippocampi (p. 45).
- Georgopoulos et al. (1993): motor-cortex neurons tuned to arm-movement direction (pp. 55–56).
- Lashley (1929): cortical lesions failed to erase maze memory, motivating equipotentiality (p. 58).
- Hebb (1949): the rule that co-active neurons strengthen their connection (pp. 62–63). Lømo and Bliss (1966–1973): LTP in rabbit hippocampus (p. 64–65).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- The enrichment findings (p. 44) support giving learners varied, actively engaging material rather than bare, passive exposure, though the book notes that equivalent human brain evidence is only suggestive.
- Hebbian pattern completion (pp. 63–64) suggests repeated co-activation of related cues builds stronger links, so repeatedly practicing items together in meaningful combinations should help later recognition from partial cues.
- The box on memory-boosting pills (p. 63) concludes that no drug replaces time spent studying; advice should emphasize sleep, low stress and attention (p. 36).

## Ch 3 — Habituation, Sensitization, and Familiarization (pp. 71–114)

**Core ideas**
- Habituation (a drop in response after repeated exposure to the same stimulus) is among the most widespread forms of learning, found even in organisms without brains. It is stimulus-specific, so a new stimulus restores responding; the return of a habituated response after a novel arousing stimulus is called dishabituation, which also helps rule out fatigue as the explanation (pp. 72–75).
- Habituation is faster for weak stimuli, shows its biggest drops on the earliest exposures, and is faster with closely packed (massed) exposures; but massed exposure produces quicker spontaneous recovery after a break, whereas spaced exposure habituates more slowly yet lasts longer (pp. 76–77). Extra exposures after responding has stopped still strengthen the learning invisibly, delaying later recovery (p. 77).
- Sensitization is the opposite-looking process: an arousing stimulus (e.g. a shock) temporarily amplifies responses to many later, unrelated stimuli; it needs fewer exposures than habituation and, unlike habituation, is not stimulus-specific. An organism's state (e.g. illness) can turn repetition into sensitization instead of habituation (pp. 78–79).
- Dual process theory holds that every presentation triggers both a habituation process (weakening the stimulus-response path) and a sensitization process (raising arousal), with the observed response being the net of the two; opponent process theory similarly explains why an initial fear can fade while a rebound pleasure strengthens with repetition (pp. 80–81).
- Exploration-based learning is often latent. Novel object recognition (more time on new than old items) reveals familiarity; priming shows prior exposure helping performance even without awareness of having seen the item; and perceptual learning is improved discrimination from repeated exposure, as in chicken sexers or experts, sometimes occurring by mere exposure with no feedback (pp. 82–87).
- Perceptual learning can be viewed as habituation to shared features of similar stimuli, leaving their distinctive features more noticeable (p. 88). Training people to tell apart faces of an unfamiliar race reduced both the other-race effect and unconscious bias in one study (p. 86).
- Tolman and Honzik (1930) showed that rats allowed to explore a maze unrewarded for ten days later outperformed continuously trained rats once food was introduced, illustrating latent spatial learning (pp. 88–89).
- In sea slugs (Aplysia), habituation is reduced neurotransmitter release at a specific synapse, and with repeated spaced exposure the synapses themselves decrease; sensitization works through modulatory interneurons releasing serotonin, which boosts transmission across many synapses (pp. 92–95).
- In mammals, perceptual learning involves cortical plasticity: receptive fields and topographic maps in sensory cortex reshape with development and with as little as two hours of repeated fingertip stimulation. Hippocampal place cells, whose fields sharpen with experience, support spatial learning, and temporal-lobe regions signal familiarity (pp. 96–105).
- Clinically, learned non-use after stroke resembles habituation and is overcome by constraint-induced movement therapy; repeated stress can sensitize people toward depression and anxiety, though moderate stress may build resilience; cochlear implants rely on perceptual learning (pp. 106–109).

**Key terms**: habituation, dishabituation, orienting response, acoustic startle reflex, stimulus specificity, spontaneous recovery, massed vs. spaced exposure, sensitization, skin conductance response, prepulse inhibition, desensitization, dual process theory, opponent process theory, novel object recognition, familiarity, priming, perceptual learning, mere exposure learning, latent learning, spatial learning, synaptic depression, homosynaptic, heterosynaptic, cortical plasticity, receptive field, topographic map, place cell, learned non-use, constraint-induced movement therapy, cochlear implant

**Notable studies / people**
- Thompson and Spencer (1966); Groves and Thompson (1970): features of habituation and dual process theory (pp. 75, 80).
- Gibson and Walk (1956): rats living with wall shapes later discriminated them faster (p. 85); Gibson and Gibson (1955): scribble discrimination without feedback (pp. 85–86).
- Tolman and Honzik (1930): exploration-first rats (pp. 88–89); Tinbergen and Kruyt (1972): wasps using pinecone landmarks (p. 89).
- Kandel and colleagues (1970s–): Aplysia gill-withdrawal habituation and sensitization (pp. 92–95).
- Dinse, Godde and colleagues (2003–2004): two hours of fingertip stimulation sharpened touch discrimination and enlarged cortical response (p. 100).
- O'Keefe and Dostrovsky (1971): hippocampal place cells (p. 102). Taub and colleagues (2002): constraint-induced therapy outcomes (pp. 106–107).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Spaced, rather than massed, repetition yields slower acquisition but longer-lasting effects in habituation (p. 76), a pattern worth testing for study scheduling; the book itself ties this to the idea of repeating after longer and longer gaps (pp. 76–77).
- Change in stimuli or format (dishabituation, p. 75) can re-engage attention when learners tune out repeated material.
- Perceptual learning without feedback (pp. 85–86) suggests that wide exposure to many varied examples helps learners distinguish similar categories, while the Aplysia/Gibson accounts suggest contrasting similar items makes distinctive features stand out (p. 88).

## Ch 4 — Classical Conditioning (pp. 115–166)

**Core ideas**
- Classical conditioning is learning that a cue predicts a biologically significant event; after repeated pairings the neutral cue (CS) triggers an anticipatory response (CR) that readies the learner for the expected outcome (US), with eyeblink, fear (conditioned emotional response), salivation, odor-shock and sexual-conditioning preparations all showing this (pp. 116–124).
- Acquisition is gradual over trials, and extinction (cue presented without the outcome) is not simple unlearning: the original association is retained, shown by spontaneous recovery after a delay, rapid reacquisition, and context-dependent renewal when the cue returns to the original context (pp. 127–128).
- Mere pairing is not enough. A cue must give new, non-redundant information: in blocking, a cue already trained to predict an outcome prevents a redundant added cue from being learned (Kamin 1969), and in overshadowing the more salient cue in a compound takes most of the credit (pp. 128–132).
- The Rescorla–Wagner model captures this: learning on each trial is driven by prediction error (outcome actual minus expected, where expectation sums the weights of all present cues), so surprising outcomes drive big changes, fully predicted ones none, and omitted expected ones reduce the association (pp. 132–135).
- The same error-correction logic applies to human category learning (Bower and Trabasso 1964; Gluck and Bower 1988's disease-diagnosis task with feedback) and causal judgment: people also tune out cues made redundant by earlier learning, and judge a cue's value by its contingency with the outcome, not just frequency of pairing (pp. 136–140).
- Prior exposure to a cue without consequence slows later learning about it (latent inhibition), which Rescorla–Wagner cannot explain; attention-based CS-modulation theories (Mackintosh; Pearce and Hall) can, and both US-modulation and CS-modulation mechanisms probably operate (pp. 140–143).
- Timing matters: delay versus trace conditioning differ, and there is an optimal CS–US interval (about 250 ms for rabbit eyeblink). Learning is also biased by ecology: taste is readily linked to illness, and sound to shock (Garcia and Koelling 1966), and natural cues condition faster (pp. 144–146).
- In the brain, the cerebellum (interpositus nucleus for the CR, cortex for timing) stores eyeblink conditioning, and an inhibitory feedback loop to the inferior olive computes actual-minus-expected US; the hippocampus supports CS modulation such as latent inhibition (pp. 147–154). In sea slugs, conditioning builds on sensitization-type mechanisms with presynaptic and postsynaptic change, and long-lasting memory needs new synapses regulated by CREB proteins (pp. 155–158).
- Clinically, drug tolerance is a conditioned compensatory response, so overdose risk rises in unfamiliar settings, and conditioning can reduce medication use (lupus case). Extinction of cues is context-sensitive; three tips from Bouton: extinguish in many contexts, spread sessions over time, and practice in the contexts where the habit was formed (pp. 159–162).

**Notable studies / people**
- Pavlov (1927): salivation conditioning (pp. 115–116). Estes and Skinner (1941): conditioned emotional response (p. 120).
- Subkov and Zilov (1937): compensatory heart-rate response to cues of adrenaline (pp. 125–126).
- Bouton and King (1983): renewal after context change (pp. 127–128).
- Kamin (1969): blocking (pp. 130–132). Rescorla and Wagner (1972): error-correction model (pp. 132–135). Rescorla (1968): contingency (p. 139).
- Bower and Trabasso (1964); Gluck and Bower (1988): human cue-competition and diagnosis learning (pp. 136–139).
- Lubow and Moore (1959): latent inhibition (pp. 140–141). Mackintosh (1975): attentional theory (p. 142).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Redundant cues are discounted (pp. 131, 137): materials that add extra explanatory detail to what students already predict may add little learning, so highlight what is new and informative.
- Bower and Trabasso and Gluck and Bower used trial-by-trial feedback on categorization (pp. 136–139), supporting feedback-based practice with many varied examples for learning diagnostic categories.
- Extinction/forgetting advice (p. 162): changing or varying the study and practice context, and spreading sessions over time, helps keep a learned response from depending on one setting; the same context-dependence suggests testing in varied contexts.

## Ch 5 — Operant Conditioning (pp. 167–212)

**Core ideas**
- Operant (instrumental) learning is a three-part association: a discriminative stimulus signals that a response will lead to an outcome (SD to R to O). The outcome depends on the response, which is what separates it from classical conditioning, where the outcome arrives regardless; the quick test is to ask whether the outcome is contingent on a response (pp. 168–170).
- Thorndike's cats escaping puzzle boxes gave the law of effect (satisfying outcomes make a response more likely). Skinner added free-operant methods (the Skinner box and cumulative recorder), where the animal controls its own response rate (pp. 168–172). A response is defined by the outcome it produces, not a fixed movement pattern (p. 173).
- Strong SD-R links become habits that fire even when a better option is present (rats running past food, "habit slips" such as driving the usual route) (p. 173).
- Complex behaviors are built by shaping (reinforcing successive approximations) and chaining (adding links one at a time, sometimes backward), as used in physical therapy, teaching speech, and training pilots or workers step by step (pp. 173–175).
- Primary reinforcers meet biological needs; secondary reinforcers (money, grades, tokens, a clicker) gain power by being paired with them, and may work as informational "signposts" that behavior is on track. Reinforcer value is relative: switching to a less-preferred reward produces negative contrast, with responding lower than if that reward had been used throughout (pp. 175–177).
- Punishment works but has pitfalls: it increases variable behavior rather than directing learners toward the desired one; signals for punishment (e.g. a police car) teach concealment; concurrent reinforcement can undermine it; and starting weak and escalating makes later strong punishment less effective. Reinforcing an alternative behavior (DRA) is often better, and attention for misbehavior can itself reinforce it (pp. 177–180).
- Timing matters: responses are learned fastest when the outcome is immediate (rats learned well at 0 s, slower at 4 s, barely at 10 s), and delayed outcomes also weaken punishment and encourage choosing small immediate rewards over larger later ones (self-control). Precommitment, such as joining a study group or removing temptations, helps people wait (pp. 180–181).
- Reinforcement can be added or removed, giving the four types (positive/negative reinforcement, positive/negative punishment); and schedules matter: fixed-ratio produces bursts and post-reinforcement pauses, variable-ratio produces fast steady responding (as with slot machines), fixed-interval produces scalloped responding, variable-interval gives steady moderate responding (pp. 182–187).
- Choice follows the matching law (time or responses allocated in proportion to relative reinforcement rates); behavioral economics frames this as finding a "bliss point"; and the Premack principle and response deprivation hypothesis show that a preferred or restricted activity can reinforce a less-preferred one, e.g. TV after homework (pp. 188–192).
- Brain substrates: the dorsal striatum supports habitual SD-R links, the orbitofrontal cortex represents the expected specific outcome, dopamine signals "wanting" (motivation to work) more than "liking," endogenous opioids may signal "liking," and the insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex are linked to aversive and motivational value of punishers (pp. 193–202).
- Addiction involves positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement (avoiding withdrawal) and a runaway "wanting" system; behavioral addictions such as gambling share circuitry. Conditioning-based treatments include extinction, distancing from cues, DRA and imposing delays (pp. 202–207).

**Key terms**: operant/instrumental conditioning, discriminative stimulus, reinforcer, punisher, free-operant, discrete trials, Skinner box, cumulative recorder, shaping, chaining, primary and secondary reinforcers, token economy, negative contrast, differential reinforcement of alternative behavior (DRA), positive/negative reinforcement, positive/negative punishment, continuous and partial reinforcement, FR/VR/FI/VI schedules, postreinforcement pause, self-control, precommitment, concurrent schedule, matching law, behavioral economics, bliss point, Premack principle, response deprivation hypothesis, dorsal striatum, orbitofrontal cortex, incentive salience, hedonic vs. motivational value, endogenous opioids, insula, dACC

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1898–1911): puzzle boxes and law of effect (pp. 168–169). Skinner (1938): free-operant methods; Skinner (1953): variable ratio in gambling (pp. 170–172, 205).
- Breland and Breland (1951) on shaping; Stoltz and Lott (1964): rats running past food (pp. 168, 173).
- Kobre and Lipsitt (1972): negative contrast in infants (p. 177). Brown (1969): weak-to-strong shock undermines punishment (p. 179). Schlinger and Blakely (1994): reinforcement delay (p. 181).
- Herrnstein (1961): matching law (pp. 188–189). Premack (1959–1962): running wheel and pinball studies (pp. 190–191). Olds (1955–58): brain stimulation reward (pp. 195–196).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Feedback timing: immediate consequences produce faster learning, and reinforcement delay reduces it (p. 181), so give feedback as soon as possible after a response, and use "clicker-like" immediate signals when full outcomes are delayed (pp. 176, 181).
- Build complex skills in sequenced approximations (shaping/chaining, pp. 173–175), and reinforce what is wanted rather than rely on punishing errors, since punishment tends to produce varied rather than directed behavior (p. 178).
- Use the Premack idea (p. 191) and precommitment (p. 181) to structure study sessions: schedule a preferred activity contingent on completing the less-preferred study task, and set up commitments (study groups) to counter the pull of immediate rewards.

## Ch 6 — Generalization, Discrimination Learning, and Concept Formation (pp. 213–266)

**Core ideas**
- Generalization (transferring past learning to new situations) always requires a balance between specificity (a narrow rule) and generality (a broad one); the chapter organizes the topic around four cases: similar stimuli with the same outcome, similar stimuli with different outcomes, dissimilar stimuli with the same outcome, and dissimilar stimuli with different outcomes (pp. 213–214).
- Responses to test stimuli fall off smoothly with dissimilarity from the trained stimulus (a generalization gradient; Guttman and Kalish 1956). Shepard interpreted this as the learner estimating the "consequential region," the set of stimuli likely to share the same consequence (pp. 215–217).
- Models with one node per stimulus predict no generalization; models using distributed or shared-element representations, in which similar stimuli activate overlapping elements, produce realistic gradients (Thorndike, Estes, Rumelhart) (pp. 217–224).
- Discrimination training narrows the gradient (Jenkins and Harrison 1962; Wills and McLaren 1997 in humans). It can produce peak shift, where responding is highest to a stimulus displaced away from the unrewarded one, explained by summed excitatory and inhibitory gradients (pp. 225–229).
- Errorless discrimination learning (Terrace) starts with an easy, exaggerated contrast and gradually makes it harder, preventing errors and frustration (used successfully with children with Down syndrome), but the resulting learning can be rigid and may not transfer to new situations (pp. 229–230).
- Dissimilar stimuli can still be generalized between: through prior co-occurrence (sensory preconditioning), through sharing the same consequences (acquired equivalence), and combinations can mean something unlike their parts (negative patterning), which requires configural representation (e.g. two flashing taillights mean hazard) (pp. 230–236).
- Concepts are mental representations and categories are classes in the world; forming concepts needs both generalization within a class and discrimination across classes, as shown by pigeons sorting music or paintings by style. Natural categories are fuzzy and built around prototypes and family resemblance (Rosch), which support inductive inference (pp. 237–241).
- A common generalization error is faulty inverse reasoning: confusing "category members have the feature" with "having the feature implies category membership," e.g. interpreting a positive mammogram, which matters especially when the category is rare (pp. 241–242).
- Representations in the brain are shaped by importance: cortical maps expand for valued distinctions and compress irrelevant ones. Primary sensory cortex is needed for narrow gradients, tone-shock pairing (not mere exposure) retunes auditory neurons through nucleus basalis acetylcholine, and the hippocampal region supports meaning-based generalization (sensory preconditioning, acquired equivalence, latent inhibition) and compresses or differentiates stimulus representations (pp. 243–254).
- Clinical and social angles: schizophrenia impairs transferring learned associations to new contexts, and stereotypes are generalizations about people shaped by social transmission and confirmation bias; the chapter asks when a statistically grounded generalization about a group becomes unfair treatment of an individual (pp. 254–261).

**Key terms**: generalization, discrimination learning, generalization gradient, consequential region, stimulus representation, discrete-component representation, distributed representation, shared elements, stimulus control, intradimensional/extradimensional discrimination, peak shift, errorless discrimination learning, sensory preconditioning, acquired equivalence, negative patterning, configural node, combinatorial explosion, concept vs. category, category learning, prototype, family resemblance, inductive inference, topographic map, receptive field, cortical plasticity, nucleus basalis, hippocampal region, stereotype, confirmation bias

**Notable studies / people**
- Guttman and Kalish (1956): pigeon gradients (p. 215). Shepard (1987): consequential region (p. 216).
- Jenkins and Harrison (1962): discrimination sharpens the gradient (pp. 225–226). Wills and McLaren (1997): same pattern in people (p. 227). Hanson (1959): peak shift (p. 227).
- Terrace (1963): errorless discrimination; Duffy and Wishart (1987): children and shapes (pp. 229–230).
- Kehoe (1988): negative patterning in rabbits (p. 234). Hall and colleagues (1993): acquired equivalence (pp. 232–233).
- Rosch (1973, 1975): prototype structure of natural categories (p. 241). Weinberger (1993): remapping in guinea pig auditory cortex (p. 249). Gluck and Myers (1993, 2001): hippocampal compression/differentiation model (p. 253). Myers et al. (2003): acquired equivalence in schizophrenia (p. 255).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Contrastive training sharpens boundaries (pp. 226–227): teaching similar concepts side by side with labeled examples of each (as with the music-style teacher example, p. 237) should narrow overgeneralization compared with showing only one category.
- The easy-to-hard sequencing of errorless learning (p. 229) supports scaffolded progression, but its rigidity (p. 230) is a caution to follow it with varied, harder, mixed practice for transfer.
- Because learning does not automatically transfer to new contexts or recombined cues (pp. 233–236, 255–258), design practice and assessment with novel examples and variations, not just repeats of the trained cases.

## Ch 7 — Episodic and Semantic Memory (pp. 267–310)

**Core ideas**
- The patient H.M., whose bilateral medial temporal lobes were removed to treat epilepsy, lost the ability to form new fact and event memories while keeping personality, attention-dependent performance and some skill learning, showing that such memories rely on distinct brain regions (pp. 267–268, 270).
- Episodic memory is memory for personally experienced events tagged with place and time ("I remember"); semantic memory is knowledge of facts, not necessarily tied to when and where it was learned ("I know"). Both are consciously accessible and flexibly communicable (together called declarative or explicit memory), unlike skill memories. Repeating a fact strengthens its semantic memory, whereas repeated similar events can blur individual episodic memories (as in parking-lot confusion) (pp. 268–271).
- Encoding principles: (1) mere exposure does not guarantee memory (BBC call-number announcements; people cannot reliably draw a penny); (2) information tied to prior knowledge is remembered better (Bransford and Johnson: a paragraph recalled twice as well when the topic was given before, not after), which supports previewing a chapter or doing readings before lecture; (3) deeper, meaning-based processing beats shallow processing (levels-of-processing effect) (pp. 274–276).
- Retrieval principles: retrieval succeeds more when test conditions match encoding conditions (transfer-appropriate processing; Godden and Baddeley's divers; practicing in the format you will be tested on), and more cues means better performance (free recall is harder than cued recall, which is harder than recognition; Bahrick's high school classmates) (pp. 277–278).
- Testing is itself a learning event: students who freely recalled a passage remembered more a week later (about 55% of ideas) than those who re-read it (about 40%), even with no feedback. "Desirable difficulties" suggest effortful retrieval, including failed attempts, helps retention; the book recommends self-testing, flashcards or blank-page recall, and notes that in-class quizzes also improve later exam performance, including on untested material (pp. 278–280).
- Memory fails through forgetting (steepest early, Ebbinghaus-like curve; directed forgetting is possible but imperfect), proactive and retroactive interference, source monitoring errors (including the DRM false-recognition effect and inadvertent plagiarism), and false memories (Loftus and others), which imagination can create and which matter for eyewitness testimony and confessions (pp. 280–286).
- New memories go through a consolidation period, and recalled memories can become labile again and be updated (reconsolidation), so memory is dynamic and can be distorted each time it is retrieved (pp. 286–288).
- Metamemory is error-prone and overconfident: people predict they will remember equally well after a day or week as immediately, and few college students self-test (11% in one poll), with most of those doing so just to assess rather than to learn, so students often misjudge how well they have learned (pp. 288–289).
- Brain: semantic knowledge is distributed across sensory and association cortex; the hippocampus is critical for new episodic memory (patient E.P.; fMRI "subsequent memory" shows greater hippocampal and left prefrontal activity for items later remembered); standard consolidation and multiple trace theories compete on whether older episodic memory leaves the hippocampus; the frontal cortex helps decide what is stored and supports source memory and metamemory; the basal forebrain and diencephalon also matter (pp. 290–301).
- Temporary amnesias (transient global amnesia, functional amnesia) and the lasting cost of concussion illustrate how memory can be disrupted without permanent loss of memory machinery in some cases (pp. 302–304).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Prefer retrieval practice over re-reading: free-recall "brain dumps," flashcards and low-stakes quizzes yielded better recall than restudy, and even unsuccessful tries helped (pp. 278–280). Quizzes in lectures help even for untested content (p. 280).
- Match practice to the eventual test format (essay vs. multiple choice) (p. 278), and give a topic or overview before detail so learners can relate new material to prior knowledge (p. 275); to push for meaning, ask learners to process material by its meaning and relations (p. 276).
- Teach learners about metamemory pitfalls (p. 289): predicted retention is overestimated, self-testing is underused, and "feels familiar" is not "can recall"; to reduce interference, space or separate confusable items and be wary of re-exposing learners to misinformation or suggestive cues (pp. 281–286).

## Ch 8 — Skill Memory (pp. 311–350)

**Core ideas**
- Skills are abilities that improve with practice and come in two broad kinds: perceptual-motor skills (sensory-guided movement patterns) and cognitive skills (problem solving and strategies, like reading, budgeting, or the Tower of Hanoi puzzle). Both are learned with similar memory mechanisms, and many real skills mix both and mix open (adapting to a changing environment) with closed (fixed sequence) elements (pp. 311–314).
- Compared with fact and event memories, skill memories are hard to convey except by demonstration, may be acquired without awareness, and need many repetitions. Perceptual-motor learning appears to underlie cognitive skills developmentally, and animals also learn cognitive-type skills such as tool use (pp. 312–316).
- Repetition alone does not improve skill; feedback (knowledge of results) is critical. Thorndike's blindfolded line-drawers improved only with feedback (p. 317). Practice follows a power law, with big early gains and ever smaller later gains, but a new source of feedback (e.g. a film of ideal movement) can restart improvement (pp. 317–318).
- Feedback frequency matters: frequent feedback in simple perceptual-motor tasks helps short-term performance but gives poorer long-term outcomes, whereas infrequent feedback does the opposite; immediate-feedback online tutorials have been reported to speed learning compared with classroom instruction (p. 318).
- Practice scheduling: massed practice gives better performance in the short term but spaced practice often yields better retention; postal trainees practicing one hour a day needed fewer total hours than those practicing four, though they were least satisfied with their schedule, so satisfaction is not a good gauge of learning (p. 319). Variable practice generally beats constant practice in later tests, even when it slows early progress, and gradual training (starting easy, increasing difficulty) can outperform practicing the hard version from the start (pp. 319–320).
- Learning can be implicit, as in the serial reaction time task, where sequences are learned without awareness, and in amnesic patients such as H.M.; yet reaching elite levels likely requires conscious commitment to deliberate practice (pp. 320–322).
- Fitts's three stages: cognitive (effortful, rule-based), associative (stereotyped actions) and autonomous (automatic motor programs, where thinking too much can impair performance). Experts also perceive differently (chess masters and skilled athletes scan and anticipate more efficiently); the "10,000 hours" claim is contested, though total practice remains critical, and talent shows up more as practice increases (twin study) (pp. 322–327).
- Skill transfer can be specific: Thorndike's identical elements theory predicts transfer in proportion to shared elements, yet learning-set formation ("learning to learn") lets frequent experience with similar tasks speed new ones, as with professional actors' encoding strategies (p. 328). Skills decay with non-use, fastest soon after last practice, and new learning can interfere with recently learned skills; sleep improved finger-sequence performance (pp. 329–330).
- Brain: basal ganglia (linking cues to responses; impaired in Parkinson's disease), cerebral cortex (relevant maps expand with practice, e.g. violinists' fingering hand) and cerebellum (timing) all contribute (pp. 331–344). Parkinson's disease impairs some skill learning (p. 345), and motor prostheses that read neural signals must themselves be learned through practice (pp. 346–347).

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1927): feedback needed for improvement (p. 317). Newell and Rosenbaum (1981) and Hatze (1976): power law and film feedback (pp. 317–318).
- Baddeley and Longman (1978): spaced training of postal workers (p. 319). Wulf and Schmidt (1997): variable practice (p. 319).
- Fitts (1964): three stages (p. 323). Ericsson and colleagues (1993): deliberate practice (p. 324). Fox et al. (1996): twins on rotary pursuit (pp. 325–326).
- Walker et al. (2002–2003): sleep and finger-tapping skill (p. 330). Packard et al. (1989): rat maze dissociations (pp. 333–334). Jog et al. (1999): basal ganglia changes (pp. 335–336). Karni et al. (1998): motor cortex expansion (p. 340). Elbert et al. (1995): violinists' cortex (p. 338).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Build in feedback: give specific knowledge of results and, once progress plateaus, add new feedback sources (models, video, expert demonstrations) (pp. 317–318); consider fading feedback frequency to support long-term retention (p. 318).
- Schedule practice in spaced sessions with variation and a gradual ramp in difficulty (pp. 319–320), and do not rely on learners' satisfaction or feeling of progress to judge the schedule (p. 319).
- Expect transfer to be limited to similar elements, so practise in conditions resembling the target performance (including scrimmage-like practice) and give a range of tasks to encourage learning-to-learn (p. 328). Plan for decay with refresher practice (p. 329).

## Ch 9 — Working Memory and Cognitive Control (pp. 351–394)

**Core ideas**
- Working memory is the active, temporary maintenance and manipulation of information for thinking and decisions; cognitive control is its use for planning, task switching, attention, stimulus selection and inhibition. The opening example of a student juggling errands and divided attention illustrates the cost of multitasking (pp. 351–352).
- In the Atkinson–Shiffrin framework, information passes from sensory memory (very brief; Sperling's partial-report method showed more is registered than can be reported) to short-term memory (kept alive by rehearsal, limited in capacity, lost with distraction) and then to long-term memory (pp. 352–354).
- Short-term capacity is limited, roughly five to nine items centered nearer five, but is determined by the number of meaningful units, so recoding into chunks that link to long-term knowledge expands what can be held (e.g. 11 digits recoded as three famous dates; phone numbers split into chunks) (pp. 354–356).
- Baddeley's model has a phonological loop (about two seconds of rehearsable speech; word-length effect; disrupted by speaking aloud), a visuospatial sketchpad, and a central executive that manipulates them and moves information to and from long-term memory. The two buffers are independent, supported by dual-task interference studies (pp. 356–359).
- A newer view treats working memory less as a separate place and more as a state in which long-term memory contents are activated and attended to (Cowan), with capacity limited by attentional bandwidth (pp. 360–361).
- Cognitive control includes updating the buffers (n-back, self-ordered search), goal setting and planning (Tower of Hanoi, which requires tracking subgoals), task switching (Wisconsin Card Sorting Test) and stimulus selection/response inhibition (Stroop task, overriding automatic habits in favor of context-appropriate rules) (pp. 361–368).
- Working-memory control correlates strongly with intelligence measures (verbal and nonverbal), which the authors link to juggling more rules, goals and ideas rather than faster processing (pp. 369–370).
- Brain: the prefrontal cortex is central; working-memory maintenance emerges from frontal-posterior circuits, abstraction of goals maps along a front-to-back frontal gradient that matures into late adolescence, and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex helps strategically retrieve long-term memories, including their source (pp. 371–385).
- The chapter box on easing working-memory load says verbal and visuospatial stores each hold about 3 to 5 items, advises converting some words to pictures to use both, warns that multitasking and stress degrade working memory (and make people overrate their performance), and recommends writing things down as an external buffer (p. 382).
- Clinical: working-memory and control deficits appear in schizophrenia and ADHD, where sustaining thoughts over delays and inhibiting distraction are difficult (pp. 386–390).

**Key terms**: working memory, cognitive control (executive function), sensory memory, short-term memory, long-term memory, Atkinson–Shiffrin model, chunking, digit span, central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, word-length effect, dual-task paradigm, delayed nonmatch-to-sample, multi-store vs. unitary-store (state-based) models, n-back, self-ordered task, Tower of Hanoi, task switching, Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, Stroop task, automatic vs. supervisory processes, bottom-up vs. top-down, prefrontal cortex, dorsolateral and ventrolateral PFC, goal abstraction gradient, ADHD

**Notable studies / people**
- Sperling (1960): partial report of letter arrays (pp. 353–354). Miller (1956): capacity and recoding (pp. 354–355).
- Baddeley and Hitch (1974): working memory model (pp. 356–357). Baddeley et al. (1975): word-length effect (p. 358). Brooks (1968): dual-task with visual vs. verbal responses (p. 358).
- Cowan (1995): state-based view (p. 361). Petrides: self-ordered tasks and PFC subdivisions (pp. 364–365, 385). Norman and Shallice (1980/1986): automatic vs. supervisory control (p. 368). Stroop (1935) (p. 368).
- Daneman and Carpenter (1980): working-memory span and reading comprehension (p. 369). Badre and D'Esposito: abstraction gradient (p. 383).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Respect small working-memory limits (pp. 354–356, 382): break material into meaningful chunks, avoid presenting many new items at once, and link items to prior knowledge so learners can recode them.
- Use both channels without overloading either (p. 382): combine words with pictures or diagrams (the book's tip is to convert some words into images), since verbal and visuospatial stores are independent, while avoiding competing tasks within the same channel (pp. 357–359).
- Discourage multitasking during study and lessons (p. 382), reduce stress, and provide external aids such as notes or checklists to offload working memory; younger learners need concrete subgoals rather than abstract goals (p. 384).

## Ch 10 — Emotional Influences on Learning and Memory (pp. 395–438)

**Core ideas**
- Emotion is a cluster of physiological responses, overt behaviors and conscious feelings. Some basic emotions appear universal (Ekman), while display rules vary by culture. Arousal (fight-or-flight) is mediated by the autonomic nervous system and stress hormones such as epinephrine and cortisol (pp. 396–398).
- Theories of emotion differ on the order of arousal and feeling: James–Lange (body first), Cannon–Bard (simultaneous), and two-factor theory (arousal interpreted via context, as in the swaying-bridge study, where aroused men were more likely to call the interviewer) (pp. 398–401).
- Learned fear is classical conditioning of emotional responses; avoidance learning blends classical and operant processes and is highly resistant to extinction, and learned helplessness (animals exposed to inescapable shock later fail to escape) is proposed as a model for human depression (pp. 406–409).
- The box on guarding against learned helplessness suggests that prior experience of successfully escaping a problem protects against helplessness, and applies this to education: a child who thinks "I'm bad at math" might be helped by mixing hard problems with solvable ones so success builds a sense of control (p. 409).
- Emotional arousal strengthens episodic memory. Rehearsal contributes, but emotion also boosts encoding: people who heard an emotional story about a boy's accident recalled the dramatic middle section better than those given a neutral version of the same pictures (Cahill et al.) (pp. 409–410).
- Retrieval is mood-congruent: people in a happy mood recall more positive memories and those in a sad mood more negative ones, which can create a vicious circle in depression (p. 411). Vivid "flashbulb" memories of dramatic events are held with high confidence but are not necessarily more accurate than other memories and can drift each time they are recalled (pp. 411–413, 424).
- The amygdala modulates how strongly other regions store emotional memories; stress hormones (epinephrine, glucocorticoids) are the pathway, blocking them removes the emotional enhancement of memory, and amygdala activity at encoding predicts later "remembering" with context (pp. 416–423).
- The box on the benefits of mild stress: mild stress and low levels of stress hormones can aid memory formation and recall in the hippocampus, but chronic or high stress impairs recall and can damage hippocampal neurons, so calming down (breathing, exercise, meditation, controlling stressors) helps test performance (p. 423).
- Clinically, phobias and PTSD reflect strong conditioned fear that fails to extinguish, and exposure/extinction therapies (including virtual reality) are widely used (pp. 428–432).

**Key terms**: emotion, fear response, universal emotions, arousal (fight-or-flight), autonomic nervous system, stress hormones, epinephrine, glucocorticoid, cortisol, James–Lange, Cannon–Bard and two-factor theories, conditioned emotional response, avoidance learning, learned helplessness, flashbulb memory, mood congruency, amygdala (lateral, central, basolateral nuclei), reconsolidation, phobia, PTSD, extinction therapy

**Notable studies / people**
- Ekman and Friesen (1971): cross-cultural recognition of expressions (p. 397). Schachter and Singer (1962): epinephrine and context (p. 400). Dutton and Aron (1974): high bridge study (p. 401).
- Seligman and Maier (1967): learned helplessness (p. 408). Cahill and colleagues (1995): emotional story and memory (p. 410). Eich et al. (1994): mood congruency (p. 411).
- Brown and Kulik (1977); Neisser (1982); Talarico and Rubin (2003): flashbulb memories (pp. 411–413).
- McGaugh, Cahill and colleagues: stress hormones and amygdala modulation (pp. 421–423).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Material that is emotionally engaging or meaningful is better encoded (pp. 409–410), but emotional intensity does not guarantee accuracy (pp. 412–413), so pair engagement with verification of facts.
- Keep stress at a moderate level during learning and testing; very high or chronic stress impairs recall, and calming strategies can help restore performance (p. 423).
- Structure early tasks so learners succeed and experience control, mixing hard items with solvable ones, to guard against learned helplessness (p. 409).

## Ch 11 — Social Learning and Memory (pp. 439–472)

**Core ideas**
- Social (observational) learning means actively monitoring others and choosing later actions based on what was observed. Unlike conditioning, what an observer takes from watching is hard to predict or detect, because the observer may show no immediate change and nothing forces copying a model (pp. 440–441).
- Bandura's Bobo doll studies showed children reproducing an adult's aggressive actions without any reinforcement or punishment, suggesting that the learning is encoded during observation and that imitation later reveals the stored memory. Context and provocation mattered: unprovoked children who saw aggression were actually less aggressive (pp. 441–442).
- Contemporary social learning theory holds that behavior can be learned from observation alone, with expected rewards and punishments influencing whether the learned action is performed (p. 442).
- Bandura's four processes for copying are attention to the model, retention in memory, ability to reproduce the action, and motivation, with motivation raised by a model's status, similarity to the observer and the attractiveness of the outcome (p. 443).
- Copying comes in forms: true imitation (reproducing motor acts) and emulation (reproducing the outcome with different actions). In two-action tests children copied details of a box-opening method more than chimpanzees and, notably, adults tended to emulate like the chimpanzees. Dolphins and apes can imitate on command (pp. 443–445).
- Behaviors that look like imitation can arise from simpler mechanisms such as emotional contagion, observational conditioning and stimulus enhancement (p. 446).
- Vocal imitation, vocal learning (e.g. song learning in birds with a template, practice and use phases), and social transmission of information (including food preferences in rats, conformity effects, and learning from film in the kicking study) extend social learning; humans transmit information across media and cultures far more widely than other animals (pp. 449–454).
- Brain: mirror neurons, song-learning circuits and hippocampal encoding of socially transmitted food preferences are discussed (pp. 460–464), and clinically, autism spectrum disorder and imitation deficits after stroke (pp. 464–468).

**Key terms**: social learning, observational learning, modeling, copying, true imitation, emulation, two-action test, do-as-I-do task, perspective taking, social learning theory, emotional contagion, observational conditioning, stimulus enhancement, vocal imitation, vocal learning, template model of song learning, social transmission of information, social conformity, mirror neurons, autism spectrum disorder

**Notable studies / people**
- Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961); Bandura (1969, 1986): Bobo doll studies and four processes (pp. 441–443).
- Whiten et al. (1996): children vs. chimpanzees on a box-opening task (p. 444). Hayes and Hayes (1952): "Do this!" training (p. 444). Nagell, Olguin and Tomasello (1993): rake emulation (p. 445).
- Galef and Wigmore (1983): social transmission of food preferences in rats (p. 454).
- The kicking-film study (Hatze 1976, discussed in ch. 8) is cited as socially transferred skill information (p. 453).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Demonstrations work as learning events even without immediate practice or reward (pp. 441–442), so worked examples and modeled solutions are legitimate teaching tools; the model's attention-getting, status and similarity to the learner influence uptake (p. 443).
- Because observers may focus on different aspects than the teacher intends and may not show what they learned (p. 440), check learning explicitly and distinguish copying steps from understanding goals (imitation vs. emulation, pp. 443–445).
- Telling learners the outcome-value of a behavior, and showing the consequences models receive, supports motivation to reproduce what was learned (p. 443).

## Ch 12 — Development and Aging (pp. 473–516)

**Core ideas**
- The chapter stresses that it describes group averages with wide individual variation; different memory abilities mature at different rates, so teaching can be tailored to age while recognizing individual differences (p. 474).
- Learning begins before birth: fetuses habituate to repeated sounds and respond again to novel ones, and newborns prefer and can be conditioned to a story their mothers read during late pregnancy, so infants are not blank slates (pp. 474–475).
- Infants learn through habituation, operant conditioning (kicking to move a mobile, in context-dependent fashion) and delay eyeblink conditioning, but harder tasks such as trace conditioning develop later; infants also show sophisticated generalization and expectations, and elicited-imitation tasks reveal memory in preverbal infants (pp. 475–477).
- Semantic memory develops before episodic memory; episodic-like memory in young children appears in behavior before it appears in verbal report, partly because hippocampus and prefrontal cortex mature slowly (pp. 477–479, Figure 12.2).
- Sensitive periods exist for imprinting, visual cortex tuning (Hubel and Wiesel), birdsong and aspects of language, where early exposure is much more effective than later (e.g. infants lose the ability to distinguish non-native speech sounds by about a year; children approximate native accents better). The book also notes disagreement: adults often progress faster in a new language using explicit memorization, whereas children rely on social imitation (pp. 479–482). Toddlers' gesturing correlates with later vocabulary, and the book offers possible reasons, including gestures eliciting more language from parents and serving as scaffolding (p. 482).
- Working memory and executive function mature latest; digit span grows from about three to four digits in young children to the adult level of about seven in late adolescence, and performance on n-back-type tasks keeps improving into young adulthood (p. 483–484).
- In healthy adults cognitive abilities including verbal memory are fairly stable from the twenties to fifties and decline thereafter (Seattle Longitudinal Study), with large individual differences. Working memory declines first (digit span about 6 to 6.5 in the elderly), possibly via greater proactive interference (pp. 485–486).
- Older adults learn new conditioned responses and skills more slowly but can still learn them, and highly practiced skills are often maintained; semantic knowledge and old memories remain strong, while forming new episodic/semantic memories is harder because of encoding difficulty. Slower presentation and meaningful material improve older adults' paired-associate learning (pp. 486–487).
- Older adults remember positive information better than negative, unlike younger adults (pp. 487–488). Genes and epigenetics (pp. 489-494), early overproduction then pruning of neurons and synapses (pp. 494-496), adolescent brain changes (pp. 496-498) and brain changes from adulthood to old age (pp. 499-504) underlie these patterns.
- The box on mental exercise and Alzheimer's disease says active mental pastimes, education and social networks correlate with lower Alzheimer's risk (consistent with cognitive reserve), but evidence is observational and an expert panel found insufficient evidence that mental exercise protects; a mix of healthy lifestyle choices may help (p. 513). The box on classical music concludes there is no solid evidence that classical music makes babies smarter (p. 493).
- Clinical sections cover Down syndrome and Alzheimer's disease, including amyloid plaques, tau tangles, and their link via chromosome 21 (pp. 505-514).

**Notable studies / people**
- Hepper and Shahidullah (1992): fetal habituation (p. 474). DeCasper and Spence (1986): prenatal story learning (p. 475).
- Rovee-Collier and colleagues: infant mobile conditioning and context dependence (p. 476). Hayne and Imuta (2011): episodic-like memory in young children (p. 479).
- Lorenz (1935): imprinting (p. 479). Hubel and Wiesel: visual sensitive period (p. 480). Marler (1970): sparrow song (p. 480). Curtiss (1977): the case of "Genie" (pp. 480–481).
- Schaie (2005): Seattle Longitudinal Study (p. 485). Canestrari (1963): slower pace helps older adults (p. 487). Charles, Mather and Carstensen (2003): positivity effect in older adults (pp. 487–488).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Match demands to maturity: young children need concrete, shorter tasks because working memory and executive control are still developing (pp. 483–484), and early years may offer sensitive periods for phonological and language learning (pp. 480–482).
- For older learners, slow the pace, use meaningful and familiar material, and allow more practice and time (pp. 486–487); old knowledge can be a strength for building new learning on.
- Be cautious about claims of easy cognitive enhancement from a single activity such as classical music (p. 493) or mental games (p. 513); the stronger-supported ideas are active engagement and varied stimulation across the lifespan (pp. 513, 44).

## Cross-cutting takeaways for teaching and learning

Ranked roughly by how directly the book's evidence supports the practice. Items marked (inference) go slightly beyond what the authors state.

1. Retrieving beats re-reading. Writing down all you can recall of a passage produced better recall a week later than re-reading it, even without feedback, and effortful or failed retrieval attempts are described as helping ("desirable difficulties"). The authors suggest flashcards, self-quizzes and blank-page recall, and report that interrupting lectures with short quizzes improves later exam performance, including on untested material (pp. 278–280).
2. Practice needs feedback, and the type and timing of feedback matter. Repetition alone did not improve blindfolded line-drawing; knowledge of results did. A new source of feedback (e.g. a film of an ideal movement) can restart progress after a plateau, frequent feedback helps short-term performance while less frequent feedback can help long-term performance, and immediate-feedback online tutorials are reported to speed learning (pp. 317–318). Immediate outcomes produce faster operant learning than delayed ones (p. 181).
3. Spread practice out, vary it, and ramp up difficulty. Spaced practice produced proficiency with fewer total hours than massed practice even though trainees liked it least; variable practice and gradually increasing difficulty often give better later performance (pp. 319–320). In habituation, spaced exposure is slower but longer lasting (p. 76).
4. Give learners an organizing frame first and process for meaning. A paragraph recalled twice as well when its topic was given beforehand (not afterwards), supporting previewing chapters and pre-reading; deeper, meaning-based processing at encoding improves later memory; mere exposure and sheer repetition do not guarantee learning (pp. 274–276).
5. Match practice to the way knowledge will be used. Retrieval is best when study and test conditions or formats match (transfer-appropriate processing), skills transfer to the extent that elements are shared (identical elements), and learning-to-learn emerges from experience with many similar tasks (pp. 277–278, 328).
6. Teach learners that their self-assessments are unreliable. Judgments of learning and feeling of knowing err toward overconfidence; students predict equal recall after a day or a week; few use self-testing (11% in one poll); satisfaction with a training schedule did not track learning (pp. 288–289, 319).
7. Respect working-memory limits. Capacity is small (about 5 to 9 items, nearer 3 to 5 in each of verbal and visual stores), chunking links items to long-term knowledge, using both verbal and visual channels spreads load, and multitasking and stress degrade performance while hiding the decline from the person (pp. 354–356, 382).
8. Contrast and sequencing shape what is learned. Discrimination training with labeled examples of both categories narrows overgeneralization (pp. 226–227); an easy-to-hard progression allows nearly errorless learning but can yield rigid, poorly transferring knowledge (pp. 229–230); prior learning about a cue can block learning about a redundant one, so new information must add predictive value (pp. 131, 137).
9. Prefer reinforcing the desired response and building complex skills stepwise. Punishment tends to increase variable behavior, can teach concealment when signalled, and loses force if escalated gradually; differential reinforcement of alternatives, shaping and chaining are the recommended alternatives (pp. 173–175, 178–179). Mixing hard and solvable problems may protect against learned helplessness in students who attribute failure to fixed inability (p. 409).
10. Memory is reconstructive, so guard against interference and suggestion. Similar items interfere both ways, source errors and false memories can be created by imagining or by suggestive prompts, and each retrieval can alter a memory, which matters for how instructors and interviewers question learners (pp. 281–288).
11. Engagement helps but must be balanced. Emotionally arousing material is encoded and retained better, and mild stress can aid memory, whereas high or chronic stress impairs recall; vivid memories feel accurate but are not necessarily so (pp. 409–413, 423).
12. Basic conditions of learning: attend, sleep, and offload. The opening tips stress full attention, linking new material to prior knowledge, imagery, multiple senses, writing things down, rhyme, good sleep and relaxing when retrieval stalls; the book later shows sleep improving skill performance (pp. 3, 330).
13. Adjust for age and maturity. Children need concrete subgoals while executive function matures into late adolescence (pp. 383–384, 483–484); older adults learn new material more slowly and benefit from slower pacing and meaningful content while retaining strong semantic knowledge (pp. 486–487).
14. Demonstrations teach even without immediate reward. Observing a model can produce learning that shows up later, and models who are attention-getting, admired or similar to the learner are copied more (pp. 441–443). (inference: worked examples and modeled solutions are legitimate teaching tools.)
15. Be cautious with shortcuts. The book finds weak support for claims that classical music, brain-boosting drugs or mental games reliably enhance cognition; the dependable route remains the hard work of learning described above (pp. 63, 493, 513).
