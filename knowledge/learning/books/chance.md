# Chance & Furlong, Learning and Behavior — chapter digest

Chance, P., & Furlong, E. E. (2023). *Learning and Behavior* (Active Learning Edition, 8th ed.). Cengage.

Orientation: an introductory learning-psychology text written from a behavior-analytic standpoint. It defines learning as a change in behavior due to experience, builds from Pavlovian and operant principles through observational learning, stimulus control and forgetting, and keeps returning to practical applications (therapy, education, parenting, animal training). Page cites are printed pages.

## Ch 1 — Introduction: Learning to Change (pp. 1–32)

**Core ideas**
- The chapter frames change as the constant that every organism must cope with, and presents learning as first of all a survival mechanism rather than a tool for academic skills (p. 1).
- Darwin's natural selection rests on the fact that offspring outnumber what resources can support, that individuals vary, and that useful variations get inherited; nature plays the role that a breeder plays in artificial selection. Without variation, selection has nothing to work on (pp. 3–4).
- Complex organs and brains build up gradually over many generations from simple precursors (light-sensitive cells to eyes; nerve nets to brains). Selection has no goal and its products are workable rather than optimal (pp. 5–6).
- Selection responds to environmental change: smaller Soay sheep as winters warm, paler lizards on white dunes, dark peppered moths in polluted woods, guppies evolving under new predators, and human skin tone tracking sunlight. When the environment stays put, the species stays put (pp. 6–9). Behavior can be selected just as bodies are (partridge chicks flapping stubby wings to climb) (p. 9).
- Selection yields three kinds of inborn behavior: reflexes (a specific stimulus tied to a simple response), modal action patterns (stereotyped, whole-organism sequences triggered by a releaser), and general behavior traits (broad tendencies such as boldness or aggression that appear across many situations and are more variable) (pp. 10–15).
- Genes demonstrably shape behavior traits, shown through selective breeding of fearful versus non-fearful rats, gene-knockout mice, and twin studies in people. Which trait level pays off depends on the local threat (pp. 15–17).
- Selection's main weakness is speed. It needs generations, so it cannot rescue species from abrupt change (invasive species, hunting, pandemics) and leaves behaviors that are now maladaptive, such as the human taste for salt and sugar. Mutation and hybridization help only slowly or unreliably (pp. 17–20).
- Learning is defined as a change in behavior due to experience, where experience means environmental change (stimuli). It is deliberately "change" rather than "acquisition," since reducing a behavior is learning too; durability is not required; behavior means anything measurable an organism does, including covert thinking and feelings; drugs, injury, disease and aging changes do not count; and behavior (not brain scans) remains the reliable measure of learning (pp. 20–24).
- Habituation, the decline of a reflex response with repeated stimulation, is the simplest example of learning and has survival value because it filters out harmless repetition. Its rate depends on stimulus intensity, number of repetitions and spacing (pp. 25–27).
- Nature versus nurture is a false split: the two are intertwined (heron/egret chick aggression depends on parental feeding; kittens that watched their mothers kill rats were far more likely to kill rats), and both heredity and prior experience shape learning ability itself (pp. 27–29).

**Key terms**: natural selection, artificial selection, reflex, modal action pattern (MAP), releaser, general behavior trait, mutation, hybridization, learning, behavior, experience, stimuli, habituation, nature–nurture.

**Notable studies / people**
- Darwin (1859) and Malthus (1798): competition for scarce resources plus inherited variation gives natural selection (pp. 3–4).
- Kettlewell (1959): dark peppered moths rose in polluted areas and fell again as air cleared (pp. 6–7).
- Gordon et al. (2009): guppies moved to a predator stream evolved higher offspring survival within about eight years (p. 7).
- Hall (1951): rats bred for fearfulness diverged over ten generations (p. 16).
- Shumyatsky et al. (2005): mice lacking one gene were bolder in an unfamiliar space (pp. 15–16).
- Schneirla (1944): army ants on a flat surface circle endlessly, showing the mechanical nature of MAPs (p. 12).
- Kuo (1930): kittens reared with mothers that killed rats mostly became rat killers (86% versus 45%) (p. 29).
- Mock (2006): cross-fostering showed chick aggression depends on parental feeding behavior (pp. 28–29).
- Sharpless & Jasper (1956), Bridger (1961): habituation in cats' brain waves and infants' heart rate (p. 25).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Because the book defines learning by measurable behavior change, define learning objectives as observable performances (what the learner can now do or do differently), not as "understanding" (pp. 21–23).
- Teach that learning includes dropping or reducing habits, not just adding content, so plan for unlearning of misconceptions as an explicit goal (p. 21).
- Habituation shows that repeating the same stimulus dulls response; vary examples and presentation to keep material from fading into background (pp. 25–26).

## Ch 2 — The Study of Learning and Behavior (pp. 33–54)

**Core ideas**
- The book adopts a natural-science stance with four assumptions: events have causes; causes come before effects; causes are natural events (not mind, spirits or willpower); and the simplest explanation that fits the data wins (parsimony). The student who "studies for a future grade" is explained by past experience of studying paying off, since a future event cannot cause present behavior (pp. 34–35).
- Because learning is a change in behavior, it must be measured behaviorally. The chapter lists seven measures: errors, topography (form), intensity, speed, latency (delay before starting), rate (occurrences per unit time) and fluency (correct responses per minute, combining accuracy and rate). Learning can mean an increase or a decrease on any of them (pp. 36–41).
- A cumulative record plots total responses over time; a steeper slope means a higher rate, a flat line means no responding, and the line can never fall (pp. 39–41).
- Four sources of evidence rank from weakest to strongest. Anecdotes (including "everybody knows" wisdom and positive anecdotes) are unreliable but can suggest leads; case studies are systematic but rest on few cases, cannot establish cause, and often rely on unreliable reports; descriptive studies use many cases and statistics but cannot test why a pattern exists; experiments manipulate an independent variable and measure a dependent variable (pp. 42–44).
- Between-subjects experiments compare experimental and control groups, using random assignment and matched sampling to even out pre-existing differences, with larger groups helping. Within-subjects experiments compare each participant against their own baseline (A) and treatment (B) periods; an ABA reversal repeats the switch to rule out outside events, like flipping a switch on and off (pp. 44–48).
- Experiments are criticized as artificial. The book answers that simple behaviors and sterile settings give control so the effect of the independent variable can be isolated; laboratory findings are then tested in field experiments in realistic settings (pp. 48–49).
- Animals are used because researchers can control genetics and learning history and can do studies that would be unethical with people. Findings must be cautiously generalized, have yielded practical applications (child rearing, education, business, sport, humane animal training), are governed by welfare guidelines, and cannot be replaced by simulations because the effects must be understood before they can be programmed (pp. 49–52).

**Key terms**: natural science approach, law of parsimony, errors, topography, intensity, speed, latency, rate, cumulative recorder, cumulative record, fluency, anecdote, case study, descriptive study, experiment, independent variable, dependent variable, between-subjects experiment, within-subjects experiment, experimental group, control group, random assignment, matched sampling, baseline period, ABA reversal design, aversives.

**Notable studies / people**
- Semmelweis (1840s): assumed hospital childbed-fever deaths had a cause, which led to changed practice (p. 34).
- Ptolemy versus Copernicus: parsimony illustrated by the simpler heliocentric theory replacing the patched geocentric one (p. 35).
- Tolman & Honzik (1930): maze errors and running time as learning measures (pp. 36–38).
- Bryan & Harter (1899): Morse code reception rate rising over weeks (p. 40).
- Hooker (1950s): showed the "neurosis" seen in gay clients was also seen in heterosexual clients, undermining a case-study inference (p. 43).
- Rosenkrans & Hartup (1967): between-subjects imitation of aggression (p. 45).
- Sidman (1960/1988): ABA reversal design (p. 47).
- Whole Language reading anecdote (Sam): shows why single stories cannot confirm or refute a method (pp. 42–43).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Use fluency (correct answers per minute) and latency (hesitation before answering) to judge mastery of facts such as arithmetic or vocabulary, not just percent correct (pp. 39, 41).
- Judge a teaching method by comparison data (control group, or your own baseline versus intervention with a reversal check), not by testimonials or one success story (pp. 42–47).
- Track learner progress over time with a simple chart (errors or rate per session) so change is visible (pp. 36, 40).

## Ch 3 — Pavlovian Conditioning (pp. 55–96)

**Core ideas**
- Pavlov's digestion research showed that salivary glands adjust to the stimulus at hand, and that dogs began salivating before food arrived once they had been fed in the lab repeatedly. He chose to study these "psychic" secretions by recording every external stimulus present when they occurred (pp. 56–59).
- An unconditional reflex (US evokes UR) is innate and stable; a conditional reflex (CS evokes CR) is acquired through experience, varies between individuals, and can fade. Conditioning pairs a neutral stimulus with a US, and the pairing occurs regardless of what the learner does; the response is a reflex (pp. 59–62).
- Higher-order conditioning lets a neutral stimulus gain power by pairing with an established CS rather than with the US (black square, metronome, food). It extends Pavlovian learning widely, including the emotional meaning of words (nonsense syllables paired with pleasant or unpleasant words took on those feelings), though the response weakens with each step from the US (pp. 63–64).
- Learning is measured by shortening response latency, by test (probe) trials where the CS appears alone, and by CR strength. Pseudoconditioning, in which a strong US merely sensitizes the learner so that any stimulus provokes a response, is ruled out by a control group that gets CS and US unpaired (pp. 65–66).
- Presentation timing matters: trace (CS ends before US) and delay (CS overlaps US onset) work; simultaneous and backward pairings work poorly. A flowchart on p. 71 helps classify examples (pp. 67–71).
- Rate of conditioning rises with contingency (the CS reliably predicts the US and the US rarely comes without it), with contiguity (a short CS–US interval, though the best gap depends on the response: under half a second for eyeblinks, minutes for fear, hours for taste aversion), with stimulus intensity, with a more natural fit between the stimuli, and with a longer gap between trials (about 20 seconds or more is better than short gaps) (pp. 70–78).
- Prior experience changes conditioning: overshadowing (a strong stimulus in a compound hogs the learning), latent inhibition (preexposure to the CS alone slows later conditioning, even 28 days afterward), blocking (a known CS prevents a new stimulus in the compound from becoming a CS), and sensory preconditioning (stimuli first paired together can later transfer a CR). Learning is fastest on the earliest pairings, giving a decelerating curve (pp. 73–77).
- Age, temperament and stress modify conditioning. Once formed, a CR can be very durable (pp. 78–79).
- Extinction (repeating the CS alone) weakens a CR but is itself new learning of "no US", not forgetting. Spontaneous recovery after a rest and faster relearning show the original learning persists, and stress before conditioning can hamper extinction (pp. 80–82).
- Theories: stimulus substitution (the CS stands in for the US, and the CR equals the UR), which fails when the CR differs from or opposes the UR; preparatory response theory (the CR readies the body for the US, as rats freeze to a tone predicting shock); and compensatory response theory (the CR offsets the US, e.g. drug cues cause the body to blunt the drug, so drinking in a new place produced stronger intoxication and unusual settings accompanied near-fatal heroin doses) (pp. 83–88).
- Awareness of the CS–US link is not required for conditioning (word-association shock study, worms, single cells); awareness is better seen as something learned (pp. 88–89).
- The Rescorla-Wagner model says each pairing adds learning equal to a constant times the remaining distance to a ceiling, producing a decelerating curve; blocking arises because the first CS has already used up most learning available. It fails to explain latent inhibition. Attention-based theories (Mackintosh, Pearce-Hall) were offered in response, and the book stresses that theories earn their keep by generating research (pp. 89–93).

**Key terms**: unconditional/conditional reflex, US, UR, CS, CR, neutral stimulus, trial, higher-order conditioning, second-order conditioning, test (probe) trial, pseudoconditioning, trace/delay/simultaneous/backward conditioning, contingency, contiguity, interstimulus interval, intertrial interval, compound stimulus, overshadowing, latent inhibition, blocking, sensory preconditioning, extinction, spontaneous recovery, stimulus substitution theory, preparatory response theory, compensatory response theory, Rescorla-Wagner model, taste aversion.

**Notable studies / people**
- Pavlov (1927): conditional salivation to metronome and other stimuli (pp. 58–62).
- Frolov (Pavlov's lab): higher-order conditioning with a black square and metronome (p. 63).
- Staats & Staats (1957): evaluative higher-order conditioning of nonsense syllables (p. 64).
- Rescorla (1968): conditioning declines as the US increasingly occurs without the CS (p. 70).
- Kimble (1947): longer CS–US gaps up to 0.4 s gave more eyeblink CRs (p. 72).
- Kamin (1969): blocking (p. 76).
- Brogden (1939b): sensory preconditioning (pp. 76–77).
- Lightfoot (1980) and Siegel (1972, 1984): drug-cue compensatory responses and unusual-setting overdoses (pp. 87–88).
- Diven (1937): conditioning without awareness (p. 88).
- Rescorla & Wagner (1972): associative-strength model (pp. 89–92).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Spacing trials: the book reports longer gaps between pairings produce better conditioning than short ones, which is an early hint that spaced rather than massed repetitions suit acquisition (p. 78).
- Because early pairings do the most work and later ones add little (decelerating curve), make the first exposures to a new link clear, distinct and well-paired rather than piling on repetition (pp. 77, 90–91).
- Preexposure to a cue alone slows later conditioning (latent inhibition) and an existing cue can block a new one, so avoid priming learners with irrelevant or redundant cues in a context where you will later want them to link cues to outcomes (pp. 75–76).

## Ch 4 — Pavlovian Applications (pp. 97–128)

**Core ideas**
- Emotions such as fear, love, hate and disgust are largely learned through Pavlovian conditioning (conditioned emotional responses); very few stimuli arouse strong emotion innately. Watson and Rayner's infant study showed a fear of a white rat could be created by pairing it with a loud noise, and similar laboratory work has made fear responses to snake pictures. Everyday fears (dentist's drill, doctor's office, math classroom) can arise the same way (pp. 98–100).
- The same principle reverses fear. Jones's gradual pairing of a feared rabbit with a pleasant snack (counterconditioning) is the model for exposure therapy. Variants include systematic desensitization (imagining a graded hierarchy of scenes while relaxed, never reaching intense fear) and virtual reality exposure therapy, which works about as well as real-life exposure for phobias such as heights, flying and spiders and is also used for PTSD (pp. 100–104).
- Prejudice is, in part, a conditioned emotional response. Pairing group names with pleasant or unpleasant words shifts feelings about the groups, so facts alone or mere contact are weak cures against long conditioning histories. Pairing a group with positive words and images can reduce bias, though it takes many trials and works less on deeply conditioned attitudes (pp. 105–108).
- Conditioning is also offered as an account of atypical sexual interests, not as the sole explanation. The book describes treatments based on aversion therapy, the limited success and relapse risk of those treatments, and the need for booster sessions (pp. 108–111).
- Conditioned taste aversion differs from typical conditioning: one pairing suffices and the delay between taste and illness can be hours. This one-trial, long-delay learning has clear survival value and is used on ranches to train livestock to avoid poisonous plants or to graze weeds rather than crops (pp. 111–115).
- Advertising works largely by pairing brands with emotionally positive stimuli (and rivals with negative ones). Experiments show brand liking follows what a product is paired with, more pairings give more liking, and such pairing does not shift strong established loyalties. Putting the brand first (as a true CS) should work better than the simultaneous or backward pairings that many ads use (pp. 116–119).
- Drug addiction phenomena can be read as conditioning: cues that accompany drug use trigger body responses that offset the drug (tolerance), produce craving and withdrawal when the drug is absent, and prompt relapse when a person returns to old settings. Rats given heroin in a new setting died more often than in the familiar one (pp. 119–122).
- Treatments from this analysis include aversion therapy and extinction of drug cues, ideally in settings resembling the real ones (virtual environments), plus ideas such as a tone signaling "no craving." Conditioning is only one part of treating addiction (pp. 122–123).
- In medicine, conditioning may help diagnose conditions (eyeblink conditioning in infant hearing and dementia; faster conditioning in autism and OCD) and explain immune effects such as conditioned allergic reactions and conditioned immune suppression in chemotherapy patients returning to the hospital (pp. 124–126).

**Key terms**: conditioned emotional response, phobia, counterconditioning, exposure therapy, in vivo exposure, systematic desensitization, virtual reality exposure therapy (VRET), evaluative conditioning, paraphilia, aversion therapy, conditioned taste aversion (conditioned food avoidance), co-branding, drug tolerance, withdrawal, relapse, conditioned immunosuppression.

**Notable studies / people**
- Watson & Rayner (1920): Little Albert's conditioned fear of a rat (pp. 98–99).
- Jones (1924): Peter and the rabbit, counterconditioning (p. 100).
- Wolpe (1973): systematic desensitization (p. 101).
- Rothbaum et al. (1995, 2000): VR treatment of fear of heights and flying (p. 102).
- Hoffman: SpiderWorld VR treatment (pp. 103–104).
- Staats & Staats (1958): ethnic labels acquiring pleasant/unpleasant value (p. 106).
- Parish et al. (1976), Olson & Fazio (2006): conditioning used to shift attitudes (pp. 107–108).
- Garcia et al. (1955): saccharin plus radiation produced taste aversion (p. 112).
- Pfister et al. (2002): horses trained to avoid locoweed (p. 113).
- Gorn (1982), Stuart, Shimp & Engle (1987), Gibson (2008): advertising conditioning (pp. 116–118).
- Siegel et al. (1982), MacRae & Siegel (1997): setting and withdrawal in morphine/heroin rats (pp. 120–121).
- Bovbjerg et al. (1990): conditioned immunosuppression (pp. 125–126).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Learners' feelings toward a subject (math anxiety, dread of a classroom) can be Pavlovian; to repair them, pair the subject with pleasant experience in small graded steps, as in desensitization, rather than relying on information alone (pp. 100–101, 107).
- Because the same conditioning that builds bias works through repeated pairings, check what emotional tone is being paired with new material (tests, praise, boredom) when you design lessons (pp. 105–107).
- Review or practice in the settings where recall will be needed, or vary settings deliberately, since cues in the learning environment come to control responding (pp. 120–122).

## Ch 5 — Operant Learning: Reinforcement (pp. 129–171)

**Core ideas**
- Thorndike tested animals in repeated problem situations (puzzle boxes, mazes) instead of trusting charming anecdotes. Escape times fell gradually over trials, which he explained with the law of effect: behavior followed by a satisfying state of affairs becomes more likely in that situation, and behavior followed by an annoying one becomes less likely. Put briefly, behavior is a function of its consequences (pp. 130–134).
- Skinner's operant (instrumental) learning studies, using the lever-and-food chamber, track behavior's rate. Reinforcement means a consequence that strengthens the behavior it follows: the behavior must have a consequence, must increase, and the increase must come from that consequence. Whether an event reinforces is known only from its effect on behavior, so even normally unpleasant events sometimes reinforce (pp. 134–137, 142).
- Positive reinforcement adds a stimulus and negative reinforcement removes or reduces one; both raise behavior. The distinction blurs in practice (a cat's escape from the box plus food, a cold room warming), and the same event can act either way (pp. 137–139).
- "Strength" is more than frequency: reinforced behavior also persists after reinforcement stops, resists punishment and added effort ("behavioral momentum"), and any measurable feature of behavior—even randomness—can be reinforced if the reinforcer depends on it (p. 139).
- Reinforcers are primary (unlearned, e.g. food, water, social contact, control over the environment, sensory stimulation) or secondary/conditioned (learned, e.g. praise, clicks, money). Secondary reinforcers satiate more slowly, can be delivered immediately, interrupt behavior less, and generalized ones such as money work across situations, but they need backing by other reinforcers. Reinforcers may also be natural (automatic results of the act) or contrived (arranged to change behavior), and the two often co-occur (pp. 141–146).
- Operant and Pavlovian learning differ chiefly in whether the important event depends on behavior (operant) or on another event (Pavlovian); they commonly occur together (as in Little Albert's reaching, or dog trainers' cues) (p. 146).
- Operant learning depends on contingency (the consequence reliably following the specific behavior, and being tied to the exact feature you want), contiguity (shorter delays work better; signaling a delay, e.g. with a tone, softens its cost), reinforcer size and quality (larger helps and can offset delay; preferred reinforcers work better), behavior difficulty and species fit, motivating operations (deprivation and similar establishing operations raise a reinforcer's value; abolishing operations lower it), prior learning history, and competing contingencies (which help explain procrastination) (pp. 147–155).
- Reinforcers on the brain side involve a dopamine reward pathway; surprising reinforcers produce more dopamine than expected ones, matching the decelerating learning curve. The book cautions that brain mechanisms do not replace analysis of environmental events (pp. 156–158).
- Thorndike's blindfolded-line studies showed that practice alone produced no improvement; knowing the result after each attempt brought rapid gains, so practice matters because it provides chances for reinforcement (p. 159).
- Theories of why reinforcers work: Hull's drive reduction (fails for many reinforcers), Premack's principle (a more probable behavior reinforces a less probable one; this can be reversed when the usual preference is flipped by restriction) and response-deprivation theory (access to a behavior held below its normal baseline becomes reinforcing). All struggle to explain why words like "yes" reinforce (pp. 159–164).
- Avoidance is explained by two-process theory (Pavlovian fear of a warning signal plus operant escape from it) or one-process theory (reduction in shock exposure itself reinforces). Evidence against two-process includes Sidman's no-signal procedure and Herrnstein–Hineline's work, and avoidance is cured by blocking the avoidance response so the learner discovers nothing bad happens (pp. 164–169).

**Key terms**: law of effect, operant (instrumental) learning, reinforcement, positive/negative reinforcement, escape and avoidance learning, behavioral momentum, primary/secondary (conditioned) reinforcers, generalized reinforcer, natural/contrived reinforcers, satiation, contingency, contiguity, motivating operation, establishing/abolishing operations, reward pathway, dopamine, drive-reduction theory, relative value theory, Premack principle, response-deprivation theory, two-process theory, one-process theory, Sidman avoidance procedure, conditioned suppression.

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1898): puzzle boxes, law of effect (pp. 130–133); line-drawing without feedback (p. 159).
- Skinner (1938): lever-press chamber and rate of responding (pp. 135–136).
- Nevin: behavioral momentum (p. 139); Page & Neuringer (1985): reinforced randomness in pigeons (p. 139).
- Rovee-Collier: infants kicking to move a mobile, showing control as a reinforcer (pp. 142–143).
- Zimmerman (1957): a buzzer paired with water became a conditioned reinforcer (p. 143).
- Hammond (1980): zero contingency stops lever pressing (p. 147).
- Schlinger & Blakely (1994): signaled versus unsignaled delay (p. 150).
- Doughty et al. (2012): larger reinforcer offsets delay (p. 151).
- Olds & Milner (1954): brain stimulation as reinforcer (p. 156).
- Premack (1959, 1962): relative value, with children's candy and pinball (p. 162).
- Solomon & Wynne (1953), Miller (1948), Sidman (1953), Herrnstein & Hineline (1966): avoidance (pp. 165–168).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Give feedback immediately after the response and make it contingent on the specific skill you want trained; delayed feedback invites reinforcement of whatever the learner did in the gap, and a signal bridging a delay helps (pp. 149–150).
- Practice without knowledge of results does not produce improvement, so pair every practice attempt with informative feedback (Thorndike's line-drawing results) (p. 159).
- Check what you are actually reinforcing: if points go for activity rather than the target performance, the activity is what grows (basketball example) (pp. 148–149). Use the Premack principle to schedule a preferred activity after a less preferred study task (p. 162).

## Ch 6 — Reinforcement: Beyond Habit (pp. 172–196)

**Core ideas**
- Shaping builds new behavior by reinforcing successive approximations of a target response when that target does not occur on its own (rat approaching and pressing a lever, pigeon turning, infant moving an arm to switch a light). It also occurs unplanned: parents can unintentionally shape tantrums by giving in only to ever more extreme protests (pp. 173–175).
- Animals appear to shape young naturally: meerkat adults give pups live prey that gradually becomes more challenging, and pups practicing with live scorpions later lost far fewer than pups that had practiced with eggs. Shaping parallels natural selection: variation plus selection yields new forms from old (p. 176).
- Good shapers reinforce small steps, reinforce immediately, use small reinforcers (large ones distract from the task), reinforce the best approximation available rather than slavishly following a plan, and back up to an earlier step when behavior slips. Failure is usually a skill problem of the trainer, not the learner's intelligence (pp. 176–177).
- A behavior chain is a sequence of linked acts. Task analysis breaks a task into links; forward chaining trains the first link and adds the next, backward chaining trains the last link first and adds earlier links (while performance always runs in normal order); missing links are shaped first. Each link is reinforced by the chance to perform the next one, and the final reinforcer sustains the whole chain (pp. 178–180).
- A problem is a situation where reinforcement is available but the needed behavior is not. Kohler's chimps seemed to solve problems by sudden insight, but follow-up studies found gradual learning (Peckstein & Brown), Harlow's monkeys and children slowly improved at solving new problems, and Epstein's pigeons solved a Kohler-style box-and-banana problem suddenly only if each component skill had been reinforced earlier. The apparent suddenness, like soup boiling over, hides a long history (pp. 181–185).
- Creativity means behaving in original ways, and original behavior can be reinforced: dolphins learned to produce novel stunts when novelty earned fish, and children's uses for objects and block constructions became more original when originality earned points or praise (pp. 185–188).
- Studies claiming rewards reduce creativity usually promise a reward for merely doing a task, which makes conventional performance the safest way to be paid. When rewards depend on creative output, creativity rises, so creativity behaves like any operant dimension (pp. 188–189).
- Superstitious behavior is maintained by coincidental reinforcement: pigeons given food on a fixed time schedule developed rituals, and children and college students did too when points or marbles came regardless of what they did. Coincidental reinforcement may not explain all human superstition, and some researchers failed to replicate the pigeon rituals (pp. 189–192).
- Learned helplessness follows inescapable shock, not shock itself; dogs stopped trying to escape even when escape became easy. Prior experience of successful escape prevents it, and Eisenberger showed that reinforcing effort despite difficulty builds learned industriousness (pp. 193–194).

**Key terms**: shaping, successive approximations, behavior chain, chaining, task analysis, forward chaining, backward chaining, problem, insight, creativity, superstitious behavior, coincidental reinforcement, learned helplessness, learned industriousness.

**Notable studies / people**
- Skinner, Breland & Guttman (1943): shaping a bowling pigeon (p. 175).
- Thornton & McAuliffe (2006): meerkat pups and live prey (pp. 175–176).
- Cheney: a rat trained through a long obstacle chain; Skinner's rat Plyny and the marble (p. 178).
- Kohler (1927): chimpanzee insight with sticks and boxes (pp. 181–183).
- Harlow (1949): "learning to learn" in monkeys and children (p. 182).
- Epstein et al. (1984): pigeon insight from reinforcement history (p. 184).
- Pryor: dolphin creativity (pp. 186–187).
- Glover & Gary (1976), Chambers et al. (1977): reinforcing originality in children (p. 187).
- Eisenberger: rewards for creativity versus for any performance (p. 188).
- Skinner (1948), Wagner & Morris (1987), Ono (1987): superstition (pp. 190–191).
- Seligman & Maier (1967), Volpicelli et al. (1983), Eisenberger: helplessness and industriousness (pp. 193–194).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- To teach complex skills, break them into task-analyzed links, teach small steps, give quick, small and specific reinforcement, and lower the standard if performance slips (pp. 176–180).
- Use backward chaining when the end of a sequence is the most reinforcing part: learners start with the last step and always experience the ending first (p. 179).
- Make early experiences solvable and reward persistence in the face of difficulty, since success at escaping difficulty inoculates against giving up, while uncontrollable failure teaches passivity (pp. 193–194). To encourage originality, reward the novel element itself, not mere task completion (pp. 187–189).

## Ch 7 — Schedules of Reinforcement (pp. 197–235)

**Core ideas**
- A schedule of reinforcement is the rule linking behavior to reinforcement. Schedules change not only acquisition but also the rate and pattern of established behavior, and these changes count as learning (pay per piece versus per hour; learning when to check the oven) (pp. 198–199).
- Continuous reinforcement (every response) produces rapid learning and suits shaping and chain-building but is rare in daily life; intermittent schedules reinforce some responses (pp. 200).
- Fixed ratio (FR) schedules produce steady high rates with a pause after each reinforcement that grows longer as the ratio grows. The pauses are explained by escape from the work or by pursuit of other reinforcers, and the book links them to procrastination; run rate is unaffected by these pause-related variables (pp. 200–202).
- Variable ratio (VR) schedules give high, steady responding with few or short pauses, hence more behavior than a comparable FR. Slot machines and other games of chance, commission sales and predator success follow VR patterns; early wins can hook pigeons on a worse-paying gamble (pp. 202–205).
- Fixed interval (FI) schedules yield a scalloped record, with little responding right after reinforcement and rising responding near the interval's end (checking baking, waiting for a bus, studying for exams, Congress passing bills). Variable interval (VI) yields steady moderate rates (pp. 205–208).
- Extinction (no more reinforcement) produces a gradual decline but also an initial extinction burst, more variable behavior, emotional or aggressive behavior, and resurgence of earlier-reinforced responses. Spontaneous recovery returns after rest. Extinction is slower than acquisition, depends on prior reinforcement history, effort and reinforcer type, and never fully erases prior learning (pp. 209–213).
- Other simple schedules: fixed or variable duration, response-independent fixed or variable time (noncontingent) schedules, and progressive schedules whose requirement rises until a break point. Stretching the ratio gradually thins reinforcement and builds persistence, but stretching too fast or too far causes ratio strain (pp. 213–217).
- Compound schedules combine simple ones: multiple (signaled alternation), mixed (unsignaled), chain (signaled sequence, reinforcement only at end), tandem (unsignaled), cooperative (reinforcement depends on several individuals, which can produce unequal work in group projects) and concurrent (choice) (pp. 217–220).
- The partial reinforcement effect (PRE): behavior reinforced intermittently resists extinction longer than behavior reinforced every time, which is paradoxical for the law of effect. Candidate explanations are the discrimination hypothesis (harder to detect the change), frustration (frustration becomes a cue to keep going), the sequential hypothesis (strings of unreinforced responses become cues), and the response unit hypothesis, which says the effect is an illusion once a response is defined as the number of acts needed to earn reinforcement. Therapists and teachers use the PRE by establishing behavior continuously, then thinning reinforcement (pp. 220–226).
- The matching law says behavior is distributed across alternatives in proportion to the reinforcement each provides; it works for ratio and interval choices, for amounts and delays, and in natural settings, and treats every situation as a choice among all available reinforcers (pp. 227–231).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- To build a new behavior, give consistent (continuous) reinforcement first, then gradually thin it to make the behavior durable once feedback is not available; thin gradually to avoid ratio strain (pp. 200, 216, 221).

- In group assignments, reinforce individual contributions as well as the group product to reduce unequal workloads (p. 219). When removing a reinforcer to reduce a problem behavior, expect an extinction burst, and keep reinforcing an alternative behavior consistently to avoid resurgence (pp. 210–211).

## Ch 8 — Operant Learning: Punishment (pp. 236–260)

**Core ideas**
- Thorndike concluded from his "Right/Wrong" word-choice tests that "wrong" did not weaken repeating an answer, and Skinner found that mild punishment (a lever "slap") suppressed pressing only briefly. The book argues both underestimated punishment because their punishers were weak (pp. 237, 251).
- Punishment is defined by effect, not retribution: a consequence that reduces the strength of the behavior it follows. Positive punishment adds an aversive after behavior; negative punishment (penalty training, including Time Out) removes something the person values. Positive punishment and negative reinforcement are easily confused because both involve aversives; the difference is whether the aversive is added (behavior falls) or removed (behavior rises) (pp. 238–240).
- Time Out only works if the setting really lacks reinforcement; chatting or giving toys during it defeats it (pp. 238–239).
- Variables affecting punishment parallel those affecting reinforcement. Contingency: shocks tied to a response suppressed it far more than the same shocks given regardless, and the more consistently punishment follows the behavior, the more it works. Contiguity: delayed punishers lose power quickly (reprimands to hyperactive children worked only if immediate). Intensity: stronger punishers produce larger reductions (pp. 241–245).
- Starting with a weak punisher and escalating teaches tolerance, so an effective level should be used from the start, though the right level may not be knowable in advance (the Israeli day care fine that increased lateness) (p. 246).
- Punishment works better when the punished behavior yields little reinforcement, when an alternative source of the same reinforcer is available, and when the learner is less deprived of the reinforcer. Frequent, low-intensity payoffs often outweigh rare, severe penalties, which helps explain risky behaviors like texting while driving (pp. 244, 247–249).
- Theories: the disruption account (the punisher merely evokes competing behavior) fails because contingent shocks suppress more than noncontingent ones. The two-process theory (Pavlovian fear of the cue plus escape) has trouble with data like Mansfield & Rachlin's, and the one-process theory (punishment weakens behavior as reinforcement strengthens it, symmetrically; low-probability behavior can punish a high-probability one) is favored (pp. 249–251).
- Punishment works quickly and can bring useful side effects, but problems include escape (and lying or cheating), aggression (including displaced aggression), general behavior suppression and apathy, potential for abuse, and imitation of the punisher by those punished (pp. 252–255).
- Alternatives: response prevention (alter the environment), extinction (find and withhold the maintaining reinforcer; watch for bursts and aggression), and differential reinforcement—of alternative behavior (DRA), of incompatible behavior (DRI) and of low rate (DRL, reinforcing only slow performance). They work best when the unwanted behavior's reinforcement is also limited (pp. 255–258).

**Key terms**: punishment, positive punishment, negative punishment, penalty training, Time Out, suppression ratio, contingency, contiguity, punisher intensity, introductory level, alternative reinforcement, motivating operations, disruption theory, two-process theory, one-process theory, escape, displaced aggression, response prevention, extinction, differential reinforcement, DRA, DRI, DRL.

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1932): "Right" strengthened and "Wrong" did not weaken answers (p. 237).
- Skinner (1938): temporary suppression by lever "slap" (p. 237).
- Boe & Church (1967): contingent versus noncontingent shocks (p. 241).
- Azrin, Holz & Hake (1963): probability of punishment; deprivation level (pp. 241, 248).
- Camp et al. (1967): delay and intensity of punishment (pp. 243, 245).
- Abramowitz & O'Leary (1990): immediate versus delayed reprimands for off-task behavior (p. 244).
- Masserman (1946): gradual escalation fostered tolerance (p. 246).
- Herman & Azrin (1964): alternative reinforcement makes punishment effective (p. 247).
- Mansfield & Rachlin (1970): a test against the two-process theory (p. 251).
- Warden & Aylesworth (1927): punishment fostering general avoidance (p. 254).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- The book's evidence favors reinforcing desired behaviors, not punishing errors; design learning environments so correct performance is noticed and rewarded rather than only catching mistakes (pp. 237, 258).
- If a response must be discouraged, deliver feedback immediately and consistently, and make sure the learner has an alternative way to earn the same reinforcer (such as attention) for a better behavior (pp. 243–244, 247).
- Frequent ridicule for "stupid" questions leads students to stop participating, so avoid public embarrassment; use response prevention and DRA/DRI (praise for staying seated, quiet hand-raising) to manage classroom behavior (pp. 254, 256–257).

## Ch 9 — Operant Applications (pp. 261–286)

**Core ideas**
- Neglect is described as an unresponsive environment: where children's behavior has little effect on caretakers, operant learning is limited and development suffers. Language is shaped by parents who reinforce babbling, then successively closer approximations of words and sentences. Hart and Risley found that the amount of parental language instruction and practice, not income, tracked children's later verbal skill (pp. 262–263).
- Self-control and persistence are learned through experience (reinforcing waiting for larger rewards, reinforcing effort despite difficulty), not simply character or age. At home, reinforcing desired behavior, supplemented by differential reinforcement and properly used Time Out, usually reduces problem behavior and parents function as teachers (p. 263–264).
- In classrooms, shifting teachers from reprimanding misbehavior to praising appropriate behavior (while ignoring minor misbehavior) reduced disruption in a second-grade class and raised on-task behavior in disruptive teenagers. DRL reduced excessive attention-seeking (pp. 264–267).
- Hopkins and Conard's changes (moving around the room, de-emphasizing reprimands, immediate feedback, praising on-task behavior) sharply cut discipline problems and sped learning, in some subjects to double the usual rate. Morningside Academy attributes students' two-year gains in a year partly to positive feedback and praise (pp. 267–269).
- Skinner's teaching machines presented small frames with questions, with advance to the next frame as the reinforcer, and mirrored what a good tutor does. Headsprout's online reading program has short lessons with frequent responses, immediate feedback, mastery criteria (five consecutive correct answers) and iterative revision until 90% of children succeed 90% of the time, which yields steady progress and low frustration (p. 269–270).
- In clinics, harmless punishment ended severe self-injury in the 1960s work of Lovaas and others, but reinforcement alternatives (such as DRI, reinforcing play with a ball) now succeed so well that aversives are rarely needed. Self-injury and even delusions can be maintained by attention or escape, and changing those contingencies can change them (pp. 271–275).
- Atypical behavior may need to occur even when reinforcers seem unavailable (Goldiamond's paradox) because discriminating observers stop reinforcing behavior that is obviously "for effect" (p. 276).
- Constraint-induced movement therapy (restraining the good limb, repeated task practice, shaping, detailed feedback) recovered use of limbs after stroke by overcoming "learned nonuse" (pp. 276–278).
- At work, performance feedback alone helps somewhat; feedback combined with incentives or praise helps more, and safety behavior can be reinforced with feedback and time off. In zoos, shaping with a clicker enabled veterinary care of an aggressive elephant, and making animals work for part of their food reduces stereotypic behavior (pp. 279–283).

**Key terms**: neglect, shaping of language, self-control, Time Out, DRL, teaching machine, Headsprout, self-injurious behavior, Goldiamond's paradox, symptom substitution, constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT), learned nonuse, performance feedback, conditioned reinforcer (clicker), environmental enrichment.

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Praise and recognize correct and appropriate behavior frequently, and make your praise-to-criticism ratio visible to yourself; teachers in the studies defaulted to reprimand, and shifting this raised both behavior and learning (pp. 265–267).

## Ch 10 — Observational Learning (pp. 287–323)

**Core ideas**
- Research on observational learning has swung like a roller coaster: Thorndike (and Watson) found no learning from watching in cats, chicks, dogs and monkeys, which depressed the field for decades until Warden's controlled studies and Bandura's work revived it (pp. 288–289).
- The book defines observational learning broadly as learning from observing events and their consequences. Social (active-model) learning involves a model whose behavior and consequences are watched, and can produce vicarious reinforcement or punishment. Asocial learning involves observing events without any model, tested with "ghost" conditions where a mechanism moves things unseen (pp. 289–290, 294–296).
- Evidence of social learning includes Warden's monkeys solving problems after watching a model, Herbert and Harsh's cats (more observation gave faster solving, observers outperformed models), chimps and children copying a door-sliding model, toddlers solving the sliding-mat puzzle, children's picture preferences following a model's approval or disapproval, and adults' word choices following another's reinforcement (pp. 290–294).
- In asocial conditions, chimpanzees and toddlers solved problems as well as or better than after watching a model on easy tasks, raising the question of how much "social" learning is truly social. The book regards apparent "vicarious" Pavlovian conditioning as probably ordinary conditioning (pp. 295–297).
- Imitation means copying a model's exact actions; emulation means achieving the model's goal by one's own route. Humans over-imitate, copying irrelevant steps even when told not to, and over-imitate more with age, unlike other apes. Explanations include evolved benefits for spreading practices and learned generalized imitation (reinforcing imitation of some acts increases imitation of unreinforced acts) (pp. 297–302).
- Task difficulty matters: models help most with hard tasks, while on easy tasks asocial exposure may be as good. Skilled and unskilled (learning) models can both work, without a clear winner. Prestigious, attractive or expressive models attract attention, and attention may be the mechanism behind model-status effects (pp. 303–306).
- Observer characteristics: species, language skill, prior learning history, age, and developmental status affect how much is gained; people who do not attend (young male chimps playing instead of watching) learn less (pp. 306–308).
- Consequences: the model's rewarded or punished behavior shapes imitation (aggressive play followed praise or criticism of the model), but ultimately the consequences of the observer's own behavior win; people imitate what works for them (pp. 308–309).
- Two theories: Bandura's social cognitive theory (attentional, retentional, motor-reproductive and motivational processes, with expectations of outcomes driving imitation) and the operant model (attending, remembering, performing and imitating are behaviors shaped by experience; modeled events are cues that imitation will pay off). The book notes the operant model's limited intuitive appeal and criticisms of Bandura's unobservable processes (pp. 311–314).
- Applications: much classroom teaching works through observation, and children's attentiveness spreads vicariously when a peer is praised. Observers can learn practical skills as well as direct learners when their attending is reinforced, and children with developmental delays may need explicit teaching of how to learn from observation. Social change and "edutainment" use models, though evidence of effects is thin, and media models can also teach aggression (pp. 315–321).

**Key terms**: observational learning, social and asocial observational learning, model, vicarious reinforcement, vicarious punishment, ghost condition, imitation, emulation, over-imitation, generalized imitation, skilled (expert) model, learning (unskilled) model, Bandura's social cognitive theory, attentional/retentional/motor-reproductive/motivational processes, operant learning model, edutainment.

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)

## Ch 11 — Generalization, Discrimination, and Stimulus Control (pp. 324–356)

**Core ideas**
- Three linked concepts are introduced through a story of Inuit seal-hunting, in which a child's seal imitation was mistaken for a seal by dogs: generalization (learning spreads), discrimination (behavior occurs in some situations but not others) and stimulus control (behavior comes under the influence of cues) (p. 325).
- Generalization can occur across people, across time (response maintenance), across behaviors (response generalization), and across situations (stimulus generalization, also called transfer, the focus of the chapter). Plotted, responding falls off with decreasing similarity to the training stimulus, giving a generalization gradient (as in Hovland's tone study and Guttman and Kalish's pigeon color-pecking study) (pp. 326–328).
- Generalization follows reinforcement, extinction and punishment alike, and extends to broad tendencies such as persistence. It cannot be assumed: hearing-impaired preschoolers' social skills trained in one setting did not transfer (pp. 328–329).
- Transfer can be improved by training across many settings and examples, varying consequences, reinforcing generalization when it appears, and arranging the natural environment (such as family contingencies) to keep reinforcing the new behavior. Teachers who assume a student who has learned a theorem will use it in the garage, and parents who assume street-crossing training will hold when chasing a ball, are making an error (pp. 329–332).
- Generalization can also be harmful: giving-up spread from unsolvable to solvable problems (Dweck & Repucci), aggression reinforced against a doll spread to children, and the same mechanism links to displaced aggression, tragic bear and parachute errors, and hate crimes against people who resemble offenders (pp. 330–331).
- Discrimination is the inverse of generalization: a steeper gradient means more discrimination. Pavlovian discrimination pairs a CS+ with the US and a CS- with no US; operant discrimination reinforces a behavior in the presence of an SD and not an SΔ. Procedures include simultaneous and successive training, matching to sample and oddity matching (pp. 332–336).
- Errorless discrimination training, which introduces the SΔ faintly and briefly and builds it up, reduces mistakes and the emotional upset that errors cause, in birds and preschoolers. The differential outcomes effect says that using different reinforcers for different correct responses speeds discrimination learning (pp. 336–337).
- Fine discriminations, such as pigeons telling Bach from Hindemith or Monet from Picasso, show what training can do. Practical applications include second-language sound training (L versus R, where feedback mattered) and detection animals such as mine-sniffing rats (pp. 337–339).
- Stimulus control means behavior reliably follows discriminative stimuli (traffic lights, signs, social settings). It grants efficiency and the possibility of managing one's own environment (removing candy) (pp. 340–341).
- Applications: "mental rotation" reaction times mirror a generalization gradient; concepts are learned through generalization within a class and discrimination between classes (chimps learning "larger than"; pigeons learning human figures, trees, and other categories, including on new slides); smoking relapse can be understood as stimulus control by cues associated with smoking, suggesting avoidance or gradual exposure without smoking in each cue situation, and eating can be cued by an empty bowl (pp. 341–349).
- Theories: Pavlov (excitation and inhibition irradiating across brain areas, circular), Spence (net of excitatory and inhibitory gradients, which predicts peak shift, confirmed by Hanson), and Lashley–Wade (gradient steepness reflects prior experience with the stimulus dimension, supported by Jenkins & Harrison) (pp. 349–354).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Do not expect transfer for free: train in varied settings and with many different examples, and include practice in the real contexts where the skill will be used (pp. 329–330).

## Ch 12 — Forgetting (pp. 357–390)

**Core ideas**
- Storage metaphors for memory (wax tablet, slate, filing cabinet, computer) are questioned because recollections change after the event and because those changes arise from environmental events. The chapter defines forgetting behaviorally as deterioration in performance of learned behavior after a retention interval, though some researchers argue it is really behavior changing with environmental change, which would make forgetting a kind of learning (pp. 358–361).
- Forgetting is not all-or-none and excludes deterioration from aging, injury or disease. Skinner's pigeons responded after four years almost as if no time had passed but extinguished faster, showing subtle forgetting (pp. 359, 361).
- Seven ways to measure forgetting: free recall, prompted (cued) recall, relearning (savings, Ebbinghaus), recognition, delayed matching to sample, extinction, and gradient degradation (a flattened generalization gradient). Free recall is a crude measure because partial remnants of learning go unnoticed (pp. 363–365).
- Forgetting grows with the retention interval, but time itself does not cause it; events during the interval do. Better original learning slows forgetting: Ebbinghaus's higher repetition counts, Krueger's overlearning (50% and 100% extra trials reduced forgetting), fluency (higher correct-per-minute rates produce less forgetting), and Bahrick's finding that thoroughness of Spanish study predicted memory 50 years later (pp. 366–369).
- Prior learning helps when it makes material meaningful (Steinbeck sentences versus scrambled; chess masters recall real positions far better than random ones, but not random layouts) and hurts through proactive interference (earlier lists, Bartlett's reshaping of "The War of the Ghosts," attitudes interfering with recall of opposing passages) (pp. 370–373).
- Subsequent learning produces retroactive interference. Less forgetting follows sleep or inactivity than waking activity, and the better the intervening list is learned, the more it interferes (pp. 374–375).
- Context matters: recall is better when conditions match those at learning (standing versus sitting, underwater versus on land, odors, even infants' mobile-kicking tied to bumper, mobile and scent). Internal state also acts as context (state-dependent learning, drug and alcohol examples, Kamin's rats' 24-hour rebound) (pp. 375–379).
- Eyewitness memory is malleable. Loftus showed question wording ("the" vs "a" headlight; "smashed" vs "hit") changed reports, including reports of broken glass that was never shown, and many wrongful convictions resting on eyewitness testimony were later overturned by DNA (pp. 380–382).
- Seven strategies to reduce forgetting: overlearn; practice with feedback (perform the behavior and get feedback, not just reread); distributed rather than massed practice (Bahrick & Phelps: monthly sessions beat daily beat same-day, tested eight years later); self-testing (Roediger & Karpicke: testing beat repeated study); mnemonics (rhymes, acronyms); use context cues (study in conditions like the test, but also in varied settings); and take a problem-solving approach to retrieval by generating cues (pp. 382–386).
- SAFMEDS: say the answer before flipping, go through all cards fast in one minute each day, then shuffle; keep cards short, use both directions, and count correct per minute as a fluency measure (p. 387).
- Evolution built memory to "keep what you need," not to be a hard drive, and learning changes a person even when specific content is forgotten (pp. 388–389).

**Key terms**: forgetting, retention interval, declarative/nondeclarative memory, semantic and episodic memory, procedural memory, free recall, prompted (cued) recall, relearning (savings) method, recognition, delayed matching to sample, extinction method, gradient degradation, overlearning, fluency, proactive interference, retroactive interference, paired associate learning, context, cue-dependent forgetting, state-dependent learning, massed practice, distributed (spaced) practice, mnemonic, SAFMEDS.

- de Groot (1966), Chase & Simon (1973): chess expertise and meaningful patterns (p. 371).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference, with the chapter's own advice marked)
- The book gives seven explicit study tactics: overlearn past one error-free pass (aim for a fluency target like 40 cards a minute), practice by actually producing the answer and getting feedback, space sessions over time, self-test rather than reread, use mnemonics, align study context with the test context while also varying study contexts for long-term use, and generate cues when stuck (pp. 382–386).
- Teachers can build in spaced review across the semester and low-stakes retrieval tests, since students favor massed study and cramming but long-term results favor spacing and testing (pp. 383–384; the text itself notes its quizzes and review questions offer distributed practice, p. 383).

## Ch 13 — The Limits of Learning (pp. 391–411)

(Chapter content runs to p. 406; pp. 408–411 are the concept-check answer key.)

**Core ideas**
- Physical characteristics set limits on what can be learned: fish cannot jump rope, a dog's smell or a hawk's vision gives it learning advantages, and chimpanzees' failure to learn to speak seems to reflect vocal anatomy as much as learning ability, since they acquired many signs (Washoe) (pp. 392–393).
- Learned behavior is not inherited. McDougall's claim that rats' offspring learn shock avoidance faster across generations was not replicated under better controls. The book notes this is a severe limit (every generation starts over) but also a safeguard, since inherited learning could trap species in obsolete behavior (pp. 393–394).
- Heredity affects learning ability across and within species: wolves outperformed dogs at a barrier problem (domestication may have relaxed selection for problem solving), dogs but not wolves use human pointing cues, and Tryon's selective breeding for maze-bright and maze-dull rats produced diverging strains. Heredity does not act alone: learning history powerfully affects learning ability (examples include Hart & Risley's language environments) (pp. 395–397).
- Anecdotes of enriched early environments (John Stuart Mill, Edith Stern) leave open, but do not prove, that a rich early intellectual environment can improve learning ability (pp. 397–398).
- Neurological damage limits learning: prenatal exposure to alcohol and drugs, neurotoxins such as lead, traumatic brain injury and malnutrition during development can reduce learning ability, even though the book maintains that the environment, not the brain, is the source of learning (pp. 398–399).
- Critical periods are stages in which particular learning is especially likely: imprinting in goslings and other animals, dogs' socialization with people between roughly 3 and 12 weeks, and the Harlows' isolated monkeys' lasting social deficits. Evidence for critical periods in people (language in the first years, caring about others) is weaker (pp. 399–401).
- Preparedness varies learning ease by situation: the Brelands' raccoon and pig showed instinctive drift, reverting to innate food-handling actions that disrupted reinforced tasks; Garcia and Koelling's rats readily linked taste with illness and sights/sounds with shock but not the reverse; pigeons autoshaped to peck lit discs; and primates and people more readily acquire fear of snakes and spiders. Seligman's continuum runs from prepared to unprepared to contraprepared, though the human case is debated (pp. 401–405).
- The closing "Learning and Humanity" box stresses that learning has no built-in aim and can produce bad outcomes as well as good (p. 405). The final word argues that society's biggest problems are largely behavioral and so can be approached through learning principles (p. 406).

**Use for teaching and studying** (my inference)
- Individual differences in learning speed reflect both heredity and learning history, and the book gives no support for fixed ability: early rich, interactive environments and experience matter, so keep expectations modifiable and support early learning (pp. 396–398).
- Learned behavior is not inherited, so each learner must be taught from the beginning; do not assume previous cohorts' mastery carries over (pp. 393–394).
- Check physical and health conditions (sensory limits, nutrition, neurotoxin exposure) when a learner cannot seem to progress, and design tasks that work with, not against, innate response tendencies (pp. 392, 398–399, 402).

## Cross-cutting takeaways for teaching and learning

1. Define learning, and plan objectives, in terms of measurable behavior change (what a learner can now do, how fast, how accurately, how fluently), and track progress with simple records such as errors, latency, rate, and cumulative graphs (pp. 20–23, 36–41).
2. Immediate, contingent consequences teach best: reinforcement works faster the sooner and more reliably it follows the specific behavior, and feedback after practice, not practice alone, produces improvement (Thorndike's blindfolded lines) (pp. 133, 147–150, 159).
3. Build new or complex skills by shaping and chaining: reinforce small steps and the best approximation available, back up when learners slip, task-analyze sequences, and consider backward chaining (pp. 173–180).
4. Reinforce what you want rather than punishing what you do not: punishment has side effects (escape, aggression, apathy, abuse, imitation) and works best only with strong consistency and an available alternative, whereas differential reinforcement and response prevention avoid those problems (pp. 247, 252–258).
5. Classroom evidence summarized in the book shows that increasing praise for appropriate behavior and attentiveness, giving immediate feedback and de-emphasizing reprimands improves both behavior and academic learning, as in Hopkins and Conard's classrooms, Morningside Academy and Headsprout (pp. 264–270).
6. Design instruction for high success: frame-by-frame lessons with frequent responses, mastery criteria and repeated revision until most learners succeed most of the time reduce frustration and sustain progress (pp. 269–270). Errorless (graded) discrimination procedures and differential outcomes also cut errors and emotional upset (pp. 336–337).
7. Thin reinforcement gradually once a skill is established, because intermittent reinforcement makes behavior persistent, but stretching too fast produces ratio strain; expect pauses and procrastination before big requirements and cramming on deadline-driven schedules (pp. 200–202, 207, 216, 221).
8. To retain material, use the book's seven tactics: overlearn (to a fluency target), practice with feedback, space sessions, self-test, use mnemonics, match study conditions to test conditions while varying study contexts for broad use, and generate cues when recall stalls (pp. 382–386). Retrieval and spacing beat rereading and massed study in the evidence cited (pp. 383–384).
9. Fluency (correct responses per minute) is a better index of mastery and a better predictor of retention than a single accurate pass; SAFMEDS-style flash-card practice puts this into practice (pp. 41, 368–369, 387).
10. Do not assume transfer: generalization must be programmed through varied examples, settings and consequences, practice in real contexts, and arranging natural reinforcement for the new behavior; concepts are built by combining generalization within a class and discrimination from other classes (pp. 329–332, 343–346).
11. Observation is a powerful teaching channel, especially for hard tasks: demonstrate, reinforce attentiveness (it spreads vicariously), and note that observers eventually do what pays off for them regardless of the model's outcome (pp. 303, 309, 315–317).
12. Persistence and helplessness are learned: reinforce effort on difficult tasks and let learners experience escapable, solvable problems so they avoid learned helplessness and build learned industriousness (pp. 193–194, 263).
13. Emotions toward subjects, teachers and groups are conditioned by what gets paired with them; deliberately pair learning with positive experiences and use graded exposure to reduce anxiety, rather than relying on information alone (pp. 98–101, 105–108).
14. Creativity and insight respond to history: reinforce novel or original performances (not mere task completion), and expect "sudden" solutions to depend on earlier reinforced component skills (pp. 184–189).
15. Evaluate teaching claims by comparison data (control groups or reversal designs), not anecdotes or testimonials (pp. 42–47).
