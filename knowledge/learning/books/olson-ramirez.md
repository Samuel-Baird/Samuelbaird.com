# Olson & Ramirez, *An Introduction to Theories of Learning* — chapter digest

**Source:** Olson & Ramirez, *An Introduction to Theories of Learning*, 10th ed. (Routledge, 2020). Page cites are printed page numbers.

**Orientation:** A survey textbook that places each major learning theorist (Thorndike, Skinner, Hull, Pavlov, Guthrie, Estes, Gestalt psychology, Piaget, Tolman, Bandura, Hebb, Bolles) within five loose paradigms (functionalistic, associationistic, cognitive, neurophysiological, evolutionary), closing each chapter with the theory's educational implications, evaluation, and criticisms, and arguing that every approach illuminates part of learning.

*This is a paraphrased study digest, not a substitute for the book. "Own inference" tags mark advice that goes beyond what the book itself says.*


## Ch 1 — What Is Learning? (pp. 3–14)

**Core ideas**
- The chapter starts from Kimble's well-known definition: a fairly lasting change in behavioral potentiality that follows reinforced practice. Its parts are: the change must be expressible in measurable behavior (overt or covert), must be relatively durable, may appear later than the experience, and must come from practice (p. 3).
- Reinforcer and reward are deliberately not treated as synonyms. In Pavlov's work any unconditioned stimulus counts as a reinforcer even if unpleasant; Skinnerians hold that reinforcers strengthen behavior that immediately precedes them, whereas rewards are social payoffs for worthy acts that come too late to strengthen anything (pp. 3–4).
- Most theorists treat learning as an intervening variable: an inferred process sitting between experiences (independent variables) and behavior (dependent variable). Skinner is the exception, holding that the behavior change is the learning and nothing further needs inferring (pp. 4–5).
- "Relatively permanent" is meant to separate learning from fatigue, illness, maturation or intoxication, but no exact duration can be set. Brief retention, such as the rapid loss of unrehearsed nonsense syllables within about 18 seconds, still seems to involve some learning, and sensitization and habituation are borderline, crude cases (p. 5).
- Learning (a change in potential to act) must be distinguished from performance (the potential actually showing up in behavior); athletes may learn a strategy from film yet not display it until game time or after an injury ends (p. 6).
- Reflexes and instincts (now usually called species-specific behavior) are unlearned and so do not count as learning. Imprinting is a mixed case: an innate sensitivity during a critical period lets a hatchling learn, apparently in one trial, to follow a moving object (pp. 6–7).
- Theorists disagree on whether reinforcement is necessary at all, so the book adopts a reinforcement-neutral definition: a relatively lasting change in behavior or behavioral potentiality from experience, not due to temporary states like illness, fatigue or drugs (pp. 7–8).
- Conditioning is a procedure; learning is the broader concept. In classical conditioning a neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus comes to elicit a conditioned response and the organism cannot control the reinforcer; in instrumental/operant conditioning reinforcement depends on the organism's own response, including escape and avoidance variants (pp. 8–9). Some theorists (e.g. Gagne with eight hierarchical kinds) think two kinds are not enough (p. 10).
- Learning supplements homeostasis and reflexes by letting organisms discover what satisfies needs and what is dangerous; classical conditioning typically needs several pairings, which protects against accidental one-off associations (pp. 10–11). The book also argues that learning research informs child rearing, therapy, and education, citing programmed learning, computer-assisted instruction and individualized instruction (p. 11).

**Key terms**: learning, behavioral potentiality, reinforced practice, reinforcer vs. reward, intervening variable, temporary body states, short-term memory, sensitization, habituation, orienting reflex, performance, reflex, instinct, species-specific behavior, imprinting, critical period, conditioning, classical conditioning (US, UR, CS, CR), instrumental conditioning, Skinner box, escape and avoidance conditioning, homeostasis

**Notable studies / people**
- Kimble (1961): source of the reinforcement-based definition of learning (p. 3).
- Peterson & Peterson (1959) and Murdock (1961): unrehearsed nonsense syllables are nearly all lost within roughly 18 seconds (p. 5).
- Lorenz: ducklings imprint on moving objects (a box, a person, another species) during a critical period (p. 7).
- Brown (1965): isolated infant squirrels still perform nut-burying actions on a bare floor, showing apparently unlearned behavior (p. 7).
- Gagne (1970): proposes eight hierarchically ordered kinds of learning (p. 10).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Do not judge learning only by what students show right now: because learning is potential and performance is its expression, assessments should be chosen with care and repeated over time, and absence of a display is not proof of absence of learning (p. 6).
- Because "relatively permanent" separates learning from transient states, check retention after a delay (and away from fatigue or stress) rather than treating immediate success as mastery (pp. 5, 8).
- The book itself notes that laboratory learning principles have already shaped classroom practices such as programmed and individualized instruction, which is the motivation for reading the later chapters as design guidance (p. 11).

## Ch 2 — Approaches to the Study of Learning (pp. 15–30)

**Core ideas**
- Because learning can only be inferred from behavior, researchers study it in many ways. Naturalistic observation gives a first classification of real-world learning (verbal, perceptual-motor, problem solving) but is hard to record accurately and lumps distinct processes together; elementism then breaks behavior into smaller parts so that laws can be found by experiment (pp. 15–16).
- Science fuses rationalism (reasoning) and empiricism (experience). A theory therefore has a formal side (its words and symbols) and an empirical side (the events it explains); a theory that sounds coherent but cannot survive tests, like astrology, has no scientific value (pp. 16–17).
- A scientific law is a regularly observed relation between classes of events. Theories go beyond laws by grouping them: the synthesizing function organizes many observations and the heuristic function points to new research. The book's example moves from food, water and sex deprivation to the abstract concepts of hunger, thirst and drive, and then to motivation (pp. 17–18).
- A theory is a tool judged by usefulness, not by being right or wrong. It must organize findings and generate testable hypotheses; confirmed hypotheses strengthen it, failed ones force revision, and of two equally good theories the simpler is preferred (parsimony) (p. 19).
- Turning theory into experiment requires an operational definition (for example, trials to criterion as a measure of learning rate), with independent variables manipulated and dependent variables measured (pp. 19–20).
- Many experimental choices are arbitrary or pragmatic: what aspect of learning to study, a single-subject (idiographic) versus group-average (nomothetic) approach, human versus animal subjects, correlational (R-R laws) versus experimental (S-R laws) methods, which variables and levels to use, and how to analyze and interpret data. The choice of dependent variable can reverse a conclusion: practice with one hand improved speed with the other hand but not error counts, so transfer looked present or absent depending on the measure (pp. 20–23). Reproducibility failures in published psychology make transparency about these choices important (p. 23).
- A model simplifies by showing what something is like (the computer as an analogy for human information processing), while a theory explains underlying processes. Laboratory work gains control but loses naturalness, so combining field observation, laboratory study, and a return to the field is attractive (pp. 23–24).
- Kuhn sees sciences changing by paradigm shifts after persistent anomalies, with "normal science" elaborating a paradigm; Popper says science begins with problems and that a theory is scientific only if it makes risky, refutable predictions, so a theory that fits every conceivable result (Freudian theory, astrology) is weak. The book reconciles them: Kuhn describes what science has been, Popper what it should be (pp. 25–28). Behavioral science has many coexisting paradigms, and associationism is the one shared assumption now being questioned (p. 26).

**Key terms**: naturalistic observation, elementism, rationalism, empiricism, formal vs. empirical aspect, scientific law, theory, hypothesis, synthesizing function, heuristic function, parsimony, operational definition, trials to criterion, independent/dependent variable, idiographic vs. nomothetic, R-R law, S-R law, model, analogy, paradigm, normal science, scientific revolution, refutability (falsification)

**Notable studies / people**
- Kuhn (1973): paradigms, normal science, and revolutions (pp. 25–26).
- Popper (1963): science starts with problems; refutability separates science from non-science (p. 27).
- Hand-to-hand transfer example: speed shows positive transfer, errors show none, so measure choice matters (pp. 22–23).
- Robinson (1986): reconciliation of Kuhn and Popper (p. 28).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- When judging whether a teaching method works, define "learning" operationally and use more than one outcome measure (for instance both speed and accuracy), since a single measure can mislead (pp. 20, 22–23).
- Treat any study technique or instructional theory as a tool to be tested against evidence, not as a belief; prefer claims that make risky, checkable predictions (pp. 19, 27).
- Classroom observation is a good source of hypotheses, but controlled comparison is needed before concluding that a practice caused better learning (pp. 15–16, 24).

## Ch 3 — Early Notions About Learning (pp. 31–53)

**Core ideas**
- Epistemology asks what knowledge is and where it comes from. Plato held that knowledge is innate and reached by turning inward (nativism and rationalism), whereas Aristotle held it comes from sensory experience that reason then examines (empiricism); labels like these name an emphasis, and no thinker is purely one type (pp. 31–32).
- Plato's reminiscence theory says true knowledge is recollection of abstract forms the soul knew before birth, while the senses only supply opinion. Because this view shaped Christian thought for about twelve centuries, it discouraged empirical inquiry (pp. 33, 35–36).
- Aristotle's laws of association (similarity, contrast, contiguity, and later frequency) explain how ideas call each other to mind. The book stresses that essentially every twentieth-century learning theory builds on associationism (p. 34).
- Descartes separated mind from body and described the body, including reflex action, as a machine, opening the way for physiological and animal research. Hobbes and Locke rejected innate ideas (Locke: the mind begins as a blank tablet), and Berkeley and Hume pushed empiricism toward skepticism; Kant answered by proposing innate categories of thought that organize experience, an ancestor of cognitive science (pp. 36–40). Mill added that combined ideas can form a whole unlike its parts (p. 40).
- Faculty psychology and phrenology treated mental faculties like muscles that practice strengthens; this "formal discipline" idea justified hard subjects like Latin and mathematics as mind training, and the book notes some evidence for it (Lehman, Lempert & Nisbett, 1988) while promising to revisit transfer with Thorndike (pp. 41–42).
- Darwin's evolution restored continuity between humans and animals, legitimized animal research, and shifted questions from "what can we know" to "how do organisms adjust to their environment," setting the stage for a science of behavior (pp. 42–43).
- Ebbinghaus brought learning and memory into the lab using material with meaningless relationships. He found that more material takes longer to learn, forgetting is steep in the first hours then slow, overlearning slows forgetting, and meaningful text (Byron's poetry) was learned in about one ninth the exposures of his syllables and retained better (pp. 43–44).
- The early schools: Wundt's voluntarism (active mind, will, apperception), Titchener's structuralism (passive association of elements by introspection, which collapsed partly by ignoring evolution, applied work and the unconscious), James/Dewey/Angell's functionalism (consciousness serves adaptation), and Watson's behaviorism (behavior, not consciousness, as the subject matter, with prediction and control as goals) (pp. 44–49). The book groups modern theories into five loose paradigms: functionalistic, associationistic, cognitive, neurophysiological and evolutionary, and suggests all contain some truth (pp. 49–50).

**Key terms**: epistemology, rationalism, nativism, empiricism, reminiscence theory, laws of association (similarity, contrast, contiguity, frequency), associationism, innate ideas, tabula rasa, primary vs. secondary qualities, naive realism, innate categories of thought, faculty psychology, phrenology, formal discipline, nonsense material, learning curve, retention curve, savings, overlearning, voluntarism, apperception, creative synthesis, structuralism, introspection, stimulus error, functionalism, behaviorism

**Notable studies / people**
- Plato (ca. 427–347 BC) and Aristotle (384–322 BC): the nativist and empiricist poles of epistemology (pp. 32–34).
- Descartes, Hobbes, Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Kant, Mill, Reid, Gall: stages in the debate over innate versus experienced knowledge (pp. 36–41).
- Ebbinghaus (1885): first learning curve and retention curve, savings measure, overlearning and meaningfulness effects (pp. 43–44).
- Wundt (first lab, 1879), Titchener, James (1890), Dewey (1896), Watson (1913): founders of the early schools (pp. 44–48).
- Darwin and Galton: evolution and the study of individual differences (p. 42).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Ebbinghaus's results support planning review soon after first learning, when forgetting is fastest, and continuing practice past first perfect recall to slow loss (p. 44).
- Make material meaningful by tying it to existing knowledge and connections, since meaningful content was learned far faster and retained better than unconnected items (p. 44).
- Be cautious about claims that studying hard subjects "trains the mind"; the book flags formal discipline as historically influential, with mixed evidence, and defers the transfer question to Chapter 4 (p. 42).

## Ch 4 — Edward Lee Thorndike (pp. 57–80)

**Core ideas**
- Thorndike's connectionism treats learning as the forming of neural bonds between situations and responses, unlike older associationism that linked ideas. It combines associationism, Darwinian functionalism, and experimental method (p. 61). His puzzle-box studies (cats escaping to reach food) showed trial-and-error "selecting and connecting": time to escape fell gradually over trials, so he judged learning incremental rather than insightful, and not mediated by reasoning or ideas. He held that the same basic laws apply across mammals, humans included (pp. 61–64).
- Early (pre-1930) laws: readiness (acting when prepared is satisfying, being blocked or forced is annoying); exercise (use strengthens a bond, disuse weakens it, strength meaning higher probability of the response); and effect (a response followed by a satisfying state is strengthened, an annoying one weakened), which broke with the view that frequency or contiguity alone fix association strength (pp. 64–66). How a later reward can strengthen an earlier response was left unresolved; he proposed an unconscious "confirming reaction" (p. 66).
- Secondary concepts: multiple response (try different responses until one works), set or attitude (what the learner brings, including drive state, decides what satisfies), prepotency of elements (only some features of a situation govern the response), response by analogy, and associative shifting (a response is carried across gradually changing stimuli, resting on contiguity rather than effect) (pp. 66–71).
- Identical elements theory of transfer: learning carries over to a new situation only to the extent that the situations share elements, which may be stimuli or procedures. Thorndike and Woodworth's work on thousands of students found little support for formal discipline, since apparent mind-training effects reflected abler students choosing harder subjects; he urged schools to teach skills resembling those needed later and to give "disciplinary" claims only reasonable weight (pp. 68–70).
- After 1930 he abandoned the law of exercise (mere repetition does little and disuse alone weakens little) and halved the law of effect: reward strengthens a connection but punishment does not weaken it. The book flags that this has large implications for education and child rearing (p. 71).
- New post-1930 ideas: belongingness (elements that naturally go together are learned more easily; in his sentence experiment, within-sentence links were recalled far more than cross-sentence links, 21.5 vs 2.75 on average), polarity (responses are easiest in the direction learned), and spread of effect (reinforcing one response also raises the likelihood of neighbors) (pp. 71–73).
- On education: Thorndike criticized lecture and demonstration because they hand over conclusions the student did not work out ("telling is not teaching"). The book adds that modern research favors active, student-centered approaches such as collaborative and problem-based learning over passive reception (pp. 73–74). His seven rules call for defining the situation and the response, forming the needed bond deliberately, avoiding bonds that must later be broken, and practicing in the form later needed, with real-life situations and demands (pp. 74–75).
- The book's profile of a Thorndikian classroom: clear objectives sized to learners, simple-to-complex sequencing, quick reinforcement of correct responses and quick correction of errors, regular examinations as feedback, and positive control instead of punishment. It ties this to the testing effect: frequent testing improves learning and retention from grade school through college, and frequent testing with rapid feedback especially improves test performance (p. 75). Criticisms: the law of effect looked circular (a satisfier is identified only by its effect), and the theory was seen as too mechanical, ignoring awareness and intention (pp. 76–77).

**Key terms**: connectionism, bond, selecting and connecting, trial-and-error learning, puzzle box, incremental vs. insightful learning, law of readiness, law of exercise (use, disuse), law of effect, satisfier/annoyer, confirming reaction, multiple response, set/attitude, prepotency of elements, response by analogy, identical elements, transfer of procedure, formal discipline, associative shifting, belongingness, polarity, spread of effect, Morgan's canon, anthropomorphizing, testing effect, active learning

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1898/1911): puzzle-box cats and monkeys; gradual learning curves (pp. 61–63).
- Romanes and Morgan: anecdotal animal psychology versus Morgan's canon of simplest explanation (pp. 59–60); Washburn (1908), The Animal Mind (p. 60).
- Thorndike & Woodworth (1901) and Thorndike (1924): transfer-of-training studies, including 8,564 high school students (pp. 68–69).
- Thorndike (1932): belongingness sentence experiment (pp. 71–72). Spread-of-effect number-guessing task with "right"/"wrong" feedback (p. 73).
- McDaniel et al. (2011), Foss & Pirozzolo (2017), Kang, McDermott & Roediger (2007): testing effect, cited by the book (p. 75). Michael (2006) and Prince (2004): active learning (p. 74).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's stated implications first, then own inference)
- The book states directly that frequent testing, plus prompt feedback and quick correction of errors, improves retention and performance, so build low-stakes retrieval practice and fast answer-checking into study materials rather than relying on rereading or lecture (pp. 74–75).
- Prefer activities where learners work out answers themselves (collaborative, problem-based) over presenting finished conclusions, since the book endorses active over passive formats (p. 74).
- Design practice to match the real task: transfer depends on shared elements, so practice in the form and context in which knowledge will be used, and vary contexts or practice the procedures that should transfer (pp. 68–70, 75). Correct errors before they are rehearsed, and favor positive feedback over punishment (pp. 71, 75) (own inference).

## Ch 5 — Burrhus Frederic Skinner (pp. 81–126)

**Core ideas**
- Skinner's radical behaviorism rejects mentalistic explanations (drive, purpose, motivation) and relies on a functional analysis of observable environment-behavior relations, aiming at prediction and control. He called the analogy between operant conditioning and natural selection central: behavior is selected by its consequences (pp. 83–86, 112).
- Respondent behavior is elicited by a stimulus (Type S, identical to classical conditioning); operant behavior is emitted and controlled by its consequences (Type R). Strength is read from response rate. A reinforcer is defined only by its effect on behavior, not in advance (pp. 84–85).
- Operant procedures: magazine training, then shaping by differential reinforcement of successive approximations, which is far faster than waiting for the response to occur; extinction returns responding to its baseline (operant level), with spontaneous recovery after a rest; noncontingent reinforcement produces superstitious rituals; a discriminative stimulus signals when a response will pay off (SD-R-SR) (pp. 87–92). Secondary and generalized reinforcers (such as money) extend reinforcement, and chains of responses are linked by secondary reinforcers backward from a final primary reinforcer (pp. 93–95). Teachers can misapply these: calling on the loudest raised hands reinforces loudness (p. 87).
- Negative reinforcement (removing an aversive event, which raises response probability) differs from punishment. Skinner, like Thorndike, held punishment suppresses responding only temporarily: in Estes's rat study, punished animals later caught up in total responses to extinction. He listed costs of punishment, including fear, little information about what to do instead, aggression, and substitution of other bad behavior. A large mother-interview study and later research on corporal punishment point the same way. Alternatives include changing the circumstances, letting time pass, reinforcing incompatible behavior, and above all extinction by ignoring the behavior (pp. 96–100).
- Partial reinforcement produces more resistance to extinction than continuous reinforcement (Humphreys's finding). Schedules (CRF, FI, FR, VI, VR) yield characteristic patterns: FI gives scalloped responding that speeds near the expected payoff, FR gives pauses then bursts, and VR produces the highest and steadiest rates (gambling). Continuous reinforcement gives the lowest rate and weakest persistence (pp. 101–104).
- Later extensions: Herrnstein's matching law (response share tracks reinforcement share) and his hyperbola, in which extraneous reinforcement slows learning of a target behavior (a piano student with an audience improves more slowly); concurrent-chain work on self-control, showing preference for large delayed rewards can be induced when a delay is unavoidable; progressive-ratio and behavioral-economics methods that gauge a reinforcer's worth by the price an animal will pay (pp. 104–111).
- The relativity of reinforcement: Premack held that a more probable activity can reinforce a less probable one (children's pinball/candy study), so reinforcers are individual and changeable; Timberlake's disequilibrium hypothesis says restricting access to any activity below its baseline can make it reinforcing. Both undercut the claim that reinforcers work across all situations (pp. 112–116).
- On education, Skinner held that learning is most effective when material comes in small steps, feedback is rapid, and learners go at their own pace; objectives should be stated behaviorally; early continuous reinforcement should shift to partial; punishment should be avoided; and classroom problems often reflect poor instructional planning (pp. 116–117). The lecture breaks all three principles, so he promoted programmed instruction with small frames, overt responding, immediate feedback, and self-pacing (pp. 118–119). The book cites a review (Schramm, 1964) in which, among 36 studies comparing programmed with traditional instruction, 17 favored programmed instruction, 18 found no difference and 1 favored traditional teaching; thousands of studies finding PSI (the Keller Plan, mastery by segments) as good or better with longer retention; CBI that mirrors these principles; and online courses rated comparable to traditional ones (pp. 119–121). Criticisms: single-subject methods were hard to compare with group studies, and refusing theory gave accumulation of phenomena over deeper explanation (pp. 121–122).

**Key terms**: radical behaviorism, respondent/operant behavior, Type S/Type R, operant level, cumulative recording, magazine training, shaping, differential reinforcement, successive approximation, extinction, spontaneous recovery, contingent vs. noncontingent reinforcement, superstitious behavior, discriminative stimulus (SD), secondary and generalized reinforcers, chaining, positive/negative reinforcement, punishment, partial reinforcement effect, CRF, FI, FR, VI, VR, concurrent schedules, matching law, Herrnstein's hyperbola, concurrent-chain schedule, progressive ratio, break point, demand elasticity, Premack principle, disequilibrium hypothesis, programmed learning, teaching machine, PSI, CBI, MOOCs

**Notable studies / people**
- Estes (1944): punishment suppresses temporarily; extinction alone works as well in the long run (p. 97).
- Humphreys (1939): partial reinforcement resists extinction more (p. 101); Ferster & Skinner (1957): schedules of reinforcement (p. 102).
- Herrnstein (1961, 1970, 1974): matching law and hyperbola (pp. 104–106); Rachlin & Green (1972): pigeons chose large delayed food when commitment came first (p. 107).
- Premack (1959, 1962), Timberlake (1980): response-based reinforcement theories (pp. 112–115).
- Sears, Maccoby & Levin (1957): punitive child rearing worse than rewarding (p. 99). Pressey (1926) and Skinner (1954, 1958): teaching machines (p. 118). Keller (1968): PSI (p. 119).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's stated implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states the three-part recipe directly: small steps, immediate feedback on correctness, learner-controlled pace; build study materials as short items needing an overt answer, with instant checking, and mastery criteria before advancing (pp. 116, 118–119).
- Move from frequent feedback early in learning toward intermittent feedback later, because partial reinforcement produces more persistence once a skill is acquired (pp. 101–104, 117).
- Prefer positive feedback and ignoring errors over punishment, and watch what behavior your feedback is actually reinforcing; reinforcers are personal, so use free-choice observation (Premack) to find them (pp. 87, 97–100, 114) (own inference for the last point).

## Ch 6 — Clark Leonard Hull (pp. 127–164)

**Core ideas**
- Hull built the first rigorous, quantitative theory of learning using a hypothetico-deductive method: unobservable postulates yield testable theorems, and experimental results confirm, revise or sink the premises. His 1952 system held sixteen postulates and 133 theorems (pp. 128–129, 143).
- In his scheme, learning and performance are separate. Habit strength grows with reinforced pairings along a negatively accelerated curve (early trials add most), and reinforcement means drive reduction (later drive-stimulus reduction, because bodily drive reduction is too slow to explain learning). Performance potential multiplies habit strength by drive (and later by incentive and stimulus intensity), so a well-learned response is not shown if any multiplier is zero (pp. 130–137).
- Fatigue-based reactive inhibition and the learned tendency not to respond (conditioned inhibition) subtract from performance potential. Hull used them to explain spontaneous recovery, extinction, and the reminiscence effect (performance improving after a rest), and they also account for the advantage of distributed over massed practice, which a 317-experiment review confirmed as robust even though modern researchers doubt Hull's explanation (pp. 133–134). A random oscillation term makes prediction probabilistic (p. 134).
- The 1952 revisions came from data: Crespi's finding that shifts in reward size change running speed immediately implied that reward size affects performance (incentive K) rather than learning; stimulus intensity matters (V); saccharine can reinforce hungry rats, supporting drive-stimulus reduction (pp. 136–138).
- The fractional antedating goal response (rG) is a small anticipatory piece of the goal response that produces its own stimuli (sG) and drives chaining: stimuli near the goal become secondary reinforcers, and the effect spreads backward through the sequence. Hull saw this as an objective stand-in for expectation and thought (pp. 138–141). The habit family hierarchy says that among alternative routes, the shortest or quickest-reinforced one is preferred, with others as fallbacks; delay of reinforcement weakens a response (pp. 141–142).
- Education (the book extends Hull, who himself stuck to rats): distributed rather than massed practice; moderate anxiety that success then reduces may aid learning (emphasized by Hull's follower Janet Taylor Spence), while too little or too much hinders it; teacher-induced anxiety can backfire, as when a class learned to fill in worksheets to earn recess instead of learning arithmetic. The book also stresses the testing effect (retrieval practice): repeated testing at spaced intervals beats restudying, works even without feedback, and works in memory-impaired patients, but students rarely self-test, so instructors should build it in (p. 144).
- Spence modified Hull: he defended S-R accounts of discrimination learning, treated habit strength as built by contiguity and frequency without reinforcement (latent learning showed learning without reward), made reward matter through incentive motivation, added rather than multiplied drive and incentive, and explained extinction by frustration producing competing responses (pp. 146–151). Amsel's frustration theory used these ideas to explain the partial reinforcement effect: animals trained with occasional nonreward learn to keep approaching despite frustration, so they resist extinction, but only if they first built strong expectancy through many continuously rewarded trials (84 vs. 24 trials) (pp. 152–155).
- Neal Miller showed autonomic responses such as heart rate and salivation can be operantly conditioned, leading to biofeedback; evidence on clinical benefit is mixed, with placebo and nonspecific effects a concern for some uses such as hypertension and tension headache (pp. 155–158). Criticisms of Hull: inconsistent predictions, limited relevance beyond the lab, and fitting equations to data after the fact (pp. 145–146).

**Key terms**: hypothetico-deductive method, postulate/theorem, stimulus trace, habit strength, drive, drive reduction, drive stimulus reduction, reaction potential, reactive inhibition, conditioned inhibition, reminiscence effect, massed vs. distributed practice, oscillation effect, reaction threshold, incentive motivation (K), Crespi effect, stimulus-intensity dynamism, fractional antedating goal response (rG–sG), habit family hierarchy, delay of reinforcement, latent learning, frustration-competition theory, frustration effect, partial reinforcement effect, visceral conditioning, biofeedback, testing effect

**Notable studies / people**
- Crespi (1942), Zeaman (1949): shifting reinforcer size changes performance quickly (p. 136).
- Sheffield & Roby (1950): saccharine reinforces hungry rats (p. 138).
- Cepeda et al. (2006): 317 experiments supporting distributed practice (p. 134).
- Spence & Lippitt (1940): latent learning of food and water locations in satiated rats (p. 148).
- Amsel & Roussel (1952), Bower (1962), Amsel (1958): frustration effect and partial reinforcement effect (pp. 152–155).
- Miller & Carmona (1967): operant control of salivation in dogs (p. 156).
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006) and others: testing effect (p. 144).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's stated implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that practice should be spaced and that repeated retrieval practice is superior to restudying; schedule many short, spaced quiz-style sessions instead of rereading, and build them in because learners tend not to do this alone (pp. 134, 144).
- Keep stress moderate and avoid contingencies that reward looking finished over actually learning (p. 144).
- Insert rest between practice bouts: performance can rise after a break (reminiscence), suggesting that cramming undercounts what a spaced schedule yields (own inference from pp. 133–134).

## Ch 7 — Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (pp. 165–210)

**Core ideas**
- Pavlov, a physiologist who won a Nobel Prize for digestion research, stumbled on "psychic" reflexes while studying digestion (the book corrects the popular story that he simply saw a dog salivate at food). In classical conditioning a neutral conditioned stimulus is repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus, which acts as the reinforcer, until the CS alone evokes a conditioned response (pp. 165–169).
- Core phenomena: extinction when the CS is presented without the US; spontaneous recovery after a rest; higher-order conditioning (a CS used to condition another stimulus, weakening by the third order); generalization (response to similar stimuli, strongest nearest the training stimulus) and discrimination, which grows with prolonged training or differential reinforcement; an optimal CS-US gap of roughly half a second in traditional procedures (pp. 169–172). Generalization parallels Thorndike's identical elements idea (p. 172).
- Pavlov explained these with cortical excitation and inhibition forming a shifting "mosaic", spreading (irradiation) to explain generalization and narrowing (concentration) to explain discrimination, and a dynamic stereotype: a stable pattern from a predictable environment that is efficient but hard to change when the environment changes. In humans, words form a second signal system, as when responses generalize by meaning, and with age (pp. 173–178).
- Classical and instrumental conditioning both depend on reinforcement and share extinction, recovery, generalization, discrimination and secondary reinforcement. The difference is that classical conditioning elicits an involuntary response while instrumental conditioning depends on an emitted response, and the two cannot be fully separated in practice (pp. 178–179).
- Modern work revises Pavlov: CRs are not always weaker copies of URs, and can prepare for or oppose the UR (for example, drug cues produce compensatory responses that underlie tolerance, which is lost in a new setting, with overdose risk) (pp. 180–181). Extinction is now seen as new learning that interferes with, but does not erase, the original association; spontaneous recovery, renewal in the original context, and reinstatement after the US show this, and contextual cues decide which memory is retrieved (pp. 181–182).
- Contiguity is not enough. Overshadowing and Kamin's blocking show that a stimulus paired with the US may fail to be learned if another cue already predicts it; Egger and Miller and Rescorla showed that the CS must carry information (contingency), and that a negative contingency teaches the CS signals safety. The Rescorla-Wagner model says each trial's learning depends on how much of the maximum is still unlearned, so blocking occurs because earlier cues have used it up. Mackintosh (attention to predictive cues) and Kamin-Wagner (surprise drives learning) explain further findings such as latent inhibition and learned irrelevance; Bolles argues organisms learn expectancies (pp. 182–194).
- Learned helplessness (Seligman): animals that experience uncontrollable shock stop trying to escape later; the key is lost control, not the shock; in humans it resembles depression. Garcia's conditioned taste aversion shows long-delay, one-trial, extinction-resistant learning, with biological preparedness, with applications to chemotherapy patients and predator control (pp. 189–190, 195–196). Watson's Little Albert study and Bregman's replication show conditioned fear and its limits; Watson and Jones's work and Wolpe's systematic desensitization (graded exposure with relaxation) reduce fear; flooding and counterconditioning are other clinical uses; Ader's work showed conditioned immune responses (pp. 197–203).
- On education the book says Pavlovian conditioning operates incidentally in classrooms: attitudes toward subjects, teachers and school form by pairing, so negative classroom experiences can create lasting aversions, and deliberate use can resemble brainwashing, as in advertising (pp. 203–204).

- The book states that affective associations form incidentally in classrooms, so avoid pairing subjects with anxiety, humiliation or coercion; negative experiences can generate lasting avoidance of school or specific subjects (pp. 203–204).

- Graded, gradual exposure to feared tasks works better than avoidance, which prevents unlearning (own inference from pp. 200–202).

## Ch 8 — Edwin Ray Guthrie (pp. 211–236)

**Core ideas**
- Guthrie reduced learning to one law, contiguity: a combination of stimuli that accompanied a movement will tend to be followed by that movement when it recurs. Predictions stay probabilistic because many stimuli and competing tendencies are always present (pp. 212–213). Learning is all-or-none on the first pairing (one-trial learning), and the recency principle says that what was done last in a situation is what will be done next time (p. 213).
- Movement-produced stimuli (feedback from our own muscles) fill gaps between an external cue and a response, linking sequences of movements; this is Guthrie's account of chaining, contrasted with Skinner's external-cue account (pp. 213–214).
- Practice improves skills not because associations strengthen gradually, but because a skill is made of huge numbers of movement-to-stimulus associations that must each be formed across varied circumstances; movements are learned in one trial, acts and skills need practice. Guthrie and Horton argued that Thorndike's gradual curves arose because the puzzle-box problem was never identical from trial to trial (pp. 214–216).
- Reinforcement is merely a mechanical arrangement: the escape or reward changes the situation abruptly, shielding the last response from being unlearned. In the Guthrie-Horton study of about 800 cat escapes, each cat repeated its own stereotyped last response, even ignoring offered food; later critics argued these were instinctive rubbing behaviors (pp. 216–217, 232).
- Forgetting is not decay but interference: it occurs only when a new response is learned in place of the old one in the same situation, so old associations persist if cues are protected from new learning (pp. 217–219).
- Habit breaking: find the cues for the unwanted act and have the person do something else in their presence. Three methods: threshold (introduce the cue at a weak level and increase gradually), fatigue (present the cue until the person tires and stops responding), and incompatible response (pair the cue with a stronger stimulus eliciting an opposing response). All are forms of associative shifting. Sidetracking avoids the cues by changing environment, only a partial escape (pp. 220–223).
- Punishment works only when it makes the person do something incompatible with the punished act, in the presence of the original cues; pain itself is irrelevant. Fowler and Miller (1963) found shock to a rat's front paws slowed running while hind-paw shock sped it up, matching this view, though the book notes Guthrie's account is incomplete (pp. 223–226). Commands that can be disobeyed teach disobedience (p. 225).
- Drives supply maintaining stimuli that keep an animal active until some act removes them; the removing act is thereby preserved, which Guthrie used to explain intentions and drug or alcohol habits (pp. 226–227).
- Education (stated in the book): little transfer is expected, since learning is tied to the stimuli present. The best place to study is where you will be tested, practice the exact behaviors that will be required, and write practice essay answers under test-like timing; students learn what lecture or reading makes them do, not its content. Guthrie, like Thorndike, rejected formal discipline and supported realistic training (apprenticeships, trade schools). The book adds that successive relearning over several sessions to a mastery criterion boosts long-term retention, and combined with retesting improves transfer to new situations (Rawson et al., 2018; Pan & Rickard, 2018) (pp. 227–228, 230–231).

**Key terms**: law of contiguity, one-trial learning, recency principle, movement-produced stimuli, movements vs. acts vs. skills, mechanical reinforcement, stereotyped behavior, interference/retroactive inhibition, threshold method, fatigue method, incompatible response method, sidetracking, maintaining stimuli, intentions, transfer of training, postremity, response probability, dynamic situations, relearning

**Notable studies / people**
- Guthrie & Horton (1946): cats in a puzzle box repeat their last response (p. 217).
- Fowler & Miller (1963): location of shock changes its effect on running speed (p. 226).
- Voeks (1950): formalized Guthrie's theory (pp. 229–230).
- Rawson et al. (2018), Pan & Rickard (2018): relearning and retesting (p. 230).
- Moore & Stuttard (1979): cat behavior reinterpreted as greeting behavior (p. 232). Harris (2000): behaviors learned at home may not transfer outside it (p. 228).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's stated implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that you should practice the exact behavior and conditions you will be tested on, including studying in the testing room and writing practice essays under time limits (p. 228).
- Because learning is tied to the situations in which it occurred, vary examples and settings so that a skill attaches to many cues (2 plus 2 with apples, books, blocks), and relearn across several sessions to criterion, with retests (p. 230).
- Correct unwanted habits by substituting a different response in the presence of the same cue, with early cues weak and graded; avoid commands and warnings you cannot enforce (pp. 222, 225) (own inference for classroom use).

## Ch 9 — William Kaye Estes (pp. 237–264)

**Core ideas**
- Estes represents the move from grand theories to "miniature systems." His statistical learning theory (stimulus sampling theory) is a mathematical version of Guthrie's contiguity idea; the book stresses that, unlike earlier theories, it offers little for classrooms or therapy and is mainly an attempt to describe how learning happens at a basic level (pp. 237–238).
- In stimulus sampling theory, a situation contains many stimulus elements, each attached to the target response or to some other response; on each trial the learner samples only a fraction (theta) of the elements, and whatever response ends the trial becomes attached to the sampled elements by contiguity. The chance of the target response equals the share of sampled elements already attached to it (pp. 238–240).
- This yields a negatively accelerated learning curve like Hull's (fast early gains, diminishing later), with learning speed depending on how large a sample is taken. A learner sampling 20 percent of elements reaches near-perfect performance in roughly 25 trials, versus about 105 for one sampling 5 percent (pp. 241–242).
- Generalization and transfer follow Thorndike and Guthrie: transfer is proportional to the shared stimulus elements. Extinction uses the same mechanism as acquisition (elements switch back to other responses), and spontaneous recovery arises because transitory elements conditioned during training are absent at extinction and later reappear (pp. 242–244). Probability matching in prediction tasks is also derived from the model (pp. 244–245).
- Estes's Markov model argues that learning can be all-or-none even when group curves look gradual: individual learners jump from chance to perfect performance at different trials, and pooled data creates an illusion of gradual improvement. In an eight-pair study with 48 people, 71 percent of items answered correctly on test 1 were right on test 2, versus 9 percent of items missed, supporting "learned completely or not at all". Critics (Underwood and Keppel) argue incremental theories with Hull's oscillation also fit (pp. 246–250).
- Later Estes embraced cognition: memory mediates between stimuli and behavior, a scanning model of decision making uses stored response-outcome knowledge, and the array model of categorization compares new items with stored exemplars using multiplicative similarity (pp. 250–256). He came to treat reinforcement as information about response-outcome relations that affects performance, not learning, since learning occurs without reinforcement; behavior is guided by feedback in a cybernetic way (pp. 256–257).
- Contributions: precise, top-down mathematical modeling that fed contemporary cognitive science and connectionist models; criticisms: narrow scope, ignoring contingency versus contiguity, and artificially constrained experiments. The book notes mathematical models have mostly refined descriptions rather than revealing new facts so far (pp. 258–260).

**Key terms**: statistical learning theory, stimulus sampling theory, stimulus elements, A1/A2 responses, theta (sample proportion), sampling with replacement, negatively accelerated curve, generalization, extinction, spontaneous recovery, probability matching, Markov process, all-or-none learning, paired-associate learning, scanning model, array model, similarity coefficient, response-outcome (R-O) relations, cybernetic model

**Notable studies / people**
- Estes & Skinner (1941): conditioned suppression technique (p. 237).
- Estes (1960, 1964a): paired-associate studies supporting all-or-none learning (pp. 246–249).
- Underwood & Keppel (1962): critique favoring incremental accounts (p. 250).
- Estes & Straughan (1954): probability matching analysis (p. 245).
- Estes (1994): array model of classification, extending Medin & Shaffer (1978) (p. 251).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference; the book itself says the theory is not readily applied, p. 238)
- If learning of single associations is closer to all-or-none, then quick checks of individual items (not class averages) show who has actually mastered a given fact; items missed on a first test tend to stay missed without further study, so retest them after new instruction (own inference from pp. 247–249).
- Because the learning curve shows large early gains and diminishing returns, shift practice time toward items not yet learned rather than endlessly rehearsing known items (own inference from p. 242).
- Practice across varied features and contexts so more of the stimulus elements become associated with the target response, which raises transfer to new settings (own inference from pp. 242–243, 252–253).

## Ch 10 — Gestalt Theory (pp. 265–293)

**Core ideas**
- Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka) began with the phi phenomenon, apparent motion that arises from flashing lights but is not found in the parts. It holds that we experience organized wholes and that dissecting experience into elements, as structuralists and behaviorists did, distorts it (pp. 265–268).
- Gestalt theory is a field theory: parts of a dynamic system influence each other, as in Lewin's life space, where behavior depends on all the psychological facts a person is currently aware of (pp. 268–269). The brain actively organizes input, according to the law of Prägnanz, which says psychological organization is as good (simple, complete, orderly) as conditions allow, with closure as an example. Phenomena such as the blind spot, scotomas and perceptual filling-in are cited as hints of an active brain (pp. 269–273).
- Behavior depends on the subjective "behavioral environment" rather than the physical one (Koffka's horseman who crossed a frozen lake unaware), so beliefs and attitudes shape action (pp. 273–274).
- Learning is a form of perception and problem solving: a problem creates cognitive disequilibrium and tension until solved; unfinished tasks are remembered better (Zeigarnik effect). Solution comes by insight after a period of mentally testing hypotheses, is sudden, error-free afterward, long retained, and transferable. Köhler's ape studies (detours, stick tools) and neuroscience of insight (gamma burst before insight solutions; positive mood helps, anxiety hinders) are cited. Gestalt theorists argued Thorndike saw gradual learning only because key problem elements were hidden from his animals (pp. 274–277).
- Transposition: animals trained to approach a darker shade chose the darker of a new pair, suggesting they learned a relation rather than a specific stimulus. Spence explained it with generalization of approach and avoidance tendencies, which also predicts where transposition breaks down, leaving the debate unsettled (pp. 278–280).
- Wertheimer's educational message (Productive Thinking): understanding-based learning is deeper, more durable and more flexible than rote memorization. He faulted both formal logic-focused teaching and drill-and-reinforcement associationist teaching for inhibiting understanding. In his parallelogram example, students taught only the formula failed on unfamiliar shapes, whereas those who grasped the principle of rearranging excess and deficit to form a rectangle could solve many forms. Katona's digit series: learners who looked for a pattern (squares of 1 to 9) recalled it perfectly weeks later, others forgot (pp. 280–285).
- Gestalt classroom: reinforcement is intrinsic, the relief of ambiguity and satisfaction of curiosity (Bruner, Holt); start from the familiar, divide material into meaningful units tied to an overall concept, allow student-teacher interaction, avoid rote memorization. Popper's view of learning as conjecture and refutation supports classroom practice in which students propose solutions and critique them, asking what is wrong with each (pp. 285–288).
- Koffka's memory trace and trace systems: traces influence similar later processes; with repetition a trace system dominates individual traces, so memories drift toward regular, familiar, complete forms (Prägnanz). Praise: critique of atomism and the turn toward intrinsic reinforcement; criticism: vague concepts and little experimental research on learning (pp. 288–291).

**Key terms**: Gestalt, phi phenomenon, phenomenology, molar vs. molecular, field theory, life space, law of Prägnanz, closure, isomorphism, geographical vs. behavioral environment, cognitive disequilibrium, Zeigarnik effect, insight, presolution period, transposition, absolute vs. relational theory, productive thinking, intrinsic reinforcement, cognitive hunger, memory trace, trace system, integrative agnosia, prosopagnosia

**Notable studies / people**
- Köhler (1925), The Mentality of Apes: detour and tool problems (pp. 274–276).
- Zeigarnik: better memory for interrupted tasks (p. 275).
- Wertheimer (1945/1959): parallelogram area study and productive thinking (pp. 280–284). Katona (1940): memorizing digits versus finding the pattern (pp. 284–285).
- Kounios, Jung-Beeman and colleagues: brain correlates of insight (p. 277).
- Spence (1937, 1942): relational versus absolute explanations of transposition (pp. 279–280).
- Popper via Berkson & Wettersten (1984): learning as conjecture and refutation (pp. 287–288).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's stated implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that teaching for understanding beats rote memorization: have learners find the underlying principle or pattern themselves (and be given prompts to look for it), because understood material is retained long and applies to new cases (pp. 280–285).
- Design lessons that start from the familiar, break material into meaningful units tied to a larger idea, and use problems that create a productive gap in understanding for students to resolve; the book endorses lectures that allow interaction (pp. 285–287).
- Use conjecture-and-critique discussions, where each proposed answer is examined for flaws, as a classroom routine from primary school to graduate school (pp. 287–288).

## Ch 11 — Jean Piaget (pp. 295–314)

**Core ideas**
- Piaget's path to his theory began at the Binet laboratory, where he noticed that children's wrong answers were more revealing than right ones, because errors followed age-related qualitative patterns. He used the open-ended clinical method of questioning to probe them (pp. 295–296). He defined intelligence as adaptation: acts that move organism and environment toward conditions suited to survival, changing as the child matures and gains experience; his approach is called genetic (developmental) epistemology (p. 297).
- Knowledge is a set of cognitive structures made of schemata (general potentials to act, such as grasping, which can also operate covertly as thought). What a child can perceive or respond to depends on the schemata available (pp. 297–298).
- Every experience involves assimilation (fitting events into existing structures) and accommodation (modifying structures when events do not fit), and accommodation is what corresponds to learning. Both occur at every level of development, although early experience demands more accommodation (pp. 298–299). The driving force is equilibration, an inborn push toward balance; a mismatch creates a mild disequilibrium that motivates change, which is Piaget's analogue of reinforcement (pp. 299–300).
- Interiorization: adaptive activity shifts from overt sensorimotor action to internal operations (reversible mental actions), first concrete operations tied to real objects and later formal operations that handle hypotheticals (pp. 300–301).
- Four stages in a fixed order, with ages only approximate: sensorimotor (to about 2; object permanence emerges), preoperational (about 2 to 7; failure of conservation, intuitive thinking), concrete operations (about 7 to 11 or 12; conservation, seriation, classes with concrete problems), formal operations (about 11 or 12 onward; hypothetical thought) (pp. 301–304). Whether conservation can be taught early remains open; maturation supplies a framework while physical and social experience are indispensable (pp. 303–305).
- Optimal learning requires material that is partly assimilable and partly novel: if it cannot be assimilated it is meaningless; if it is fully assimilated nothing is learned. Experiences should be moderately challenging, changed in small steps tailored to each learner's structure, which led Piaget to favor individual attention for reasons different from Skinner's (p. 304).
- Piaget rejected the "copy theory" of knowledge in S-R psychology: knowing involves acting on and transforming the world. Compared with Gestalt theory, he held that organizing ability develops, whereas Gestaltists held it innate; this leads to different teaching emphases (pp. 305–307).
- Education (stated in the book): build instruction around the learner's current cognitive structure, offer mildly challenging experiences, determine each student's level, favor opportunities for discovery over direct instruction, and avoid external reinforcement as harmful to understanding. Behaviorists and Piaget both end up favoring individualization, for different reasons (pp. 307–308).
- Criticisms: the clinical method lacks controls; cultural generalization is weak; infants appear to grasp object permanence and physical laws earlier than Piaget thought; many adults fail the water-level task; and direct instruction, or guided discovery, has outperformed pure discovery in elementary science (Klahr and colleagues; Mayer) (pp. 308–310).

**Key terms**: genetic epistemology, schema, content, cognitive structure, assimilation, accommodation, functional invariants, equilibration, interiorization, operations, reversibility, concrete and formal operations, sensorimotor, preoperational, conservation, object permanence, egocentric, transduction, clinical method, copy theory of knowledge, progressive equilibrium, guided discovery

**Notable studies / people**
- Piaget's clinical method and observation of his own children (pp. 296–297).
- Conservation of liquid task, preoperational failure (pp. 302–303); Roman numeral and letter circle problem (pp. 303–304).
- Baillargeon and others: early object permanence and physical knowledge in infants (p. 309).
- Kalichman (1988) and Hecht & Proffitt (1995): water-level task (p. 309).
- Klahr & Nigam (2004): direct instruction beat discovery in science concepts; Mayer (2004): guided discovery effective (p. 310).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's stated implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that material should be partly known and partly new, with mild challenge, and that teachers should find out each student's level first; pretests or diagnostic questions of existing understanding can set the entry point of a study guide (pp. 304, 307–308).
- Because direct or guided instruction outperformed unguided discovery in the studies the book cites, favor guided discovery (hints, scaffolds, worked examples) over leaving learners alone (pp. 308, 310).
- Look at wrong answers as diagnostic of what structure the learner is using, and design follow-up questions that expose the reasoning behind an error (own inference from p. 296).

## Ch 12 — Edward Chace Tolman (pp. 315–342)

**Core ideas**
- Tolman blended Gestalt ideas with behaviorism: methodologically a behaviorist but substantively a cognitive theorist who studied "molar" (whole, goal-directed) behavior and not isolated twitches. His purposive behaviorism treats purpose as a descriptive property (behavior persists until a goal is reached), and rats were his preferred subjects (pp. 316–319).
- What is learned is what leads to what (an S-S or sign-sign relation), summarized as a cognitive map of the environment. With a map an organism can choose alternate routes when one is blocked, usually the least effortful. Motivation matters mainly as a perceptual emphasizer and does not drive learning itself (p. 319).
- Reinforcement is replaced by confirmation: tentative expectancies (hypotheses) that are confirmed become beliefs (means-end readiness), a cognitive process requiring no need state. Pauses at choice points in a maze (vicarious trial and error) suggest options are being tested mentally (pp. 320–321).
- The learning/performance distinction is central: knowledge stays dormant until need prompts action (like knowing where a drinking fountain is). Tolman and Honzik (1930) showed latent learning: rats first unrewarded, then rewarded from day 11, quickly matched or exceeded always-rewarded rats, supporting reinforcement as a performance variable; S-R theorists countered with incentive motivation and removal from the apparatus as a reinforcer. Latent extinction (extinction after merely seeing an empty goal box) also fits expectancy explanations, though the dispute is not resolved (pp. 320–323).
- Place learning versus response learning: rats learned to go to a location more easily than to make a fixed turn, and with the familiar path blocked they chose the alley pointing directly at the goal location, not nearest alleys, though S-R theorists proposed alternative readings (pp. 323–327). Switching the type of reward (bran mush to sunflower seeds) disrupted performance beyond what incentive value alone explained, supporting reinforcement expectancy (pp. 327–328).
- The formal system uses independent variables (maintenance schedule, goal appropriateness, cues, motor demands, maze pattern, accumulated experience), individual-difference variables (heredity, age, training, endocrine state) and intervening variables including hypotheses; Tolman introduced intervening variables to psychology and Hull borrowed them (pp. 328–333). His six kinds of learning were cathexes, equivalence beliefs, field expectancies and field-cognition modes (transferable problem-solving strategies), drive discriminations, and motor patterns (pp. 333–335).
- Education (stated in the book): like Gestalt theory, stress thinking and understanding; let students test hypotheses in problem situations, so learning is largely eliminating wrong strategies; use small-group discussion; the teacher acts as a consultant in clarifying and confirming or disconfirming hypotheses; present topics from several viewpoints to build a cognitive map; extrinsic reinforcement is unnecessary because learning is continuous (pp. 336–337).
- Contributions and critique: his spatial learning work anticipated comparative cognition and later hippocampal and entorhinal findings on cognitive maps; his idea that reinforcement gives information echoes in later theories. The theory has too many variables to test and produced few practical applications (pp. 337–338).

**Key terms**: molar behavior, purposive behaviorism, intervening variable, S-S learning, cognitive map, principle of least effort, expectation/hypothesis, confirmation, means-end readiness (belief), vicarious trial and error, learning vs. performance, latent learning, latent extinction, place vs. response learning, reinforcement expectancy, behavior ratio, cathexis, equivalence belief, field expectancy, field-cognition mode, drive discrimination, motor pattern

**Notable studies / people**
- Tolman & Honzik (1930): latent learning in maze (pp. 321–322).
- Tolman, Ritchie & Kalish (1946a, b): place versus response learning; radial alley test (pp. 323–327).
- Elliott (1928): changing reward type disrupts maze performance (pp. 327–328).
- Tolman (1938, 1949): determiners of behavior at a choice point; six kinds of learning (pp. 328–335).
- Moser, Moser & McNaughton (2017); O'Keefe & Nadel (1978): neural basis of cognitive maps (p. 337).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's stated implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that students should test their own hypotheses in problem situations, discuss in small groups, and meet a topic from different viewpoints to form a flexible map; the teacher's job is to help confirm or disconfirm ideas (pp. 336–337).
- Because knowledge can stay latent until it is needed, check for learning with transfer or application tasks and not just immediate performance, and let learners explore material before any graded performance (own inference from pp. 320–322).
- Keep feedback informative and tied to what the student expected, since confirmation, not reward, shapes beliefs; surprising or mismatched rewards can disrupt performance (own inference from pp. 320, 327–328).

## Ch 13 — Albert Bandura (pp. 343–376)

**Core ideas**
- Earlier accounts of observational learning: Thorndike and Watson found no learning by watching in cats, chicks, dogs, monkeys. Miller and Dollard treated imitation as reinforced instrumental behavior (same, copying and matched-dependent behavior, plus generalized imitation), and Skinner treated the model as a discriminative stimulus; all required the observer to respond and be reinforced (pp. 344–347). More recent animal studies (chickens, quail, rats, chimpanzees) show nonhumans can learn from watching, sometimes with delay and without direct reinforcement (p. 347).
- Bandura separates observational learning from imitation: you can learn information from watching without copying (swerving around a pothole you saw another car hit). A "model" is anything conveying information, including films, instructions or demonstrations (pp. 347–348).
- Bandura (1965): children watching a model who was rewarded, punished or neither for aggression differed in how aggressively they acted, but when offered an incentive all reproduced the behavior, showing that all had learned it. As in Tolman and Honzik, reinforcement affects performance, not learning; observers must be aware of the contingencies for consequences to matter (pp. 348–350).
- Four processes govern observational learning: attention (influenced by models who are similar, respected, competent, high status, attractive), retention (imaginal and especially verbal coding, rehearsed later), behavioral production (physical ability plus cognitive rehearsal with self-comparison against the stored template), and motivation (reinforcement creates expectancies and acts as an incentive to perform) (pp. 350–353).
- Reciprocal determinism: person, behavior and environment mutually influence each other, so behavior can shape the environment as well as the reverse (pp. 353–354). Behavior is largely self-regulated through internalized standards; self-reinforcement tends to maintain behavior better than external reinforcement, extrinsic rewards can reduce motivation, standards that are too high produce distress, and moderate subgoals motivate best. Perceived self-efficacy (built from success, seeing similar others succeed, and persuasion) predicts effort, persistence and less fear (pp. 354–355).
- Moral conduct rests on internalized codes and self-contempt, but people disengage through justification, euphemism, advantageous comparison, displacement or diffusion of responsibility, distorting consequences, dehumanization and blaming victims; Bandura opposes stage and trait theories as too stable an account of behavior (pp. 356–358).
- Modeling can teach new responses, inhibit or disinhibit, facilitate existing responses, spur creativity by exposure to varied models, and teach abstract rules (observe varied examples, extract the common principle, apply it to new cases) (pp. 358–359). In treating phobias, multiple models were better than a single one for symbolic modeling, and participant modeling (modeling plus guided contact) was most effective, beating symbolic modeling and desensitization, with generalization to other fears (pp. 360–362). Media can model aggression and novel crimes (pp. 363–365).
- Social cognitive theory stresses human agency. Mirror neuron research (Parma monkeys and human systems) is presented as a plausible neural basis for observational learning, including automatic mimicry that can be stored for later or suppressed (pp. 365–368).
- Education (stated in the book): teachers are influential models of skills, problem-solving strategies, moral codes, standards, rules and creativity, and the more closely one imitates an effective model the better the understanding (e.g., imitating accent aids comprehension). Self-efficacy work has been applied from elementary school to medical education; intrinsic reinforcement from meeting personal standards matters more than extrinsic reinforcement; goals should be individually set, neither too easy nor too hard (pp. 368–369).
- Evaluation: major contribution was showing learning by watching without imitation or reinforcement; critics say reciprocal determinism defies causal analysis and the theory is very broad (pp. 369–370).

**Key terms**: observational learning, imitation, matched-dependent behavior, generalized imitation, vicarious reinforcement and punishment, learning vs. performance, attentional/retentional/behavioral production/motivational processes, delayed modeling, reciprocal determinism, self-regulation, performance standards, perceived self-efficacy, moral disengagement mechanisms, disinhibition, facilitation, abstract modeling, vicarious extinction, symbolic/live/participant modeling, social cognitive theory, agency, mirror neurons, chameleon effect

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1898, 1901), Watson (1908): no observational learning in animals (p. 345). Miller & Dollard (1941) (pp. 345–346).
- Bandura (1965): model consequences and children's aggression (pp. 348–349).
- Bandura & Kupers (1964): children adopt models' self-reward standards (p. 354).
- Bandura, Grusec & Menlove (1967); Bandura & Menlove (1968); Bandura, Blanchard & Ritter (1969): modeling for fear reduction (pp. 360–362).
- Nicol & Pope (1993) and others: observational learning in animals (p. 347).
- Rizzolatti and colleagues: mirror neurons (p. 367).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that teachers should deliberately model skills and problem-solving strategies, use credible, competent models, and help students set personal goals of moderate difficulty (pp. 368–369).
- Show worked examples that make steps and verbal reasoning explicit, with several different models or cases so learners extract the common rule (abstract modeling), then have them rehearse and compare their own performance to the model (own inference from pp. 351–352, 359, 361).
- For fearful or low-confidence learners, graded success experiences and guided participation build self-efficacy more than encouragement alone (own inference from pp. 355, 361–362).

## Ch 14 — Donald Olding Hebb (pp. 377–414)

**Core ideas**
- Hebb began as a Pavlovian but was moved by Lashley's findings (mass action and equipotentiality: learning loss depends on how much cortex is destroyed, not where) to reject the brain as a switchboard. His clinical work with Penfield's patients suggested that intelligence comes from experience and that early experience matters more than adult experience (pp. 378–379).
- Restricted and enriched environments: adults with congenital cataracts and chimpanzees reared in darkness had to learn to see; rats raised as pets in Hebb's home outperformed lab-caged rats; enriched rearing speeds learning (Bennett et al., Rosenzweig), and a few hours daily in an enriched environment can reverse early deprivation effects. A correlational study ties lower family income and parental education to smaller cortical area and hippocampus. Hebb's explanation: varied early sensory experience builds more neural circuits later used for new learning (pp. 380–382).
- Cell assemblies (patterns of neurons that become linked by firing together, the neural basis of an idea) join into phase sequences (a stream of thought). The neurophysiological postulate says that when one cell repeatedly helps fire another, the efficiency of that connection increases. Early learning is slow associative buildup of assemblies and sequences, whereas later learning is quick, insightful rearrangement of what exists, so child and adult learning differ (pp. 382–383).
- Arousal theory: performance is best at a moderate level of arousal that depends on the task (simple, well-practiced tasks tolerate wide arousal; very complex tasks need lower arousal). Reinforcement is whatever moves arousal toward optimal, a rise or a fall, unlike Hull's drive reduction (pp. 384–385).
- Memory: short-term memory is reverberating neural activity that consolidates into long-term structural change. Duncan's electroshock study showed disruption is greatest right after learning and negligible after about an hour; retrograde amnesia fits. The patient H.M. showed that hippocampal damage blocks consolidation of declarative memory while procedural learning (basal ganglia) survives, so repetition alone does not create long-term memory (pp. 385–389).
- Neuroscience built on Hebb: Olds and Milner's brain reinforcement centers (dopamine in the nucleus accumbens seems tied to wanting rather than pleasure, and dopamine-deficient mice still learned), Kandel's Aplysia work on habituation and sensitization, long-term potentiation and depression (the Hebbian synapse, "fire together, wire together"), and lifelong neuroplasticity including dendrite growth with experience, neurogenesis in the hippocampus, and recovery after injury; stress and depression reduce plasticity while novel experience increases it (pp. 389–401).
- Artificial networks apply the Hebb rule and later delta or back-propagation rules (NETtalk learned to read aloud) (pp. 401–406).
- Education (stated in the book): for young children provide a rich, varied sensory environment because the more is represented neurally the more the child can think about; for older children help them rearrange what they know in new, creative ways; keep stimulation at an optimal level for the task and student, avoiding both commotion and deadening quiet (pp. 406–407).
- Evaluation: Hebb linked higher processes to synapses, and the theory remains heuristic; criticized for relying only on excitation and for resisting revision (pp. 407–408).

**Key terms**: switchboard conception, mass action, equipotentiality, restricted/enriched environment, cell assembly, phase sequence, neurophysiological postulate, fractionation and recruitment, arousal theory, optimal arousal, reverberating activity, consolidation, short-term vs. long-term memory, retrograde and anterograde amnesia, declarative and procedural memory, hippocampus, reinforcement centers, dopamine, habituation, sensitization, long-term potentiation, long-term depression, Hebbian synapse, neuroplasticity, neurogenesis, axonal sprouting, parallel distributed processing, Hebb rule, delta rule

**Notable studies / people**

- Hebb (1949); Bennett, Diamond, Krech & Rosenzweig (1964): enriched environments (p. 381).
- Duncan (1949): ECS timing and consolidation (p. 386). Milner and H.M. (pp. 387–388).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that young children benefit from a rich, varied environment and that older learners benefit from being helped to reorganize what they know, plus a moderate level of stimulation in the classroom (pp. 406–407).
- Because consolidation after learning is vulnerable and repetition alone does not guarantee long-term storage, avoid stacking disruptive or highly arousing activity immediately after new material, and use spaced review to re-engage consolidation (own inference from pp. 386–389).
- Match arousal to task difficulty: use calmer, lower-stimulation settings for complex work and allow more energizing conditions for simple practice (own inference from pp. 384–385).

## Ch 15 — Robert C. Bolles and Evolutionary Psychology (pp. 415–448)

**Core ideas**
- Evolutionary psychology applies Darwinian and neo-Darwinian ideas (natural selection acting on heritable variation, adaptation, inclusive fitness) to learning. The chapter cautions that evolution has no foresight, adaptations are not necessarily optimal, and a trait's current use may be an exaptation or side effect (spandrel), not its original function (pp. 416–418).
- Bolles argued that learning consists mostly of S-S expectancies (classical conditioning) and R-S expectancies (operant and instrumental), formed without reinforcement by directional contiguity, and that many of these expectancies are innate. He rejected the "empirical principle of equipotentiality," the assumption that laws of learning hold equally for any stimulus, response and species (pp. 419–420).
- Motivation restricts response flexibility, and the niche argument says tasks that fit an animal's evolved predispositions are learned easily, while tasks that violate them yield anomalies. Seligman's preparedness continuum describes how readily associations are learned. The environment of evolutionary adaptedness reminds us that traits may mismatch modern settings (pp. 420–422).
- Evidence in instrumental learning: Petrinovich and Bolles found that rats learned water-reinforced position habits better, but food-reinforced alternation better, matching foraging ecology. Escape and avoidance rely on species-specific defense reactions (freezing, fleeing), so tasks needing other responses (a pigeon pecking to avoid shock, a rat pressing a bar to avoid shock) are learned poorly. Bolles judged the choice of required response more important than reinforcement contingencies (pp. 422–424).
- Misbehavior of organisms: the Brelands' raccoons and pigs drifted toward instinctive food-related behavior despite delayed or lost reward (instinctual drift). Autoshaping, where pigeons peck an illuminated key preceding free food even when pecking cancels food, is read as a food-signal eliciting innate pecking, not response learning (pp. 424–428).
- Classical conditioning is also constrained: Garcia and Koelling's rats linked taste to nausea and audiovisual cues to shock but not the reverse; rats and quail pick different cues (taste versus color) when made ill; female rats' taste aversions extinguish faster. Timberlake's biological behaviorism argues that lab tasks are tuned to species and that behavior in them is overdetermined, so findings should not be generalized across species without regard to preparedness (pp. 428–432).
- Human applications: phobias for snakes and spiders arise easily and resist extinction, may involve nonconscious fear processing (backward masking studies), and capture attention; mate preferences across 37 cultures (kindness and intelligence ranked highest), scent and immune-gene cues, a behavioral immune system, and language as biologically prepared (Chomsky's challenges, Nicaraguan sign language, specific language impairment) (pp. 432–440).
- Education (stated in the book): evolutionary psychology gives no specific teaching techniques but bears on curricula: teach what children are developmentally ready for, spend cultural resources (schools included) on inhibiting natural tendencies toward selfishness, prejudice and aggression where society values cooperation and tolerance, build on prepared abilities such as language (the book mentions early bilingual study), and avoid "nothing-butism," the view that behavior is determined by genes or culture alone (p. 441).
- Contribution and criticism: it insists on pairing proximate explanations (reinforcement history) with ultimate explanations (selection history), resolving apparent exceptions to learning principles. Criticisms (circularity, genetic determinism, social Darwinism, "innate means unlearned") are answered by the claim that evolution guides and constrains learning but does not replace it (pp. 441–443).

**Key terms**: natural selection, adaptation, exaptation, spandrel, inclusive fitness, ethology, S-S and R-S expectancy, empirical principle of equipotentiality, niche argument, environment of evolutionary adaptedness (EEA), preparedness continuum, species-specific defense reactions (SSDRs), misbehavior of organisms, instinctual drift, autoshaping, Garcia effect, biological behaviorism, overdetermined behavior, fear module, backward masking, behavioral immune system, specific language impairment, proximate vs. ultimate explanation

**Notable studies / people**
- Petrinovich & Bolles (1954): food versus water reinforcement in maze position and alternation tasks (p. 422).
- Breland & Breland (1961): "The Misbehavior of Organisms" (p. 425).
- Brown & Jenkins (1968), Williams & Williams (1969), Jenkins & Moore (1973): autoshaping (pp. 426–427).
- Garcia & Koelling (1966); Wilcoxon, Dragoin & Kral (1971) (pp. 428–429).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book says it has no specific teaching techniques; these are mostly own inference)
- The book states that curricula should match developmental readiness, make use of prepared abilities like language, and deliberately counter natural biases like prejudice or aggression through school activities (p. 441).
- Design tasks that fit natural response tendencies of learners (use familiar formats and modes, such as spoken language for language learning); poor task fit can masquerade as lack of ability or motivation, a parallel to the book's point that required response choice governs learning (own inference from p. 424).
- Be alert to instruction that arouses strong aversions or fear (taste-aversion and phobia research show rapid, durable learning of danger associations), so avoid pairing material with illness, shame or threat (own inference from pp. 428–430, 433).

## Ch 16 — A Final Word (pp. 449–458)

**Core ideas**
- Recent trends: theories are narrower (specific domains such as causal learning or fear learning) than the grand systems of Thorndike, Pavlov, Skinner and Hull; neuroimaging makes everyone feel like a neuroscientist, creating an illusion that complex functions sit in one or two brain places, although every region takes part in many processes (pp. 449–450).
- Neuroscience in education has been over-interpreted. The left-brain/right-brain notion misread split-brain research: hemispheres specialize in degree, and a normal person has one integrated brain, yet right-brain/left-brain curricula and everyday talk persist despite contrary data (pp. 450–451).
- The twelve assumptions of "brain-based" education (Caine and Caine) mostly restate earlier learning theory (parallel processing, search for meaning, patterning, emotion, challenge versus threat linked to Hebb's arousal ideas), so the authors call them theory-based assumptions, and say they would improve teaching but are neither new nor revolutionary (pp. 451–452).
- Learning styles: Pashler and colleagues found about 71 style-assessment schemes, separated study preferences from the learning style hypothesis (that teaching matched to style yields better outcomes), and required controlled studies showing a crossover interaction; they found only one possibly qualifying study and weak evidence. Rogowsky and colleagues found no benefit from matching instruction to preferred modality and warned of disservice to learners accommodated in their preference instead of strengthening weaker skills. Pashler et al. urge basing teaching on research, not intuition (pp. 452–454).
- Interest in applying learning principles to practical problems (education, therapy, institutions, public health, climate attitudes) is growing (p. 454).
- Open questions: how learning changes with maturation; how many kinds of learning exist (some may be all-or-none and some incremental, with interactions such as anxiety effects reversing by type of learning); and what reinforcement really is (incentive, information, or something else), which should be rethought, with learning generally continuous and reinforcement mainly a performance variable except in classical conditioning. The field has grown more differentiated and has no final answers; multiple approaches are needed (pp. 455–457).

**Key terms**: less ambitious theories, neuroimaging illusion, left-brain/right-brain myth, brain-based education, learning styles, study preferences, meshing hypothesis, crossover interaction, interaction, maturation and learning, kinds of learning, reinforcement reconsidered, differentiation of the field

**Notable studies / people**
- Levy (1985) and Beaton (1985): corrections to the two-brain myth (pp. 450–451).
- Caine & Caine (1990, 1994, 1997): brain-based learning assumptions (pp. 451–452).
- Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2009): review finding little evidence for learning styles (pp. 453–454).
- Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal (2015): test of the meshing hypothesis (p. 454).
- Uttal (2011): brain regions participate in many cognitive processes (p. 450).

**Use for teaching and studying** (the book's implications first, plus own inference)
- The book states that learning-style matching is unsupported and that research, not intuition, should guide teaching; do not design separate study materials for "visual" versus "auditory" learners, and instead use the modality that suits the content (pp. 453–454).
- Treat claims phrased as "brain-based" or "left-brain/right-brain" with caution; check whether they restate older learning principles or have controlled evidence (pp. 450–452).
- Because reinforcement's role and the kinds of learning remain unsettled, choose methods according to the type of learning at hand (skill, concept, attitude) and test them (own inference from pp. 455–457).

## Cross-cutting takeaways for teaching and learning

Ranked roughly by how directly they bear on designing effective teaching and study materials.

1. **Test yourself and your students often, with quick feedback.** The book reports that frequent testing improves learning and retention from grade school through college, that frequent testing paired with rapid feedback is especially effective, and that retrieval practice works even without feedback and in memory-impaired patients; because learners rarely self-test, instructors should build it in (pp. 75, 144). Successive relearning to mastery over several sessions, combined with retesting, further boosts long-term retention and transfer (p. 230).
2. **Space practice rather than massing it.** Distributed practice beat massed practice across 317 experiments, and a rest can even raise performance (reminiscence effect), though Hull's fatigue explanation is no longer accepted (pp. 133–134). A Hullian classroom would distribute practice and use spaced testing (p. 144).
3. **Prefer active, student-centered work over passive reception.** Thorndike distrusted lecturing ("telling is not teaching"), and the book states that research since has favored active approaches such as collaborative and problem-based learning (p. 74). Piaget, Tolman, and the Gestaltists likewise stress hypothesis testing and discussion (pp. 285–288, 308, 336–337).
4. **Pair activity with guidance.** Unaided discovery lost to direct instruction in elementary science studies, and guided discovery appears particularly effective (p. 310); Skinner's programmed instruction also uses small steps, overt responses, immediate feedback and self-pacing, with supportive evidence from programmed learning, Keller's PSI mastery courses, and computer-based instruction (pp. 116, 118–121).
5. **Aim for understanding, not rote memory.** Wertheimer and Katona showed that grasping a principle or pattern gives durable, flexible knowledge, whereas formula-only learners fail on unfamiliar problems (pp. 280–285). Meaningful material is also learned faster and retained better (Ebbinghaus, p. 44).
6. **Practice in the form and context where the knowledge will be used.** Transfer depends on shared elements (Thorndike), so teach realistic tasks and practice under test-like conditions, including studying where you will be tested (pp. 68–70, 75, 227–228, 231). Do not rely on "mental discipline" arguments for hard subjects (pp. 42, 68–69).
7. **Do not mistake performance for learning.** Learning is potential that may show up later; reinforcement and incentives often govern performance rather than learning (pp. 6, 321–322, 349). Assess with delayed and transfer tasks, not only immediate displays.
8. **Give feedback that is quick, informative, and correct errors early.** Reinforce correct responses promptly and correct mistakes before they are practiced (p. 75). Reinforcement is best treated as information about what works (pp. 256–257, 350, 456), and rewards can be misapplied, as when calling on the loudest hands rewards loudness (p. 87).
9. **Avoid punishment as a main tool; use positive control and extinction.** Thorndike's revision and Skinner's analysis found punishment suppresses without eliminating behavior (pp. 71, 97–99); alternatives include changing the situation, reinforcing incompatible behavior, and ignoring unwanted behavior (p. 100). Guthrie adds that any corrective consequence must produce an incompatible response in the presence of the original cue (pp. 223–225), and the book notes the evidence on punishment is complex (p. 226).
10. **Model skills and strategies; build self-efficacy.** Observational learning needs no reinforcement or imitation; models with credibility and competence are attended to, and abstract modeling across varied examples teaches general rules (pp. 350–351, 359, 368). Goals of moderate difficulty and success experiences build self-efficacy, which predicts effort and persistence (pp. 354–355, 369).
11. **Pitch material between the known and the unknown.** Material that cannot be assimilated is meaningless and fully assimilated material teaches nothing, so use mild challenge and diagnose the learner's current level (pp. 304, 307–308). Gestalt teachers similarly start from the familiar and use meaningful units (p. 286).
12. **Mind emotional associations and arousal.** Classroom pairings of subjects with anxiety or humiliation can create lasting aversions (pp. 203–204), moderate anxiety may help while excess disrupts (p. 144), and optimal arousal depends on the task (pp. 384–385, 407).
13. **Exploit rich early environments and plasticity.** Enriched sensory experience supports later problem solving (pp. 380–382, 406), and the brain stays plastic throughout adulthood, though stress reduces plasticity (pp. 397–401).
14. **Distrust popular shortcuts: learning styles, left/right-brain, "brain-based" claims.** Controlled studies found no benefit from matching instruction to preferred modality (pp. 453–454), the two-brain idea misreads split-brain research (pp. 450–451), and "brain-based" principles mostly restate earlier theory (pp. 451–452).
15. **Treat theories as tools and decide with evidence.** Good theories are testable and revisable (pp. 19, 27); measure learning with more than one outcome measure (pp. 22–23), and expect different kinds of learning to need different methods (pp. 455–457).
