Moses 6:55 explicitly describes children as conceived in sin; Mosiah 3:16 describes their fall through Adam and Christ's atoning blood in relation to their sins. These are exactly the tensions the claims notes require the response to address. Name the language briefly, restore its neighbouring context, and keep the synthesis identified as the presenter's reading. Suggested shape: Moses uses the words "conceived in sin," and Mosiah speaks about little children falling through Adam; those same passages reject inherited parental guilt and call children blessed through Christ. This need not settle a theological dispute, but it should let viewers hear the difficulty rather than only be told it exists.

## Claim 1: developmental inappropriateness and potential emotional abuse

**Strongest surviving objection:** Canonical innocence does not establish safety in practice. A child can be officially innocent while experiencing a suffering-for-you story as personal responsibility or emotional obligation; eight-year-olds remain children. The script now recognizes both limits rather than offering Moroni as empirical proof. Its final challenge to a broader judgment is defensible only if it preserves the critic's qualified language and does not imply she said all Atonement teaching is abusive.

**Omitted context:** The 0:12–0:24 framing makes the issue fear, shame, guilt, and manipulation; 1:02–1:20 adds the child's model of love as a debt. The opening handles the first issue, but the debt issue is only indirect. Apply finding 2. Her 1:20–1:27 constructive alternative is played and accepted, which improves fairness.

## Claim 2: young children's capacity for theology

**Strongest surviving objection:** A simple loving explanation does not demonstrate that young children can safely integrate an abstract sacrificial-salvation account, and a doctrinal accountability threshold cannot answer a developmental question. The script makes neither claim. It concedes the need for comprehensible teaching and gives a dated curriculum example rather than pretending no young children hear about suffering.

**Omitted context:** The developmental statement supports the later concrete inference that a child's wrongdoing caused suffering. The selected next clip and response retain that connection. The quoted curriculum also directs teachers to read D&C 19:16 to younger children and ask why Jesus suffered; this strengthens the concern, but the existing heading citation already concedes the essential causal connection. No additional omission is necessary to repair beyond findings 1–2.

## Claim 3: shame, repentance, obedience, and worthiness

**Strongest surviving objection:** Adult intent and anti-shame reading do not establish actual childhood experience. Official language about sins, repentance, and suffering can generate the criticized inference without a teacher explicitly telling children they are bad. The script accepts the possibility and calls for changed teaching. Its remaining weakness is the vague counter-text acknowledgment, repaired by finding 1.

**Omitted context:** The critic says children may internalize the message and then connects it with behavior shaping; the script preserves may, accepts the possibility, and does not turn her psychological prediction into a claim about written doctrine. The adjacent debt question deserves explicit acknowledgment under finding 2.
