# Reliance on feelings to establish historical claims

## General response

The objection: a subjective spiritual confirmation (Moroni 10:4-5) is
used to settle what are ultimately empirical, historical questions (did
ancient peoples migrate to the Americas and produce the Book of Mormon;
did Joseph Smith translate an actual ancient Egyptian text) — a category
error, since feelings aren't the right kind of evidence for historical
claims regardless of how strong or genuine the feeling is. Take this
category distinction seriously rather than blurring it.

## Catholic and Protestant response

Both traditions generally keep this distinction cleaner than the
objection assumes LDS practice does — historical claims about Jesus
(resurrection, empty tomb) are argued evidentially/historically in
apologetics (e.g., minimal-facts arguments), with spiritual confirmation
treated as separate from, not a substitute for, that evidential case.
Hold LDS apologetics to the same standard: historical claims
(Book of Mormon historicity, Joseph Smith's translation claims) should be
argued on historical evidence, with spiritual witness offered as
additional, not primary or substitute, support.

## Exmo/atheist response

Concede the category point directly rather than defending feelings as
historical evidence: a spiritual witness cannot by itself establish that
ancient Nephites existed, any more than a Christian's feeling that the
Bible is true establishes the historicity of specific Old Testament
events. The defensible position separates the questions — historical
claims get evaluated by historical method (see the Book of Mormon
historicity cluster of files), and spiritual confirmation, whatever its
epistemic status (`circular-testimony-epistemology.md`), answers a
different question (is this project worth engaging with as a spiritual
practice, does it produce good fruit) than "did this specific historical
event occur." Keep those two questions visibly separate on screen rather
than letting spiritual confirmation do double duty as historical
evidence.
