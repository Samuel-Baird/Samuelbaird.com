# "Doubt your doubts" / discouragement of skepticism

## General response

The specific phrase (popularized by Dieter F. Uchtdorf's October 2013
General Conference talk, though the sentiment predates that address) is
often read by critics as instructing members to distrust their own
critical faculties whenever they conflict with faith claims. Quote the
actual context rather than the soundbite alone — Uchtdorf's talk pairs
"doubt your doubts" with explicit acknowledgment that the Church's
history includes real, serious difficulties and that asking questions is
legitimate, which is regularly left out when the phrase is cited
critically.

## Catholic and Protestant response

Most religious traditions have some version of counseling perseverance
through doubt rather than immediate departure (compare Hebrews 10:23,
"let us hold fast the profession of our faith without wavering," or the
broader Christian tradition of encouraging faith through periods of
"dark night of the soul"-type doubt, per John of the Cross) — name this
shared pastoral impulse honestly while still engaging the specific
critique below.

## Exmo/atheist response

Take the critique seriously rather than only citing fuller context: even
in its fuller form, instructing believers to distrust doubt as such
(rather than evaluating doubt on its merits, case by case) risks
functioning as an unfalsifiability mechanism (see
`unfalsifiability-of-truth-claims.md`) and can discourage legitimate
critical engagement with real historical evidence. The more defensible
version of the pastoral advice distinguishes emotional
discouragement/despair (which perseverance-through-doubt language is
usually actually addressing) from evidential doubt about specific factual
claims (which deserves direct evidential engagement, not dismissal) —
this distinction is worth making explicit on screen since the original
talks rarely make it as sharply as this response requires.
