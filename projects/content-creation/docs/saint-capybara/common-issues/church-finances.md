# Church finances

## General response

The Church does not publish detailed financial statements (unlike its
practice before 1959). A 2019 whistleblower complaint (former Ensign
Peak Advisors employee David Nielsen) alleged the Church's investment
arm held approximately $100 billion in reserve funds, raising questions
about tithing use versus charitable/humanitarian spending scale. State
the documented facts (the whistleblower complaint's existence and
central figures, and the Church's general non-disclosure practice) before
any interpretive framing.

## Catholic and Protestant response

Both the Catholic Church (Vatican finances) and various large
Protestant denominations/megachurches have faced their own financial
transparency controversies — this is not evidence the LDS case is less
serious, but it does show the underlying tension (large religious
institutions balancing financial prudence, ecclesiastical autonomy, and
donor transparency expectations) is a general one across well-resourced
religious institutions, not unique to LDS practice.

## Exmo/atheist response

Give the direct, current state of the public record:

- The 2019 whistleblower complaint to the IRS alleged Ensign Peak
  Advisors accumulated roughly $100 billion in reserves funded by
  tithing, invested rather than spent on charitable causes, potentially
  in tension with the entity's tax-exempt status requirements.
- The Church's public response affirmed the funds exist as a reserve
  against future needs (a "rainy day fund" framing) and that tithing
  supports current operations and increasing global humanitarian and
  temple-building spending, while not disputing the scale of the
  reserve fund itself.
- A subsequent SEC settlement (2023) found Ensign Peak had used shell
  companies to obscure its holdings from required public disclosure over
  more than 20 years, resulting in a financial penalty — a documented,
  legally-adjudicated finding, not merely an allegation, and should be
  cited as such directly.

**Certainty level:** the scale of the reserve fund and the SEC's
disclosure-violation finding are `fact`; whether the overall financial
approach is prudent stewardship or a departure from tithing's intended
purpose is a `disputed` value judgment that reasonable people, including
faithful members, disagree about — present both without resolving it by
assertion.

## Added 2026-09-12 — the affirmative case for why a reserve this size makes sense

These are separate from, and don't soften, the `fact`-grade concealment
finding above (the SEC settlement stands on its own and gets no
reframing — see `research-protocol.md` sec 7's "true and damaging"
category). This section is the positive argument for *why the Church
holds a large reserve at all*, which is a different question from
whether it should have disclosed the reserve's size honestly.

- **Index-fund normalization.** Both Palantir and TKO are S&P 500
  constituents. Anyone holding a broad U.S. index fund — a target-date
  retirement fund, a 401(k) default option, a total-market ETF — already
  owns a slice of both, in roughly the same proportion the index assigns
  them. Ensign Peak's Palantir position sits close to Palantir's own
  index weight (see the `pltr-sp500-weight-slickcharts` source already
  used in `reviews/church-corporate-landlord`), which is the signature of
  broad market exposure, not a bespoke bet. This doesn't make the
  weapons-maker screening gap (documented above and in the script) go
  away — it's a separate point about two specific, frequently-named
  holdings, not a defense of the whole portfolio's composition.
- **Endowment logic, not business logic.** A university endowment and a
  for-profit company are structured around different goals: a company
  distributes profit to owners, an endowment exists to fund an
  institution's mission across time horizons longer than any single
  budget year. Ensign Peak has no outside shareholders — nothing it holds
  is profit belonging to a private individual, which is the actual legal
  test for non-profit status (`irs-501c3-exemption-requirements`), not
  the reserve's size.
- **Why a global, still-growing church needs a reserve, specifically
  now.** Church membership growth has slowed markedly in the U.S. and
  Europe — falling birth rates and an aging membership base mean fewer
  new members are being born into or joining the Church there than a
  generation ago. Meanwhile, membership and convert baptisms are growing
  fastest in parts of Africa, Latin America, and Asia — regions where the
  Church is starting from a much smaller existing base of local
  meetinghouses, temples, and trained local leadership infrastructure.
  Building that infrastructure costs money up front, well before local
  tithing revenue in a newly-growing area could cover it. **This claim
  needs a citable source before use on screen** — the Church's own annual
  general-conference statistical report breaks out worldwide membership
  and unit growth by area and would be the right primary source; it
  hasn't been pulled and verified for this project yet, so treat this
  bullet as a research lead, not a sourced claim.
- **Not all Church-owned real estate is investment property.**
  Meetinghouses, temples, and welfare/humanitarian facilities are
  operational property — built and run at a cost to serve members and
  communities, not to generate a return. The investment side (commercial
  real estate, farmland, residential property managed by Property
  Reserve Inc./Deseret Management Corporation — a distinct entity from
  Ensign Peak, don't conflate the two on screen) is a separate slice of
  holdings, managed for the same long-horizon reserve-building purpose as
  the stock portfolio. **Also needs a citable source** for the specific
  operational/investment breakdown before it can be used as a factual
  claim rather than a general structural point.
- **Per-member math as a rhetorical check, not a claimed total.** Even a
  deliberately generous estimate — say $300 billion, well above any
  figure actually disclosed by the Church or alleged in the 2019
  whistleblower complaint (~$100B) or the SEC settlement record — divided
  across the Church's approximately 17 million members works out to
  roughly $17,000-18,000 per member. Useful for showing that even an
  inflated headline number isn't obviously excessive once divided across
  a global membership base this size, **but state the actual
  hypothetical framing on screen** ("even if we imagine a much bigger
  number than anything disclosed...") rather than presenting $300B as a
  real, sourced total — it isn't one. The exact current membership figure
  needs to be pulled from the Church's own published statistics before
  Gate A rather than assumed at "18 million."
- **The reserve also isn't only for members.** Church humanitarian aid
  (disaster relief, refugee assistance, clean water projects) goes to
  non-members as a matter of stated policy, not exception. A large
  reserve serving as a hedge against a future large-scale need — a
  regional disaster, a global economic downturn — is consistent with
  that stated humanitarian mission, not just with building meetinghouses
  for members. This is a values-based argument (why keeping a large
  reserve is a reasonable thing to do) rather than a factual dispute, and
  should be presented as such — it doesn't resolve whether the specific
  historical concealment was justified, which it wasn't (see above).
