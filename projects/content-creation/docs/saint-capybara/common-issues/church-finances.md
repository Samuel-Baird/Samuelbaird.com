# Church finances

## General response

The Church does not publish detailed financial statements (unlike its
practice before 1959). A 2019 whistleblower complaint (former Ensign
Peak Advisors employee David Nielsen) alleged the Church's investment
arm held approximately $100 billion in reserve funds, raising questions
about tithing use versus charitable/humanitarian spending scale. State
the documented facts (the whistleblower complaint's existence and
central figures, and the Church's general non-disclosure practice) before
any interpretive framing.

## Catholic and Protestant response

Both the Catholic Church (Vatican finances) and various large
Protestant denominations/megachurches have faced their own financial
transparency controversies — this is not evidence the LDS case is less
serious, but it does show the underlying tension (large religious
institutions balancing financial prudence, ecclesiastical autonomy, and
donor transparency expectations) is a general one across well-resourced
religious institutions, not unique to LDS practice.

## Exmo/atheist response

Give the direct, current state of the public record:

- The 2019 whistleblower complaint to the IRS alleged Ensign Peak
  Advisors accumulated roughly $100 billion in reserves funded by
  tithing, invested rather than spent on charitable causes, potentially
  in tension with the entity's tax-exempt status requirements.
- The Church's public response affirmed the funds exist as a reserve
  against future needs (a "rainy day fund" framing) and that tithing
  supports current operations and increasing global humanitarian and
  temple-building spending, while not disputing the scale of the
  reserve fund itself.
- A subsequent SEC settlement (2023) found Ensign Peak had used shell
  companies to obscure its holdings from required public disclosure over
  more than 20 years, resulting in a financial penalty — a documented,
  legally-adjudicated finding, not merely an allegation, and should be
  cited as such directly.

**Certainty level:** the scale of the reserve fund and the SEC's
disclosure-violation finding are `fact`; whether the overall financial
approach is prudent stewardship or a departure from tithing's intended
purpose is a `disputed` value judgment that reasonable people, including
faithful members, disagree about — present both without resolving it by
assertion.
