# Blood atonement rhetoric

## General response

Brigham Young and other 19th-century leaders used rhetoric describing
certain grievous sins as requiring the sinner's own blood to be shed for
atonement to be possible — "there are sins that [blood atonement
applies to]" is documented directly in period sermons
([source](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/v12n01-a03f/13)). State plainly
that this rhetoric existed and was repeated by top Church leadership,
rather than denying or minimizing it.

## Catholic and Protestant response

The relevant theological question: is inflammatory, punitive rhetoric
from a 19th-century frontier religious and social context evidence of
doctrine, or of a specific historical-cultural register that doesn't
reflect settled theology even when used by leaders? Engage this
directly — most Christian traditions have their own history of harsh,
period-specific rhetoric (e.g., Reformation-era and colonial-era
execution and persecution rhetoric within various Protestant and
Catholic contexts) that isn't treated as binding doctrine today, which
is a fair and relevant parallel, not a deflection.

## Exmo/atheist response

Give the historically precise picture: scholarly debate exists over how
much blood atonement rhetoric reflected actual sanctioned practice versus
harsh rhetorical/deterrent language never systematically carried out —
BYU Studies notes "even assuming that [historian Will] Bagley is right
and that Brigham Young and others believed in blood
atonement" [the practical implementation remains
disputed](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/42-1-45d4/169), while other
scholarship states blood atonement "was fully developed by Brigham
Young" as taught doctrine at the time
([source](https://corpus.noahairmet.com/b/v12n01-a03f/13)). Present both
sides of this specific historiographical debate rather than picking the
more comfortable one — whether the rhetoric ever translated into
sanctioned violent practice (as opposed to remaining severe preached
doctrine) is a genuinely contested historical question, distinct from
whether the rhetoric itself existed, which is not contested.
