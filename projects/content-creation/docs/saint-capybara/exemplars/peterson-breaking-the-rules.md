## The passage

Peterson opens with a direct address to a shared, recognizable experience — asking
how many times a reader has tried to explain their own faith's doctrines, only to be
told flatly, "that is not what you believe" — before naming Krister Stendahl's three
rules for understanding another religion honestly (learn from adherents, not
opponents; don't compare your ideal to their worst practice; leave room for "holy
envy"). The essay then works through specific critics who violate each rule, closing
each example with a short, dry aside rather than an editorial paragraph. The closing
line after the last example: "Case closed. But is it?" — a one-sentence turn that
reopens the question instead of declaring victory.

## Take

- **Open on the audience's own experience of the double standard**, not on a defense.
  The first sentence names something the viewer has already felt, before any facts
  arrive.
- **A jab as a two-word rhetorical question, not a paragraph.** "Case closed. But is
  it?" does the work of an entire counter-argument in four words, placed at the very
  end of an example, not the essay.

## Do NOT take

- The essay uses real critics' names throughout with page-length quotation and
  rebuttal; Saint Capybara's clip-response format already handles attribution
  differently (a clip beat, not a block quote) — don't import the essay-length
  cataloging approach into a 60-180s Short.

## Beat translation

Opening (general audience, any "you're not really Christian/not really following the
Bible" claim): *"Ever notice how the same critics who'd never let you define their
faith are perfectly happy defining ours?"*

Closing jab, modeled on "Case closed. But is it?": *"Case closed, they'll say. Except
it isn't — it never was."*
