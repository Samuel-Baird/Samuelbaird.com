The Mormon chieftain has lived out the allotted life of man, his age being seventy-six years. For some time back infirmity has borne heavily upon him, and it has been supposed that he must succumb to some one of the constantly recurring attacks. He was a man of fine physique, with robust constitution, and three years ago Dr. Bernhisel expressed the belief that, if acute disease should not carry him off, he had stamina enough to last him ten or twenty years. He has paid scrupulous regard to his health, and for many years past has been cared for by his numerous wives as tenderly as a child. But the fatal attack came at last, and Brigham Young has passed away with all his inspiration and all his influence of grace.

He has led the Mormon Church thirty-three years. He was baptized into the Latter-day faith in 1832, and the following April he had his first meeting with the Prophet Joseph Smith. During the visit of this zealous disciple to the founder of the faith at Kirtland, the former "spake in tongues," and this was the first time the gift had been demonstrated. In later life he discouraged such exhibitions, which shows his sense of the utter worthlessness of such idle profession. On the organization of the Church in Kirtland, May 7th, 1834, it numbered one hundred and thirty men, Hyrum Smith, Brigham Young, Heber C. Kimball, George A. Smith, Orson Hyde, Parley P. Pratt and Wilford Woodruff being regarded as its leading elders. The following February the "Twelve" were appointed, Brigham Young being second of the quorum.

According to Stenhouse, Joseph Smith was impressed with Brigham Young's active qualities, for it is said that when he first beheld this disciple he predicted that the time would come when Brother Brigham would preside over the Church. It is also said that the Prophet, just before his death, made the further prediction that "if Brigham Young ever becomes President of the Church, he will lead it to h—." Whatever his opinion of Brigham, there is no question that the Prophet found him a very zealous servant, having sufficient of the courtier in his composition to indulge his leader's right to do as he pleased, and never once finding fault with his personal rule. On the death of Joseph Smith at Carthage jail, June 27th, 1844, the succession to the vacant prophetship lay between Sidney Rigdon and Brigham Young. The former had been the Boanerges of the new faith, and had rendered it important aid, but he was too visionary to deal with his ambitious and strong-willed antagonist. He had seen Joseph revel in dreams and revelations, and witnessed their astonishing effects upon the minds of the bewildered Saints. To step into the deceased Prophet's shoes, he supposed he was required to do something like him. Hence he entered upon the shadowy realm of revelation. He had nightly visions about Gog and Magog, and saw wonderful things which were about to take place. But Brigham was a more practical minded ecclesiast. Joseph was a natural born seer and occupied a pedestal of his own; but Brigham brought a different spirit to the work. "I am not a prophet, or revelator, as Joseph was," his successor would remark, and he posed his function in another way. "He saw before him," says one annalist of the Church, "a multitude of people who had been gathered by revelation, and who had fed upon it daily. There was but one thing that could be done — make them work out an idea, 'Build up the kingdom of Joseph; build up to Joseph!' 'He is looking down upon us, and is with us as much as before.'" Thus, people labored for Joseph, and Brigham controlled and garnered the results for himself.

The new chieftain was a shrewd Yankee; born in Vermont, totally illiterate, and a glazier by trade. He was upwards of thirty when he joined the Church, and his experience in life had been a rugged one. He was the last man in the Latter-day sect that would have been chosen for a Divine oracle, being entirely lacking in the religious element, and materialistic in his habit of mind. But he was made of the stern stuff that is needed for ambition, and was endued with that knowledge of men which enabled him to use them to his own ends. "The past style of doing business was to be changed," says Stenhouse; "the loose ends were to be tied up, and everything was to be put upon a strictly commercial basis."

After the death of Joseph Smith (as now) great interest was felt as to the successorship. On the 8th of August a special conference was held at Nauvoo, where it was decided, without a dissenting voice, that the Twelve should administer the affairs of the Church, Brigham Young being president of the quorum. And the rule was maintained nominally by this body, although Brigham was the controlling spirit. The fall of '46 found the Saints in Winter Quarters, preparing for an exodus to Salt Lake. The following spring they started, Orson Pratt leading a detachment, and Brigham following with the main body. The settlement having been made, the pioneers returned to the Missouri river to escort their families to the new Jerusalem. And while journeying backward over the plains, we are told, the leader of the faith first conceived the idea of restoring the First Presidency. Arriving at Winter Quarters, he made known his plan to a few trusty friends, and by a masterly resort to cajolery where it would serve, and intimidation where it was needed, he silenced all opposition, and on December 6th, 1847, at a council of the people, succeeded in procuring his election to the Presidency, with Heber C. Kimball and Franklin D. Richards as counsellors. This was no other than an act of usurpation, but it was history repeating itself. Cromwell, a man of similarly iron will, emerged from his fellow zealots as Lord Protector, and First Consul Bonaparte found slight difficulty in founding an empire.

Nearly thirty years have elapsed since this consummation of his ambitious plan, and Brigham Young has ruled over the Church with unquestioned sway, exercising a despotism nowhere equaled except in barbarous Turkey. In 1850, Utah was organized into a Territory of the United States, made subject to the laws of the country, and officers appointed to administer its affairs. But Brigham, by the system of terror he adopted, by his arrogant assumption of Divine power, and his unscrupulous use of the ignorance and credulity of his followers, was not only able to still all dissensions within the fold, but has actually succeeded in preventing the execution of the laws, and until the day of his death was de facto Governor.

It is an easy matter to criticize the manner in which he has performed his great work, and to depreciate his personal qualities. As we have said above, he was illiterate, and has made a frequent boast that he never saw the inside of a school house. His habit of mind was singularly illogical, and his public addresses are the greatest farrago of nonsense that ever was put in print. He prided himself on being a great financier, and yet all his commercial speculations have been conspicuous failures. He was a hierophant, and pretended to be in daily intercourse with the Almighty, and yet he was groveling in his ideas, and the system of religion he formulated was well nigh Satanic. Yet with this grossness, this illiteracy, this entire ignorance of the arts of government, he has succeeded, during an entire generation, in holding absolute sway over a hundred thousand followers, in driving and controlling their every act, and so dominating their confidence and affections as to stand to them as a very Deity. His death is regarded as the fall of a great man in Israel, and thousands mourn his loss as a personal bereavement.

Yet we believe that the most graceful act of his life has been his death. While Brigham lived and exercised his despotic sway there could be no material progress in Israel. Because he taught his fanatical followers to regard the citizens of the United States as their enemies, and the evangel he preached was one of implacable hate. But now this man has gone, his ferocious rule will die with him. No other man can hope to gain the power that he possessed. His ambition was to found a dynasty, but his two sons lack the qualities to command the confidence and respect of the people, and the light of the world has too far permeated this hitherto darkened land for another Prophet to arise in Israel. "The Twelve" will be quite apt to assume the directory, and administer the affairs of the Church as in its early days in Nauvoo. But dissensions will arise. John Taylor and George Q. Cannon will strive after the dictatorship, and in this ambitious attempt will certainly come into conflict with the sons of the deceased ruler, and most likely with other pretenders from the quorum. Great scandal will come to the Church, and during the struggle for the mastery, thousands will shake off the yoke. It has long been apparent that the defrauded followers of the priesthood have grown restive under the heavy exactions imposed upon them, and the greedy struggle of their leaders for the spoils of office will break up the spell that has so long lulled their senses into inaction, and when disintegration once takes on an active shape, the whole decaying structure will rapidly fall to pieces. If the death of Brigham Young shall be supplemented this fall by an act of Congress giving the people of Utah a free ballot and an amended jury law, the extirpation of priestly tyranny will be complete, and Utah will be Americanized and politically and socially redeemed.
