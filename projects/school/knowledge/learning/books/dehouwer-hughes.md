# De Houwer & Hughes — The Psychology of Learning (knowledge digest)

**Citation:** Jan De Houwer and Sean Hughes, *The Psychology of Learning: An Introduction from a Functional-Cognitive Perspective*, MIT Press, 2020 (page numbers below are the book's printed pages).

**Orientation:** The authors define learning as an effect (behavior change caused by environmental regularities) and keep two separate levels of explanation apart throughout: functional knowledge (which environmental conditions change behavior) and cognitive/mental-process theories (what hidden information-processing steps might mediate that change). Each chapter sets out the functional findings first, then the mental-process theories that try to account for them.

---

## Ch 0 — Introduction: What Is Learning and How Can We Study It (pp. 1–36)

**Core ideas**
- Learning is treated as ontogenetic adaptation: a change in the behavior of an individual organism, during its lifetime, that is caused by a regularity in its environment. Both an observable behavior change and a causal link to the environment are required (p. 4).
- "Behavior" is defined broadly: muscle actions, autonomic responses, brain activity and private events such as thoughts all count (pp. 4–5). Because causes cannot be seen directly, calling a change "learning" is always a hypothesis; the infant grip reflex that fades could be learning or maturation, and only manipulating the environment can tell them apart (p. 5).
- A regularity is anything beyond a single stimulus or response at one moment. Research has concentrated on three kinds: a single stimulus (noncontingent presentations, e.g. habituation), several stimuli (classical conditioning), and stimuli plus behavior (operant conditioning) (pp. 6–9). "Complex learning" is change produced jointly by several regularities, split into moderated learning (standard regularities combined) and metaregularities (a regularity as an element of another) (p. 9).
- A procedure (what the experimenter arranges) is not an effect (a causal claim), and neither is a mental process. Seeing a pairing procedure does not prove conditioning occurred; other regularities may be doing the work, so controls are needed (pp. 11–12, 31).
- Functional approach: study five groups of moderators (stimuli/behaviors in the regularity, the tested behavior, the organism, the broader context, features of the regularity) and abstract away from surface detail to principles defined by function, such as CS/US/CR or reinforcer, so that behavior can be predicted and influenced (pp. 13–18).
- Cognitive approach: explain how learning happens by positing mental mechanisms that mediate between regularity and behavior. Such mechanisms are never directly observable, producing an inference problem (pp. 20–22).
- The two approaches play different "games" with different things to explain and different explanations (Table 0.1, p. 27). They can help each other: functional findings test mental theories, and mental theories predict new functional findings (pp. 27–28).
- The authors warn against defining an effect by its proposed mechanism (e.g. equating conditioning with association formation), because a refuted theory would then seem to erase the phenomenon itself (box 0.4, p. 31). Latent learning is, for them, only a gap in time between experience and behavior change, not proof of mental representations for functionalists (box 0.2, p. 23).

**Key terms:** learning (as effect), ontogenetic vs phylogenetic adaptation, regularity, metaregularity, noncontingent stimulus presentation, classical conditioning, operant conditioning, complex learning, moderated learning, procedure vs effect vs mental process, functional approach, cognitive approach, moderator vs mediator, abstract functional knowledge, analytic-abstractive level, effect-centric level, explanandum/explanans, radical vs methodological behaviorism, latent learning

**Notable studies / people**
- Pavlov (1927): bell–food pairings raise salivation; the example used to show abstraction to CS/US/CR (pp. 16–17).
- Skinner (1938, 1953): lever pressing for food; radical behaviorism treats thoughts as covert behavior and is the closest relative of the book's functional stance (pp. 9, 25).
- Tolman & Honzik (1930): rats explored a maze, later found food quickly; used to contrast functional and cognitive readings of latent learning (p. 23).
- Seidel (1959): sensory preconditioning, the opening example of moderated learning (p. 9).
- Weaver et al. (2004): maternal licking alters rats' later gene activity under stress, offered as "genetic learning" (p. 15).

**Use for teaching and studying (own inference)**
- Define learning outcomes as observable changes in what a learner does under specified conditions, and test by comparing conditions, not by assuming a lesson caused the change (inferred from pp. 5, 12).
- Describe study and teaching techniques by their function (what each does to performance) rather than by label or by a theory of why it works (inferred from pp. 17–18, 31).
- Keep "what works" (functional evidence) distinct from "why it works" (mental-process theory) in study materials, so advice survives if a theory is revised (inferred from pp. 27–31).

## Ch 1 — Effects of Regularities in the Presence of a Single Stimulus (pp. 37–56)

**Core ideas**
- Habituation is a decline in response intensity with repeated presentation of one stimulus; sensitization is the opposite, a growing response. Habituation is an effect, so saying a response shrank "because of habituation" is circular (pp. 37–38, 40).
- The speed of habituation depends on the stimulus: affective (pleasant or unpleasant) images habituate skin-conductance responses more slowly than neutral ones, and biologically significant sounds produce less durable, more context-specific habituation (pp. 39–40). Very intense stimuli can produce sensitization instead, and habituation generalizes to similar stimuli (p. 40).
- Different components of the orienting response (head turn, skin conductance, heart-rate slowing, EEG) habituate at different rates, and stimulus type interacts with component, so the orienting response may not be one unitary reaction (p. 41).
- Mere exposure and Fechner's law: repeated exposure first raises liking and, with continued repetition, can turn to dislike (p. 40).
- Opponent reactions: with repeated stimulation the primary reaction weakens while the counter-reaction strengthens (drug "high" shrinks and withdrawal grows; jogging discomfort shrinks and the afterglow grows) (pp. 41–42).
- Dishabituation (a different stimulus restoring a habituated response) shows the response is still in the repertoire, so the decline was not mere fatigue or loss of ability; it also underlies variety in eating, as pleasure from one food returns after a bite of another (pp. 47–49).
- Habituation has been shown in humans, spiders, slime mould and Mimosa plants, so learning as defined here does not need a brain or neural connections (box 1.2, pp. 44–48).
- Massed presentations habituate quickly but the effect fades fast; distributed presentations habituate more slowly but last longer (p. 49). An attention-away instruction speeds habituation (p. 42).
- Mental-process theories: Sokolov's model (the organism builds an internal model of the environment and responds only to mismatch) and Bradley's refinement (responses driven by novelty and by significance, where novelty fades quickly and significance slowly) explain stimulus and component differences better (pp. 50–52). Solomon's opponent-process theory posits a stable a-process and a b-process that grows, comes sooner and lasts longer with repetition; context stimuli can trigger the b-process, which accounts for craving, relapse in drug-linked settings and context-dependent tolerance (pp. 53–56).

**Key terms:** habituation, sensitization, dishabituation, orientation response, behavioral repertoire, mere exposure, dynamics of affect, massed vs distributed practice, discrepancy model, novelty vs significance, opponent-process theory, a-process, b-process, tolerance, craving, genetic learning

**Notable studies / people**
- Sokolov (1960–75): discrepancy model of orienting-response habituation (p. 50).
- Bradley (2009): novelty/significance account (pp. 51–52).
- Solomon & Corbit (1973), Solomon (1980): dogs' heart rate to repeated shocks; reaction shrinks, rebound grows (pp. 53–54).
- Epstein et al. (2009, 2011): habituation to food predicts later weight gain (p. 49).
- Gagliano et al. (2014): Mimosa leaves stop closing after repeated drops but still close when shaken (box 1.2, p. 47).
- Iacono & Lykken (1983): ignoring tones speeds habituation (p. 42).

**Use for teaching and studying (own inference)**
- Repetition of the same material in the same form dulls response; vary examples or format to restore attention (dishabituation) (inferred from pp. 48–49).
- Massed repetition gives quick but fragile change; spacing gives slower but longer-lasting change, supporting spaced practice in study plans (stated for habituation on p. 49; applying to study is my inference).
- Pairing repeated study with new, motivationally relevant material slows the decline in engagement (inferred from pp. 51–52).

## Ch 2 — Classical Conditioning: Effects of Regularities in the Presence of Multiple Stimuli (pp. 57–116)

**Core ideas**
- Classical conditioning is defined only by the environmental regularity: a stimulus counts as a CS when reactions to it depend on its relation to a US. It occurs with simple or complex stimuli and many behaviors, including fear, judgments, liking and key pecking (autoshaping) (pp. 57–58, 61, 66–71).
- Evaluative conditioning: pairing a neutral item with a liked or disliked one shifts liking, with practical uses in advertising; it is relatively resistant to extinction (pp. 71–72, 88). Conditioning also occurs by observation and by instructions (pp. 61–62).
- Selectivity: some CS–US pairs are learned more easily than others (taste with nausea, audiovisual cues with shock), and species differ (mammals vs birds) (pp. 63–65, 74). Ecologically valid CSs yield different, adaptive effects, such as improved reproductive efficiency in male quail (pp. 74–76).
- Conditioning is typically accompanied by awareness of the CS–US relation, and a secondary task that diverts attention weakens it; changes in expectancy tend to precede changes in physiological reactions (pp. 72–73, 76–77).
- Contingency beats contiguity: strength depends on the probability of the US given the CS compared with without it (delta P), so CS-alone and US-alone trials matter; positive contingency yields excitatory, negative yields inhibitory conditioning (pp. 77–79).
- Cue competition: overshadowing and blocking show that even a perfect X–US relation may not be learned if another cue already predicts the US; the relevant quantity is conditional contingency, and backward blocking occurs too (pp. 80–84).
- Preceding and following experience: CS pre-exposure, US pre-exposure and prior noncontingency slow later learning (so a child's pre-visit to the dentist or a patient's pre-visit to the ward can reduce acquired aversions) (pp. 85–87). Extinction (CS alone) weakens the response but not permanently: spontaneous recovery and renewal in a new context show it is fragile, and it is weaker for biologically relevant CSs (pp. 87–89).
- Occasion setting and the context-dependence of relations (pp. 90–91); forward conditioning usually beats backward and long delays weaken it except for intrinsically related pairs like food and nausea (p. 92).
- Mental-process theories: S–R accounts fail on contingency, sensory preconditioning, US revaluation and CR ≠ UR (pp. 93–95). S–S associative models handle more; Rescorla–Wagner ties learning to surprise (expectation discrepancy) and explains blocking, but cannot explain backward blocking or extinction phenomena (pp. 98–102). Wagner's SOP, Bouton's context-dependent inhibitory learning (extinction as new learning, not erasure), Miller's comparator model, attention models (Mackintosh, Pearce–Hall) offer alternatives (pp. 102–110). Propositional models say conditioning is mediated by conscious, truth-evaluable relational propositions, which fits awareness findings, causal-reasoning effects on blocking and secondary-task effects, but struggles with the Perruchet effect and lacks precision (pp. 110–115).

**Key terms:** CS, US, CR, UR, evaluative conditioning, autoshaping, aversion learning, intrinsic relation, US revaluation, counterconditioning, contingency awareness, contiguity vs contingency, delta P, conditional contingency, overshadowing, blocking, backward blocking, sensory preconditioning, higher-order conditioning, CS/US pre-exposure, extinction, spontaneous recovery, renewal, occasion setting, forward/backward conditioning, S–R vs S–S models, Rescorla–Wagner, expectation discrepancy, SOP, comparator model, propositional model, Perruchet effect

**Notable studies / people**
- Garcia & Koelling (1966): taste goes with illness, light–sound with shock (pp. 63–65).
- Rescorla (1966): contingency, not just pairings, drives conditioning (p. 78).
- Kamin (1968): blocking (pp. 80–81).
- Bouton (1993): context-dependent extinction; clinical consequences for exposure therapy and relapse (pp. 88, 105–106, 106–107 boxes).
- Brown & Jenkins (1968), Hearst & Jenkins (1974): autoshaping, including pecking that costs food (p. 67).
- Domjan (2005): ecologically valid CSs, sexual conditioning in quail (pp. 75–76).
- Hofmann et al. (2010): meta-analysis, contingency awareness is the strongest predictor of evaluative conditioning (p. 73).
- Waldmann & Holyoak (1992), De Houwer et al. (2002): blocking depends on whether cues are treated as causes and whether effects can add (pp. 113–114).

**Use for teaching and studying (own inference)**
- Extinction is context-bound, so practise or unlearn in varied settings and revisit later; this reduces renewal and recovery of old errors or fears (inferred from pp. 88, 106 box 2.6).
- When replacing an old label or habit, expect the old cue to block the new one; change the surrounding cues too (inferred from box 2.4, pp. 82–83).
- Make the target relation obvious and keep attention on it; distraction and uncertainty about which cue goes with which outcome weaken learning (inferred from pp. 72–73, 76–77, 112–113).

## Ch 3 — Operant Conditioning: Effects of Regularities in the Presence of Stimuli and Behavior (pp. 117–180)

**Core ideas**
- Operant conditioning is the effect of regularities between behavior and consequences. It is analyzed as a three-term contingency (antecedent Sd, response R, consequence Sr), with each term defined both descriptively (the procedure) and functionally (what actually controls behavior) (pp. 117–120).
- Reinforcement increases and punishment decreases behavior, with positive/negative and escape/avoidance subtypes. A behavior stopping after a scolding may reflect the scolding itself rather than punishment (pp. 121–122).
- Operant behavior is always a class of behaviors defined by a unit (force, novelty, speed, variability); changing the reinforced unit changes what is learned, and reinforcing novelty raises creativity (pp. 120, 133–134). Shaping builds new behavior step by step by gradually shifting the criterion, relying on natural variability (pp. 134–136).
- What serves as a reinforcer cannot be read off the stimulus: the law of effect, drive reduction and dopamine accounts all fall short. Premack's principle and the response deprivation model define reinforcers by relative behavior frequency, and context matters (e.g. food is no reinforcer when plentiful) (pp. 126–131). Reinforcer devaluation shows outcome value can change after learning (p. 131).
- Humans seem to learn contingencies mainly when they become aware of them; apparent unconscious operant learning often reflects a different, correlated rule that participants did notice. Verbally stated rules can override actual contingencies (rule-governed vs contingency-governed behavior) (pp. 136–137).
- Context: reinforcing alternative behavior (DRO) can displace unwanted behavior, distinct outcomes for different responses speed learning (differential outcomes effect), and establishing operations (deprivation, learned cues) alter reinforcer value, distinct from Sds, which signal availability (pp. 138–141).
- Contingency matters: p(Sr given R) must exceed p(Sr without R); conditional contingency effects parallel blocking (pp. 141–144).
- Reinforcement schedules: ratio schedules yield higher rates than interval schedules, variable ones more steady rates than fixed, and partial reinforcement more persistence than continuous, a result that makes sense when the unit of behavior is "repeated responding" (pp. 144–147, 154–156). The matching law relates relative response rates to relative reinforcement rates under concurrent variable-interval schedules; choosing small immediate over large delayed reinforcers models impulsivity, and a precommitment choice point makes delayed rewards more attractive (pp. 148–151).
- Indirect relations: conditioned and token reinforcers, and Sds influencing responses linked only through a shared outcome (pp. 151–153).
- Extinction produces an initial burst of responding, more variability and sometimes aggression; intermittent past reinforcement makes extinction slower (partial reinforcement extinction effect) (pp. 154–156). Learned helplessness is now re-read as controllability learning (pp. 153–154).
- Stimulus control and relational responding: discrimination vs generalization; responding to relations between stimuli (NAARR) occurs in humans and other animals, while arbitrarily applicable relational responding (AARR, e.g. stimulus equivalence) is documented only in verbal humans and is proposed as the basis of language (pp. 156–163).
- Punishment works better with immediacy, high contingency, intense onset, clear signals, and an alternative way to get the same benefit; gradual escalation is counterproductive (box 3.4, pp. 163–166). Critiques of reinforcement as "undermining intrinsic motivation" often confuse reward with reinforcer (box 3.6, pp. 177–179).
- Mental-process theories: S–R models (Thorndike, Hull, Mowrer's two-factor theory) are contradicted by devaluation findings; R–Sr and Sd–Sr models and Dickinson's combined model explain devaluation, habits and indirect Sd effects; propositional (causal-learning) models explain babies' sensitivity to control and avoidance, and relational responding (pp. 166–177).

**Key terms:** three-term contingency, Sd, R, Sr, response/operant class, unit of behavior, reinforcement, punishment, positive/negative reinforcement, escape, avoidance, law of effect, Premack principle, response deprivation, incentive learning, sensory reinforcement, shaping, DRO, differential outcomes effect, establishing operation, fixed/variable ratio and interval schedules, continuous vs partial reinforcement, concurrent schedules, matching law, concurrent-chain, self-control, conditioned/token reinforcer, learned helplessness, acquisition, extinction, extinction burst, partial reinforcement extinction effect, stimulus control, discrimination, generalization, peak shift, NAARR, AARR, stimulus equivalence, relational frame theory, rule-governed behavior, two-factor theory, habit

**Notable studies / people**
- Thorndike (1911): puzzle box; law of effect (pp. 122, 126).
- Skinner (1938; Ferster & Skinner 1957): Skinner box; schedules of reinforcement (pp. 123, 146).
- Premack (1962); Timberlake & Allison (1974): reinforcers as behaviors; response deprivation (pp. 129–130).
- Azrin & Holz (1966): intense first shock suppresses; gradual escalation does not (p. 124).
- Neuringer (2002): reinforcing novelty increases variability (p. 133).
- Hammond (1980): equal food rates with and without pressing eliminate lever pressing (p. 142).
- Colwill & Rescorla (1985, 1988): outcome devaluation; indirect Sd effects (pp. 131, 152–153).
- Herrnstein (1970): matching law (p. 148); Rachlin & Green (1972): concurrent-chain (pp. 150–151).
- Maier & Seligman (2016): helplessness reinterpreted as failure to learn control (p. 154).
- Watson (1967, 1971): babies respond to contingent but not noncontingent mobile movement (p. 176).
- Deci (1971), Eisenberg & Cameron (1996): the intrinsic-motivation debate (box 3.6, pp. 177–178).

**Use for teaching and studying (own inference)**
- Give feedback immediately and make it clearly tied to the specific response; delays and weak contingencies reduce effect (inferred from pp. 142, 164).
- Shape complex skills in sequenced steps, shifting the criterion only as the previous level becomes reliable (pp. 134–136).
- Do not ignore requests intermittently and then give in; inconsistent reinforcement makes unwanted behavior more persistent when you try to stop it (pp. 155–156). Reinforce alternative, incompatible behavior rather than relying on punishment (pp. 138, 165).

## Ch 4 — Complex Forms of Learning: Joint Effects of Multiple Regularities (pp. 181–198)

**Core ideas**
- Complex learning means behavior change caused by the joint action of several regularities. Moderated learning covers phenomena already met (sensory preconditioning, higher-order conditioning, conditioned reinforcement, differential outcomes effect) plus Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer, in which a stimulus paired with food raises a response that earned food (pp. 182–183).
- Regularities that share an element can be treated as intersecting regularities, a fourth type of regularity. In the Hughes et al. evaluative-learning experiment, a brand's liking changed because its response outcome (a neutral ideograph) was also linked to a positive or negative image (pp. 183–185).
- Metaregularities have regularities as elements. They allow relations (same, opposite, bigger) to function as stimuli, e.g. as the Sd in relational matching-to-sample; this is relational learning, to be distinguished from relational behavior, and regularities can in principle serve any stimulus function (pp. 185–188).
- Relational contextual cues signal which stimulus relation is relevant in a given context and can be single stimuli, relations or even regularities, but how they are acquired is still poorly understood (pp. 189–191).
- "Learning 2.0": in verbal humans, even basic conditioning may work because a regularity (a pairing, reinforcement) acts as a cue to respond as if the stimuli are equivalent, so evaluative conditioning and mere exposure are interpreted as arbitrarily applicable relational responding. Instructions and observation are likewise cues, the effect of a regularity should be context dependent, and learning in verbal humans may differ in kind from learning in animals (pp. 191–194). Opposite "context trials" reduced evaluative conditioning (p. 193).
- Shared (and related) features principle: when stimuli share one feature, people tend to act as though they share others (box 4.1, p. 195).
- Mental-process view: chains of associations can handle some complex learning but need backward spreading of activation and cannot represent how stimuli are related; propositional models, which can combine propositions by inference and encode relation type, fit NAARR/AARR better, yet do not explain why only verbal humans show AARR. The authors predict that undermining the inference should undermine the effect (pp. 196–198).

**Key terms:** simple vs complex learning, moderated learning, metaregularity, intersecting regularities, Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer, relational behavior vs relational learning, relational contextual cue, arbitrarily/nonarbitrarily applicable relational responding, Learning 2.0, rule-governed behavior, shared features principle, related features principle, propositional inference

**Notable studies / people**
- Hughes, De Houwer & Perugini (2016): intersecting operant regularities transfer valence to brands (pp. 184–185).
- Cook & Harris (1937): instructions alone can establish a fear response (p. 193).
- Hughes, Ye & De Houwer (2019): opposite word pairs weaken evaluative conditioning (p. 193).
- Hayes, Barnes-Holmes & Roche (2001): relational frame theory and relational contextual cues (pp. 189, 194).
- Hughes et al. (in press): shared features principle (box 4.1, p. 195).

**Use for teaching and studying (own inference)**
- Teach concepts as networks of relations (same, opposite, part of) and explicitly label the relation, since people respond to relations between items and not just items (pp. 185–188).
- Provide clear cues about which relation or context applies when examples share multiple features; ambiguity about the relevant relation will cause misapplication (pp. 189–190).
- Because instructions and verbal rules can produce learning without direct experience (p. 193), pair explanations with examples, and check that students have not drawn false analogies from shared surface features (p. 195).

## Ch 5 — Applied Learning Psychology: Using the Principles of Learning to Improve Well-Being (pp. 199–246; the chapter proper ends p. 232, and pp. 233–246 are the authors' reflections on the "Think It Through" questions)

**Core ideas**
- The authors argue that what holds for rats and pigeons also shapes human behavior, and that the principles of learning are already used in disability treatment, therapy, addiction treatment, parenting, schools, health, organizations and environmental behavior (pp. 200–201). These tools can serve prosocial or selfish ends (pp. 202, 231, box 5.2).
- Applied behavior analysis targets behavior as the problem, not mental processes. Before intervening, the analyst runs a functional analysis (antecedents and consequences), obtains baseline data, specifies the target in measurable terms in a behavioral contract, records it, and then manages contingencies, usually involving the people who control the reinforcers (pp. 204–206). A kindergarten example shows how attention can reinforce crying or self-injury and how reinforcing incompatible behavior can replace it (pp. 204–205).
- Interventions are evaluated with single-subject designs: A-B-A-B reversals, or multiple baselines across settings, individuals or behaviors when reversal is unethical (pp. 206–208). Generalization is trained directly (stimulus, response, maintenance) rather than assumed (p. 208).
- Developmental disabilities: early intensive behavioral interventions (about 20–40 hours weekly from age 3–4, with caregivers as co-therapists) shape skills in a developmental sequence; natural environment training uses naturally occurring motivation, and the PEAK system adds relational learning (pp. 208–210).
- Clinical: classical and operant ideas underlie behavior therapy (functional analysis, systematic desensitization, exposure), cognitive therapy added mental content, and newer approaches (ACT, DBT, behavioral activation) re-emphasize context and the function of thoughts rather than correcting them (pp. 210–214).
- Substance use is analyzed as choice between drug and abstinence; contingency management reinforces verified abstinence with vouchers or deposit contracts, and intermittent prizes can stand in when continuous reinforcement is impractical (pp. 214–216).
- Groups: nurturing environments (Biglan) reinforce prosocial behavior, reduce toxic conditions, set limits and promote prosocial values; coercive family cycles (negative reinforcement of aggression) are replaced via behavioral parent training. "Behavioral vaccines" are simple, widely used practices; practices, programs and policies differ in scale (pp. 216–220, box 5.1).
- Schools: avoid the "coercive trap" by praising on-task behavior and ignoring disruptions; the Good Behavior Game (team contingencies, scoreboard, interval-based play) lowers disruption and was linked to better outcomes into adulthood; direct instruction uses mastery, modeling, extensive responding and immediate feedback; Positive Behavioral Intervention and Support works at individual, classroom and school levels (pp. 220–222).
- Climate change is analyzed as a contingency problem: immediate reinforcers beat delayed consequences, and organizations are controlled by their own contingencies, so interventions need economic and social consequences, policy, and attention to systems, not just individuals (pp. 223–228).
- Few real-world interventions derive directly from cognitive learning theories (Bouton's context-dependent extinction and expectancy-violation ideas for exposure therapy are exceptions), which the authors call a challenge for that tradition; they propose "psychological engineering," a shared functional-cognitive framework for applied work (pp. 228–230).
- The closing reflections reiterate that functional explanations are legitimate when judged by prediction and influence, that cognitive theory has not yet delivered many applications, and that the relation is asymmetrical (functional work does not need cognitive theory, but cognitive work needs functional findings) (pp. 243–246).

**Key terms:** applied behavior analysis, functional analysis, baseline, behavioral contract, event/interval/time-sample recording, contingency management, single-subject design, A-B-A-B, multiple baseline, stimulus/response generalization, behavioral maintenance, early intensive behavioral intervention, natural environment training, PEAK, systematic desensitization, exposure, cognitive behavioral therapy, ACT, abstinence reinforcement, voucher-based treatment, deposit contracting, nurturing environments, coercion, behavioral vaccine, practices/programs/policies, Good Behavior Game, direct instruction, Positive Behavioral Intervention and Support, mitigate/adapt/suffer, cap and trade, psychological engineering

**Notable studies / people**
- Patterson (1982): coercive family interactions between aggressive children, parents and siblings (p. 218).
- Biglan (2015): nurturing environments and behavioral vaccines (pp. 216–217).
- Barrish, Saunders & Wolf (1969), Kellam et al. (1998, 2008): Good Behavior Game and later outcomes (p. 221).
- Higgins, Silverman & Heil (2008); Stitzer & Petry (2006): reviews cited for contingency management of drug use (pp. 214, 216).
- Surwit (1972): pre-exposure reduces children's dental fear (p. 228).
- Skinner (1987): asked why we are not acting to save the world, blaming the lack of a language for analyzing contingencies (p. 227).
- Craske et al. (2014): applying expectancy violation and context ideas to exposure therapy (p. 228).

**Use for teaching and studying (own inference)**
- Before redesigning a course or study routine, record a baseline of the target behavior (attendance, practice time, error rates) and define it measurably, then compare after the change (pp. 206–208).
- Use mastery progression with frequent responding and immediate corrective feedback, advancing only when a skill is secure; competence itself becomes reinforcing (p. 222).
- Make desired behavior easy to start and immediately reinforced (group or team contingencies, visible scoreboards, praise for on-task work), rather than relying on delayed benefits or on punishment (pp. 220–221, 225).

## Cross-cutting takeaways for teaching and learning

1. Define learning by observable behavior change caused by the environment, and test whether a lesson really caused a change by comparing conditions (pp. 4–5, 12).
2. Keep functional claims ("this arrangement improves performance") separate from mechanistic explanations ("because of memory associations"); this prevents advice from collapsing when a theory is revised (pp. 27, 31).
3. Immediacy, clear contingency and signalling of when a response will pay off drive operant learning; delayed or unsignalled feedback is weak (pp. 119, 142, 164).
4. Spacing trades speed for durability: massed repetition habituates faster but fades sooner; distributed repetition lasts longer (p. 49).
5. Extinguished or corrected responses return outside the setting where they were corrected; vary contexts and expect relapse (pp. 88, 106).
6. Prior cues compete with new ones (blocking, overshadowing), so replacing a habit or label means changing the surrounding cues too (pp. 80–83).
7. Awareness of the relation and attention to it matter for human learning; distraction and unclear relations weaken conditioning, and apparent unconscious learning often hides a different consciously noticed rule (pp. 72–73, 76–77, 136–137).
8. New complex behavior is built by shaping, shifting the criterion in small steps from variable existing behavior, and reinforcing a unit such as novelty yields novelty (pp. 133–136).
9. Intermittent reinforcement makes behavior persistent and resistant to extinction; schedules shape rates and steadiness (pp. 144–147, 154–156).
10. Reinforcing incompatible alternatives (DRO) and giving distinct outcomes for different responses beat punishing the unwanted behavior; punishment is effective only under strict conditions and escalating mild punishment backfires (pp. 138–139, 163–166).
11. Reinforcers are defined by function and depend on context, deprivation and the behavior they follow; reward and reinforcer are different concepts, so the "rewards kill motivation" debate is largely a level-confusion (pp. 126–131, 139, 177–179).
12. Humans respond to relations between stimuli and to verbal rules, so teach relations explicitly and use instruction, but check for false rules or analogies formed from shared features (pp. 160–163, 193, 195).
13. In applied work, run a functional analysis, set measurable targets, take baselines, use single-case or multiple-baseline checks, and train generalization and maintenance deliberately (pp. 204–208).
14. Mastery-based direct instruction with modeling, frequent responding and immediate corrective feedback, plus classroom systems like the Good Behavior Game, are the book's most concrete educational applications (p. 221–222).
15. Beware of coercive cycles in classrooms and homes where punishment and escape reinforce each other; shift toward reinforcing prosocial and on-task behavior (pp. 217–221).
