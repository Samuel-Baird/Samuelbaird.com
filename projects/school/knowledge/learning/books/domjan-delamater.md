# Domjan & Delamater — *The Essentials of Conditioning and Learning* (digest)

Citation: Domjan, M., & Delamater, A. R. (2023/2024). *The Essentials of Conditioning and Learning* (5th ed.). American Psychological Association.

Orientation: a compact, behavior-analytic and experimental treatment of animal and human learning that takes a "general-process" stance (shared basic rules across species and tasks), moves from habituation and Pavlovian conditioning through operant learning, extinction, punishment, avoidance and stimulus control, and closes with the memory mechanisms behind all of it. Page cites below are printed page numbers.

## Ch 1 — Basic Concepts and Definitions (pp. 3–16)

**Core ideas**
- Learning shows up as a change in behavior, but the change can be either more of a response or less of one; learning to hold back is often as important as learning to act (p. 4).
- Learning is a lasting change that comes from relevant experience. That separates it from fatigue, motivational shifts and momentary changes in stimulus conditions (short-lived), and from maturation and evolution (long-lasting but not due to practice with the specific behavior) (pp. 4–6).
- Maturation can make learning easier but does not itself require practice of the specific behavior, and maturational gains spread broadly, whereas learned gains tend to stay tied to what was practiced, with only limited generalization to similar situations (pp. 5–6).
- Learning can be "behaviorally silent": what is learned may be a stimulus–stimulus relation or may need the right opportunity or motivation to be expressed, so performance is not identical to learning and special test procedures are sometimes needed (pp. 6–7).
- The book defines learning as a relatively enduring change in the mechanisms of behavior brought about by experience with environmental events specifically related to that behavior; this mechanism can be studied at behavioral, neural-system and cellular/molecular levels (pp. 7–8).
- Because learning is a cause of behavior change, it cannot be observed directly or established by naturalistic observation; it must be inferred by comparing an experimental condition (with the training experience) against a control condition treated identically otherwise (pp. 8–11).
- Careful control procedures matter as much as training procedures, and some landmark advances came from rethinking controls. Designs can be between-group or single-case, the latter comparing each individual against its own baseline and being especially suited to applied and clinical translation (pp. 11–12).
- The general-process assumption is that the rules of learning are universal even when stimuli, responses and speeds differ across species; this justifies studying nonhuman animals, which allow better control of history, genetics, motivation and the learning environment (pp. 12–14).

**Key terms**: fatigue, motivation, maturation, practice, evolution, S–S learning, performance, level of analysis, naturalistic observation, experimental observation, independent variable, dependent variable, experimental condition, control condition, between-group design, single-case experiment, baseline data, general-process approach.

**Notable studies / people**: Darwin and Romanes (1890s era) treated learning as evidence of intelligence (p. 3); Pavlov saw learning procedures as a window on the nervous system (p. 7); Sidman (1960) formalized single-case designs in the Skinnerian tradition (p. 12); Church (1964) and Rescorla (1967) are cited as cases where control-procedure analysis advanced the field (p. 12); Rescorla (1988) is cited for equating test conditions across groups (p. 10).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- To claim that a method "works," compare it against a matched no-training or alternative-training group tested the same way, rather than pointing to people who succeeded (pp. 10–11).
- Do not equate current performance with learning: test with several formats (recognition as well as recall) and under favorable motivation/opportunity, since knowledge can be present but not displayed (pp. 6–7).
- Treat an "understood it in class" feeling as unproven until it survives to later testing, since the book treats retention across time as part of what counts as learning (p. 5).

## Ch 2 — The Substrate for Learning: Unconditioned Behavior (pp. 17–29)

**Core ideas**
- Shaping (changing behavior by rewarding successive approximations) is often compared to molding clay, but the book prefers a wood-grain analogy: behavior is uneven material with built-in tendencies, so some changes are easy and others resist training (pp. 18–19).
- Evidence for that unevenness: animals readily learn to approach and handle food-related stimuli but have trouble learning to release or withdraw from them (p. 18). Any learning therefore reflects the interaction between the training procedure and pre-existing behavior (p. 19).
- The reflex (Descartes) is the smallest unit of unlearned behavior: a specific stimulus reliably producing a specific response, carried by a stimulus-to-response path (the reflex arc); reflexes support breathing, feeding, posture and protection (pp. 19–20).
- Ethologists extended elicited behavior to modal action patterns: coordinated, species-typical response patterns (not single muscle twitches) that occur similarly in most members of a species though with some variability, which is why "modal" replaced "fixed" (p. 22).
- A narrow stimulus feature, the sign (or releasing) stimulus, is often enough to trigger a modal action pattern. Examples are the red spot on a gull parent's bill and the red belly of a rival stickleback; humans show comparable sign-stimulus effects (pp. 22–24).
- Tasks that fit an animal's evolved responses and stimuli are "biologically prepared" and learned easily; tasks contrary to that history are hard or impossible (pp. 23–24).
- Unlearned behavior is organized by motivational state (the hydraulic model: drive builds, lowers response thresholds, and is discharged by performing the response) and sequentially, from flexible appetitive behavior to stereotyped consummatory behavior. Appetitive behavior is the part most likely to be modified by conditioning (pp. 24–26).
- Behavior-systems theory (Timberlake) refines this into modes (general search, focal search, food handling/ingestion) and modules. The modes run in a linear but reversible order, are spread out in time, and each shifts both the responses shown and the stimuli attended to (pp. 26–28).

**Key terms**: shaping, reflex, elicited behavior, reflex arc, ethology, modal action pattern, species-typical behavior, sign stimulus, releasing stimulus, biological preparedness, motivational state, hydraulic model, appetitive behavior, consummatory behavior, behavior system, general search mode, focal search mode, food handling and ingestion mode.

**Notable studies / people**: Skinner (1953) coined shaping (p. 18); Rachlin (1976) offered the wood analogy (p. 18); Hearst & Jenkins (1974) and Breland & Breland (1961) on easy versus hard-to-train responses (p. 18); Descartes on the reflex (pp. 19–20); Tinbergen (1952) on stickleback courtship as a chain of elicited responses (p. 21); Tinbergen & Perdeck (1950) on the red-spot sign stimulus in gull chicks (p. 23); Timberlake (1994) on feeding as a behavior system (p. 26).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Match tasks to what learners already do naturally: skills built on existing tendencies are acquired faster, and tasks that fight those tendencies need more scaffolding (pp. 18–19, 24).
- Expect training to shape flexible, search-like (appetitive) components more than rigid ones, so design practice around the adjustable parts of a skill (p. 26).
- Treat each stage of a multi-step skill as drawing on different cues; practice the stage-specific cues rather than only the end behavior (p. 28).

## Ch 3 — Habituation and Sensitization (pp. 31–44)

**Core ideas**
- Reflexes are not the automatic, invariant reactions Descartes imagined: repeated presentation of an eliciting stimulus can lower responding (habituation), raise it (sensitization), or produce a rise followed by a fall; this keeps organisms from wasting effort on irrelevant events while staying alert to important ones (pp. 32, 35).
- Both processes are evolutionarily widespread, are studied with infant looking time and startle responses, and habituation deficits appear in several neuropsychiatric conditions (p. 33). In infants, a simple pattern habituated steadily while a complex pattern first increased then decreased looking (p. 33).
- Habituation is specific to the repeated stimulus: changing the stimulus brings back the response. This rules out simple fatigue and points to a central-nervous-system change; infant researchers use a novel-stimulus test to check the baby is still attentive (pp. 35–36).
- Habituation weakens with time without stimulation (spontaneous recovery); more time off means more recovery, and recovery shrinks over repeated cycles. Some habituation (such as to a new taste) is long-lasting, and only long-term effects count clearly as learning (pp. 36–38).
- Frequency and intensity effects depend on what is measured: rapid, frequent repetition produces more short-term habituation but less long-term habituation; more intense stimuli usually habituate more slowly (p. 38).
- Dishabituation is recovery of a habituated response after a different stimulus intervenes; together with stimulus specificity it rules out sensory adaptation. Infant research and other animal research use the word slightly differently (pp. 38–39).
- Sensitization grows with stimulus intensity, can be short- or long-term, and is not stimulus-specific (for instance, fear boosts startle and illness boosts general taste neophobia) (p. 40).
- Dual-process theory: habituation arises in the direct stimulus–response pathway and builds up whenever the stimulus occurs (universal); sensitization arises in a separate "state" system tied to arousal and need not involve the eliciting stimulus. Observed behavior is the net of the two, each decaying with time. The theory explains short-term effects but not long-term ones, which are likely associative (pp. 40–43).

**Key terms**: habituation effect, sensitization effect, orienting response, startle response, stimulus specificity, spontaneous recovery, flavor neophobia, short-term habituation, long-term habituation, dishabituation, sensory adaptation, short-term sensitization, long-term sensitization, dual-process theory, S–R system, state system, afferent neuron, interneuron, efferent neuron.

**Notable studies / people**: Bashinski, Werner & Rudy (1985) on infant fixation to simple versus complex checkerboards and stimulus specificity (pp. 33–35); Dissegna et al. (2018) on newborn chicks' freezing to predator calls and dishabituation by white noise (p. 38); Thompson & Spencer (1966) on the dishabituation terminology (p. 39); Groves & Thompson (1970) proposed dual-process theory (p. 40); Davis (1970) and Davis & Wagner (1968) on frequency and intensity effects (p. 38); Wagner (1976, 1981) as an associative account of long-term effects (p. 43).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Repetition of identical material breeds decline in attention; vary the form, context or example (a novel stimulus) to bring attention back, but expect the bored response to return specifically to the repeated item (pp. 35, 38).
- Massed, rapid repetition lowers responding quickly but gives weaker long-lasting habituation than spaced exposure would suggest, so for durable effects do not judge by within-session change alone (p. 38).
- Strong, significant or arousing events can raise general responsiveness to unrelated material; be aware that arousing context affects reactions to whatever follows (pp. 40, 43).

## Ch 4 — Pavlovian Conditioning: Basic Concepts (pp. 45–61)

**Core ideas**
- Associative learning, unlike single-stimulus learning, means one event comes to activate a representation of another. In Pavlovian conditioning a conditioned stimulus (CS) paired with an unconditioned stimulus (US) comes to elicit a conditioned response (CR) (pp. 45–46). The popular bell-and-dog story is inaccurate: Pavlov's early work used the visible features of objects placed in the mouth, which is a case of object learning (p. 46).
- Contemporary preparations are appetitive (food reward, e.g., pigeon key-peck sign tracking, or rats showing sign tracking, goal tracking, or both) and aversive (eyeblink, fear conditioning with freezing as the measure); CRs are often skeletal, and rats differ individually in which CR they develop, with sign-tracking linked to incentive motivation, addiction-related traits and impulsivity (pp. 47–50).
- The CR need not resemble the UR (e.g., freezing to a cue that predicts shock, which itself causes jumping). The CR is better predicted by the behavior system the US activates and by which mode of that system the CS joins; the CS–US interval matters (short intervals favor focal responses/panic, long ones favor general search/anxiety) (pp. 50–52).
- Conditioning also changes how the organism handles the US. Drug cues trigger compensatory adjustments that produce tolerance, which is reversible when the drug is taken in unfamiliar surroundings, a point with overdose implications; sexual cues likewise improve copulatory efficiency in quail (pp. 52–53).
- CS effectiveness depends on novelty (latent inhibition: unreinforced preexposure slows later conditioning, explained by reduced attention or by interfering memory) and on CS–US relevance (selective association) (pp. 53–54).
- Garcia and Koelling showed rats readily link taste with illness and audiovisual cues with shock, but not the reverse; people show similar selective food aversions, and primates learn fear of snakes more readily than of flowers (pp. 55–56).
- Showing that a behavior change is associative requires controls against sensitization and the like: CS-alone and US-alone controls are inadequate, the random control (Rescorla) has weaknesses, and the discriminative (CS+ vs CS−) and unpaired controls are further options; none is universally satisfactory (pp. 56–58).
- Pavlovian conditioning is pervasive because real-world cues are precursors to events in space and time: object features, drug rituals, mate-seeking cues and warnings of aversive events (pp. 58–60).

**Key terms**: associative learning, US, UR, CS, CR, object learning, appetitive conditioning, aversive conditioning, fear conditioning, sign tracking, autoshaping, goal tracking, magazine conditioning, incentive motivation, eyeblink conditioning, freezing, behavior systems, drug tolerance, latent inhibition, taste aversion learning, CS–US relevance, selective association, CS-alone control, US-alone control, random control, discriminative control, unpaired control.

**Notable studies / people**: Pavlov's associates Vul'fson and Snarskii (early salivary work) (p. 46); Hearst & Jenkins (1974) and Tomie et al. (1989) on autoshaping (p. 48); Flagel et al. (2009) on rats splitting into sign trackers and goal trackers (pp. 48–49); S. Siegel (2008, 2016) on conditioning accounts of drug tolerance and overdose (p. 53); Garcia & Koelling (1966) on selective association (p. 55); Rescorla (1967) on the random control (p. 56); Benedict & Ayres (1972) on chance pairings undermining it (p. 57).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Cues present when something is learned become predictors of it, so anything learned in only one setting may weaken or fail elsewhere; vary and practice in the settings where performance will be needed (pp. 52–53).
- Unfamiliar-by-default cues condition best; material already heavily familiar and "unreinforced" (latent inhibition) is harder to attach new meaning to, so expect extra resistance when re-purposing familiar cues (pp. 53–54).
- When judging whether a program caused a change, include a comparison condition that controls for mere exposure (an analog of the unpaired or discriminative control) (pp. 56–58).

## Ch 5 — Stimulus Relations in Pavlovian Conditioning (pp. 63–82)

**Core ideas**
- Timing arrangements differ sharply in effectiveness: simultaneous CS–US presentation yields little anticipatory responding, delayed conditioning (CS starts shortly before the US and stays on until it) is the standard effective procedure, and adding a gap between CS offset and US (trace conditioning) can sharply cut responding, with as little as half a second disrupting fear conditioning (pp. 64–66).
- Trace conditioning requires bridging time and recruits additional brain areas, notably the hippocampus (p. 66).
- The best CS–US interval depends on the response system: fractions of a second for the rabbit nictitating-membrane response, up to a few minutes for fear, and hours for taste aversion ("long-delay learning"), although even there shorter gaps work better (pp. 66–67).
- Animals also learn precisely when the US will occur relative to the CS (temporal coding), suggesting conditioning involves more than a bare association; imprecise timing knowledge at a railroad crossing is the book's example (p. 68).
- Contiguity is not sufficient for conditioning: in blocking, a new cue paired with the US alongside an already-trained cue is poorly learned because it is redundant (the prior cue already predicts the US), even though its timing relation to the US is identical to that in controls. Conditioning tracks how well a cue signals the US (pp. 68–70).
- Contingency compares the probability of the US given the CS to the probability given no CS (positive, negative or zero). Contemporary analysis treats contingency as a procedural variable whose effects work largely through conditioning of background context cues (pp. 70–72).
- Conditioned inhibition (the CS signals the absence of the US) requires that the US be expected first. It is created by A+ / AB− training or by a negative CS–US contingency, and is detected with a summation test (compared against a neutral cue) and, traditionally, a retardation-of-acquisition test (pp. 72–76).
- Hierarchical relations: an occasion setter (T) signals whether a target CS (L) will be paired with the US. Evidence it is truly hierarchical includes distinctive response forms to each cue and the fact that extinguishing T leaves its modulation intact; sequential rather than simultaneous presentation favors this learning; negative occasion setters keep working even after being paired with the US (pp. 76–80).

**Key terms**: simultaneous conditioning, temporal contiguity, delayed conditioning, trace conditioning, trace interval, CS–US (interstimulus) interval, long-delay learning, temporal coding, blocking effect, credit assignment, contingency, context conditioning, conditioned excitation, conditioned inhibition, A+/AB− procedure, negative contingency, summation test, retardation-of-acquisition test, hierarchical stimulus relation, positive occasion setting (conditioned facilitation), negative occasion setting.

**Notable studies / people**: Kamin (1965) on trace interval and fear conditioning, and Kamin (1969) on blocking (pp. 66, 69); Schneiderman & Gormezano (1964) on delayed eyeblink-type conditioning and optimum intervals (pp. 65, 67); Garcia et al. (1966) on long-delay taste-aversion learning (p. 67); Rescorla (1967, 1968) on contingency and Wagner et al. (1968) on relative cue validity (p. 68); Pavlov (1927) on inhibition as a counterpart of excitation (p. 72); Holland (1977, 1989) on distinct responses to a tone and a light in occasion setting (pp. 78–79); Knight et al. (2004) on trace conditioning in human brain imaging (p. 66).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Present a cue or warning shortly before the thing it predicts and keep the interval short and consistent; large or varying delays, or gaps that end the cue before the event, weaken learning (pp. 65–67).
- Redundant information is learned poorly: do not pile new cues on top of ones the learner already uses to predict the outcome; introduce new material on its own first if it must be learned well (pp. 69–70).
- Teach "when does it not apply" through contrast cases after the rule is firmly expected (the A+/AB− logic), and use conditional cues ("only in situation X") sequentially rather than simultaneously (pp. 73–74, 79).

## Ch 6 — Pavlovian Conditioning Mechanisms and Theories (pp. 83–100)

**Core ideas**
- Two accounts of what is learned: S–R learning (the CS directly triggers the CR) versus S–S learning (the CS brings to mind an internal picture of the US, and what the animal then does depends on its current motivation toward that US) (pp. 84–85).
- US devaluation is the diagnostic test: reduce the value of the US after training (satiety, taste aversion, lowered sexual motivation) and see whether responding to the CS drops. In sign-tracking rats and in quail it dropped substantially, indicating mainly S–S learning, with a little residual S–R responding (pp. 85–87).
- This suggests clinical interventions can target the US's value instead of only extinguishing the CS (p. 88). A US also has sensory features (e.g., flavor) and hedonic features (pleasure), and a CS may activate either or both, via different neural mechanisms (p. 88).
- Rescorla–Wagner model: learning on a trial is proportional to prediction error, the gap between the maximum the US supports and the summed associative value of all cues present (ΔV = k(λ − V)). Early trials produce big gains and later ones small gains, and blocking follows because a cue already predicting the US leaves no error to learn from (pp. 89–91).
- The model predicts counterintuitive effects such as overexpectation, in which two separately trained cues combined and followed by the original single US both lose strength; it explains conditioned inhibition as negative associative value and extinction as error-driven decline (pp. 91–93).
- Weaknesses of the model: extinguishing a conditioned inhibitor alone does not remove its inhibition (but extinguishing the CS+ it was trained with does), and extinction appears to be new learning rather than simple unlearning (p. 94).
- Attentional models (Pearce–Hall and others) hold that a CS's associability changes with training: surprise on one trial raises attention to the CS on the next trial, so these models act prospectively and struggle with blocking on the first compound trial (pp. 94–95).
- Time matters: responding depends on intertrial interval relative to CS duration (the I/T ratio of rate expectancy theory) because a high ratio makes the CS more informative than the background, though findings are mixed (pp. 95–96). The comparator hypothesis says responding depends on comparing the target CS's strength with that of the context cues present during training, so extinguishing context or a blocking cue later can reveal hidden responding (revaluation) (pp. 96–99).
- Another unresolved issue is whether compounds are encoded as sums of elements (Rescorla–Wagner) or as unique configurations (Pearce) (p. 100).

**Key terms**: S–R learning, S–S learning, US devaluation, sensory versus hedonic features, Rescorla–Wagner model, prediction error, asymptote, salience, overexpectation, associability, Pearce–Hall model, rate expectancy theory, I/T ratio, comparator hypothesis, revaluation effect, elemental versus configural encoding.

**Notable studies / people**: Rescorla (1973) on US devaluation logic (p. 85); Derman et al. (2018) on lever sign tracking after pellet-devaluation (p. 86); Holloway & Domjan (1993) on quail and reduced sexual motivation (pp. 86–87); Kamin (1969) on surprise and blocking (p. 89); Rescorla & Wagner (1972) on the model (p. 89); Pearce & Hall (1980) on attentional change (p. 94); Gallistel & Gibbon (2000) on rate expectancy (p. 95); R. R. Miller & Matzel (1988) on the comparator hypothesis (p. 96); Pearce (1987) on the configural approach (p. 100).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Learning is driven by surprise: material that the learner already predicts adds little, so build in prediction (ask learners to anticipate answers) and focus new input where their expectations are wrong (pp. 89–91).
- Do not rely on a learner's performance alone to infer what was learned; a value-change or transfer test (US-devaluation logic) can reveal whether they hold the underlying relation or a rote response (pp. 85–86).
- Because extinguishing a competing cue can release a hidden response, correcting or removing the stronger competing association may be more effective than drilling the weak one directly (pp. 98–99).

## Ch 7 — Instrumental or Operant Conditioning (pp. 101–119)

**Core ideas**
- In instrumental conditioning, whether a significant outcome (reinforcer) occurs depends on the learner's own action; operant behavior is defined by the environmental change it produces, and variations in how the movement is made are ignored. Earlier chapters concerned events outside the learner's control (pp. 102–103).
- Two research traditions: Thorndike's discrete-trial puzzle-box and maze work (experimenter-set trials, latency measured; place versus response learning tested in plus mazes) and Skinner's free-operant method (response can occur any time; rate of responding is the key measure) (pp. 103–106).
- Many "new" responses are familiar components recombined or familiar movements brought under new stimulus control; entirely novel behavior is acquired by shaping, reinforcing successive approximations, which works because behavior is variable and moving the criterion shifts the whole distribution (pp. 106–109).
- Shaping requires a clearly defined target, a documented starting repertoire, and a planned sequence of steps; the bicycle example builds pedaling, then steering, then balance (p. 109).
- Instrumental conditioning selects responses, so the reinforcer must follow the target response immediately; with delay, other activities intervene and get reinforced. The deleterious effect of delay can be reduced by a conditioned (secondary) reinforcer such as a star later exchanged for a prize, or by a brief marking stimulus that makes the response memorable (pp. 110–111).
- Thorndike's law of effect says reinforcement stamps in an association between the situation S and the response R, with the outcome as a catalyst rather than part of the association. It underlies modern accounts of habits, and reinforcer-devaluation tests show that responding becomes insensitive to outcome value mainly after extensive training, though extended training does not always produce habits (pp. 111–114).
- Goal-directed behavior depends on associations that include the outcome: S–O (cues activate outcome memory, which can motivate responding via Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer), R–O, O–R (bidirectional), and the hierarchical S–(R–O), which corresponds to Skinner's three-term contingency. All are sensitive to outcome devaluation (pp. 114–116).
- Biological constraints (pig "misbehavior" with coins, difficulty conditioning grooming) are explained by S–O associations producing Pavlovian approach/manipulation responses that compete with the required response. Because instrumental procedures inevitably create several associations at once, isolating neural mechanisms is hard (pp. 116–118).

**Key terms**: instrumental behavior, reinforcer, response–reinforcer contingency, operant, puzzle box, discrete-trial method, T-maze, plus maze, free-operant method, rate of responding, response variability, shaping, successive approximations, response–reinforcer contiguity, conditioned (secondary) reinforcer, marking stimulus, law of effect, S–R association, habit, reinforcer devaluation, goal-directed behavior, S–O association, Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer, R–O association, bidirectional hypothesis, S–(R–O) association, three-term contingency, biological constraints.

**Notable studies / people**: Thorndike (1898) on cats in puzzle boxes (pp. 103–104); Small (1899, 1900) on the maze (p. 104); Skinner (1938) on the free-operant method and (1969) on the three-term contingency (pp. 105, 115); Packard & McGaugh (1996) on place versus response learning (p. 105); Colwill & Rescorla (1985) on the outcome's role (p. 112); Dickinson (1985) on responses becoming outcome-independent after extended training (p. 113); Breland & Breland (1961) on the pig and the coin (p. 117); Timberlake et al. (1982) on S–O explanations of constraint (p. 117).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Give feedback or reward immediately after the target act; when real consequences must be delayed, bridge the gap with an immediate token or a distinctive marker that identifies exactly which act was correct (pp. 110–111).
- Teach complex skills by shaping: define the end performance, assess the starting repertoire, then raise the criterion in small steps, and sequence component skills (as with pedal, steer, balance) so each is solid before combining (p. 109).
- Early learning is goal-directed, extensive repetition can turn it into habit; encourage deliberate practice with purpose first and then repeated practice in realistic cues where automatic performance is the aim (pp. 112–114).

## Ch 8 — Schedules of Reinforcement (pp. 121–137)

**Core ideas**
- A schedule of reinforcement is the rule that determines which responses get reinforced. Schedules control response rate, pattern and persistence in extinction, and the book argues the schedule can matter more than reinforcer size; modern digital monitoring (wages, games, phones) makes schedules ubiquitous (p. 122).
- A cumulative record plots responses accumulated over time, so the slope shows response rate (flat means no responding, steeper means faster) (p. 123).
- Ratio schedules reinforce after a count of responses regardless of time. Fixed-ratio produces a pause after reinforcement followed by a high steady run, with larger ratios giving longer pauses; variable-ratio produces steady high rates with no predictable pauses (gambling is the classic case) (pp. 124–125).
- Interval schedules reinforce the first response after a time has elapsed; the reinforcer is not delivered automatically and stays available until collected. Fixed-interval yields a post-reinforcement pause and accelerating "scallop" responding (resembling students cramming before exams), variable-interval yields steady, moderate responding, and a limited hold adds urgency and raises response rate (pp. 126–128).
- The feedback function explains rate differences. In ratio schedules reinforcement rate rises linearly with response rate, so short interresponse times are reinforced and rates are high; in interval schedules the payoff plateaus quickly, so long interresponse times are favored and rates are lower (pp. 128–130).
- Concurrent schedules (two alternatives available at once) model choice. Behavior matches relative reinforcement rates (Herrnstein's matching law), with the generalized version accounting for differences in effort, reinforcer type and switching difficulty (pp. 131–132). Practical consequence: effort devoted to a target task depends on available alternatives and their reinforcement, so competing sources of reward (e.g., a phone during math) reduce target behavior (p. 133).
- Concurrent-chain schedules involve choice with commitment. They show a preference for variable over fixed (equal-rate) schedules in pigeons and are the standard model of self-control, choosing between smaller-sooner and larger-later rewards (pp. 133–134).
- Self-control reflects delay discounting (value falls with waiting time). Steeper discounting predicts poorer self-control, drug use and worse health, and early self-control predicts adult outcomes; discounting can be reduced by gradually lengthening delays to the larger reward and by training inhibition, time estimation and episodic future thinking (pp. 134–135).

**Key terms**: schedule of reinforcement, cumulative record, simple schedules, ratio schedule, interval schedule, fixed-ratio (FR), variable-ratio (VR), postreinforcement pause, ratio run, fixed-interval (FI), variable-interval (VI), scallop pattern, limited hold, feedback function, continuous reinforcement, interresponse time (IRT), concurrent schedule, matching law, generalized matching law, concurrent-chain schedule, choice link, terminal link, self-control, smaller-sooner versus larger-later, delay discounting function.

**Notable studies / people**: Ferster & Skinner (1957) established characteristic schedule patterns (pp. 122, 125, 127); Herrnstein (1970) on the matching law (p. 132); Baum (1974) on the generalized matching law (p. 132); Andrzejewski et al. (2005) on preference for variable schedules (p. 134); Moffitt et al. (2011) on childhood self-control and adult outcomes (p. 135); MacKillop et al. (2011) and Yi et al. (2010) on steeper discounting in addiction (p. 135).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Spaced, deadline-driven exams create the scallop pattern of cramming; frequent low-stakes checks or unpredictable quizzes should flatten study effort across the term (pp. 126–127).
- Ratio-style rewards (credit per unit of work completed) drive high output; variable schedules maintain steady effort, but the book also flags that ratio pressure feels stressful, so match to the goal (pp. 125, 130).
- Lower the reinforcement value of competing activities during study and make larger delayed rewards feel nearer (shorten or step up delays), because choices follow relative reinforcement and delay discounting (pp. 132–135).

## Ch 9 — Theories of Reinforcement (pp. 139–154)

**Core ideas**
- A theory of reinforcement must say what makes something a reinforcer and how it strengthens the reinforced response (p. 140). Thorndike's "satisfying state of affairs" labeled rather than explained reinforcers; Skinner focused on prediction and control rather than mechanism (pp. 140–141). A modern neural account ties the S–R strengthening to dopamine signals from unexpected rewards, which also underlies computational reinforcement learning (p. 141).
- Hull's drive-reduction theory held that a stimulus reinforces if it reduces a homeostatic drive; it explained food and water, extended to secondary reinforcers via Pavlovian conditioning and to acquired drives (conditioned fear, drug-cue motivation), but failed to explain sensory reinforcement such as lights, music or watching a toy train, and was abandoned (pp. 141–144).
- Premack's differential-probability principle redefined reinforcers as responses: the opportunity to do a more probable activity reinforces a less probable one, regardless of drives. It became a tool for educational and clinical practice (for example, using a child's preferred repetitive activity to teach arithmetic or language) (pp. 144–145).
- Problems with Premack: response probabilities are hard to measure and unstable, token economies sidestep this by letting learners exchange tokens for varied activities, and the principle tells which activities reinforce but not how reinforcement works (pp. 145–146).
- Response deprivation hypothesis (Timberlake and Allison): restricting access to an activity below its baseline level makes that activity reinforcing; giving unrestricted supply (a week's food for a press) would not work. It needs only a baseline measure and moves the explanation into the constraints of the procedure itself (pp. 146–147).
- Response allocation and behavioral economics treat instrumental procedures as constraints on a behavioral bliss point (preferred distribution of activities). The imposed schedule line cannot pass through the bliss point, so the learner shifts toward it at some cost: more instrumental responding and less reinforcer activity (pp. 148–150).
- How much the learner adjusts depends on elasticity of demand (essential value) and, above all, the availability of substitutes for the reinforcer; with substitutes available, a contingency may have little effect (pp. 150–152). Substitutes may not be evident in advance, so a person's whole range of activities should be monitored when introducing a contingency (p. 152).

**Key terms**: homeostasis, drive state, drive reduction theory, primary reinforcer, secondary (conditioned) reinforcer, acquired drive, sensory reinforcement, Premack (differential probability) principle, token economy, response deprivation hypothesis, response allocation, behavioral economics, behavioral bliss point, equilibrium point, schedule line, disequilibrium, elasticity of demand, essential value, substitute.

**Notable studies / people**: Thorndike on "satisfiers" (p. 140); Skinner (1953) on functional analysis (p. 141); Hull on drive reduction (pp. 141–143); Berlyne (1969) on sensory reinforcement (p. 144); Premack (1965) (p. 144); Charlop et al. (1990) and Hanley et al. (2000) on perseverative behavior as reinforcer (p. 145); Timberlake & Allison (1974) (p. 146); Hursh & Silberberg (2008) on essential value (p. 150); Schultz (1998) and Petter et al. (2018) on dopamine and reinforcement (p. 141).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Find reinforcers by observing what a learner freely does most (Premack), and make that activity contingent on completing the less preferred task; a token system works when preferences are varied or unstable (pp. 145–146).
- Before building a reward contingency, check which substitutes the learner has; if an equally attractive alternative exists, the reward will not increase target behavior much (pp. 151–152).
- Expect trade-offs: a contingency pushes the learner away from their preferred allocation, so keep the required work-to-reward ratio realistic, or the learner may give up the reward rather than do the work (pp. 150–151).

## Ch 10 — Extinction of Conditioned Behavior (pp. 155–171)

**Core ideas**
- Extinction means omitting the US (CS alone) or omitting the reinforcer after a response; it is produced by a procedure, whereas forgetting is a loss over time. It lowers responding, raises response variability, and produces frustration, but frustration only arises when a reward was expected (pp. 156–157).
- Unlearning (Rescorla–Wagner view) may occur partly, but most accounts hold that extinction mainly adds new suppressive learning: CS–no-US or response–no-reward associations, frustration signals, an inhibitory S–R association, reduced attention, or hierarchical negative occasion setting (pp. 157–158, 170).
- Because the original learning survives, extinguished responding can return. Spontaneous recovery follows a rest period; renewal occurs when the context changes (returning to the acquisition context, ABA, or moving to a new one); reinstatement follows exposure to the US alone (pp. 158–163).
- Renewal arises because extinction memory is more context-specific than acquisition memory; the CS becomes ambiguous (signals both US and no-US) and the context disambiguates it, much as sentence context disambiguates a word. Practical lesson: fear learned in one place spreads easily, but therapy gains in an office may not travel (pp. 160–162).
- Ways to make extinction more durable: more extinction trials, spaced trials (longer intertrial intervals reduce spontaneous recovery), extinction in several contexts, reminder cues for the extinction memory (e.g., a spider-phobia study in which recalling the treatment context lowered fear in new settings), and compounding extinction stimuli to magnify the surprise of omitted reward (pp. 163–164).
- Paradoxical effects follow from frustration: after continuous reinforcement, more training (overtraining extinction effect) and larger rewards (magnitude-of-reinforcement effect) cause faster, not slower, extinction (pp. 165–166).
- The partial-reinforcement extinction effect (PREE): intermittent reinforcement leaves behavior far more persistent in extinction than continuous reinforcement (gambling and giving in to tantrums are examples). It is not just poorer detection of change, since the effect persists after a continuous-reinforcement phase (pp. 167–168).
- Two accounts of PREE: frustration theory (the response becomes linked to expecting frustration) and sequential theory (memory of recent nonreward becomes a cue to respond); both rest on S–R learning and both have empirical support. Persistence is undesirable in therapy but valuable in many life domains (pp. 168–170).

**Key terms**: extinction, forgetting, frustration, response variability, inhibitory S–R association, spontaneous recovery, renewal (ABA renewal), reinstatement, negative occasion setter, reminder cue, compound extinction, paradoxical reward effects, overtraining extinction effect, magnitude-of-reinforcement extinction effect, continuous reinforcement (CRF), intermittent/partial reinforcement (PRF), partial-reinforcement extinction effect (PREE), discrimination hypothesis, frustration theory, sequential theory.

**Notable studies / people**: Amsel (1958) on frustration (p. 157); Pavlov on response inhibition and spontaneous recovery (pp. 157–159); Bouton (1993, 2017) on renewal and context (pp. 159–161); Schachtman et al. (1985) on reinstatement with rats (p. 162); Tsao & Craske (2000) and Urcelay et al. (2009) on spacing (p. 163); Mystkowski et al. (2006) on reminder cues in spider-fear therapy (p. 164); Jenkins (1962) and Theios (1962) on testing the discrimination hypothesis (p. 168); Capaldi (1967, 1971) on sequential theory (p. 169); Horsley et al. (2012) on gamblers (p. 167).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- To make a new, correct response replace an old habit, practice it in several settings and spread the practice out, and add a retrieval cue linking back to the setting where the replacement was learned, since extinction-like learning is highly context-bound (pp. 160, 163–164).
- If persistence is wanted (grit with difficult tasks), use intermittent rather than continuous reward after the behavior is established; if you need fast cessation, avoid intermittent rewards before withdrawing the reward (pp. 167–170).
- Expect extinction bursts of variability and frustration when removing reinforcement; plan for relapse after rests or "free" reintroductions of the original reinforcer (pp. 157, 159, 162–163).

## Ch 11 — Punishment (pp. 173–187)

**Core ideas**
- Punishment is an aversive consequence made contingent on a response. It need not be physical (reprimands, fines, time-out, lost privileges count), and punishment research is sparse because of ethical concerns and funding, so findings come mainly from 1960s animal studies (pp. 174–175).
- Thorndike and Skinner claimed punishment was ineffective; systematic work showed that it can quickly produce long-lasting suppression when properly applied (pp. 174, 177).
- Everyday punishment often fails because it is intermittent, delayed, begins mild and escalates, and is signaled by cues telling the person when it is in effect (as in traffic enforcement). It works when it is consistent, immediate, intense from the first offense, and not signaled (as with an electric outlet) (pp. 175–177).
- Key parameters: a strong response–punisher contingency makes suppression specific to the target response (otherwise general fear-based suppression results); delay reduces specificity and depth of suppression; higher intensity suppresses more (pp. 177–178).
- History interacts with intensity: starting with mild punishment can immunize against later intense punishment (habituation), while starting with intense punishment sensitizes the individual to later mild punishment (p. 178).
- Signaled (discriminative) punishment suppresses behavior only in the presence of the signal (e.g., cursing stops around parents only), and a signal that predicts the punisher can overshadow the response so it is blamed instead (pp. 179–180).
- Punishment is a cost–benefit contest against the reinforcement maintaining the response: it works less well if the response is frequently reinforced, and much better when an alternative behavior is positively reinforced (pp. 180–181).
- Paradoxical facilitation occurs when punishment signals attention or other reward, or when the punished act is an escape response from an aversive stimulus (pp. 181–182).
- Punishment given in anger tends to be intermittent, delayed, signaled by the punisher's mood and unaccompanied by alternative reinforcement, so it violates the parameters of effective punishment; alternatives are time-out (loss of access to reinforcement, requiring a real drop in reinforcement) and DRO (scheduled reinforcers cancelled or reset after the target response, delivered otherwise) (pp. 183–186).

**Key terms**: punishment, response–reinforcer contingency and contiguity (for aversive events), habituation to punishment, sensitization to punishment, discriminative punishment, discriminative suppression, escape, negative reinforcement, signal for reinforcement, time-out, differential reinforcement of other behavior (DRO), negative punishment.

**Notable studies / people**: Thorndike (1932) and Skinner (1953) on ineffectiveness (p. 174); Azrin & Holz (1966) and Church (1969) as the main parametric researchers (p. 177); Camp et al. (1967) on contiguity (p. 178); Azrin et al. (1963) and N. E. Miller (1960) on immunization by mild punishment (p. 178); St. Claire-Smith (1979) on a signal blocking punishment (p. 180); Church & Raymond (1967) on punishment and reinforcement rate (p. 180); Holz & Azrin (1961) on punishment as a signal (p. 181); Gershoff (2013, 2016) on broader problems of punishment (p. 184).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Pair any consequence for an unwanted behavior with immediate, consistent delivery and with clear reinforcement of an incompatible desirable behavior; this combination is the book's most robust recipe (pp. 176–177, 181).
- Do not escalate gradually from trivial to severe consequences; mild early consequences breed tolerance (p. 178). Also watch whether attention is given only after misbehavior (p. 181).
- Check that time-out genuinely removes reinforcement (a pleasant room does not) and that the baseline classroom or home setting is itself reinforcing (p. 185).

## Ch 12 — Avoidance Learning (pp. 189–205)

**Core ideas**
- In avoidance conditioning the instrumental response prevents an aversive event. It is adaptive in daily life but excessive avoidance (e.g., compulsive handwashing) can be maladaptive. The central puzzle is how "nothing happening" can reinforce responding, and what role Pavlovian processes play (pp. 189–191).
- Bechterev's finger-withdrawal procedure, meant as Pavlovian, was accidentally instrumental because lifting the finger avoided the shock (p. 191).
- Discriminated (signaled) avoidance uses a warning stimulus; a response during it cancels the shock (avoidance trial) and a late response ends it (escape trial). In shuttle boxes, one-way avoidance is easier than two-way because a consistently safe side exists (pp. 191–193).
- Free-operant (Sidman) avoidance has no warning cue: shocks recur at a shock–shock interval unless a response, which restarts a response–shock safe period. Responses are most beneficial late in the interval, producing a scallop-like pattern (pp. 193–195).
- Two-factor theory (Mowrer): Pavlovian fear is conditioned to the warning signal, and avoidance is instrumentally reinforced by fear reduction when the response ends the signal. Supporting evidence includes reduced avoidance after extinguishing fear of the signal and "escape-from-fear" experiments; contradicting evidence is that fear usually declines as avoidance becomes proficient (the driving example), perhaps because avoidance becomes habitual (pp. 195–198).
- Internal temporal cues can serve as warning stimuli in free-operant avoidance (late-interval cues acquire aversiveness) (p. 198). A third factor, safety signals, treats cues generated by the avoidance response (proprioceptive feedback, added tones) as Pavlovian inhibitors that are themselves reinforcing and help explain persistence after fear has faded (pp. 198–200).
- Extinguishing avoidance is hard: turning off the shock does not work because fear reduction and safety signals still reinforce the response. Success typically needs blocking the avoidance response so the warning cue can be experienced without the outcome (and fear extinguished); safety signals resist extinction and also block fear extinction by making the absence of shock fully predicted (pp. 200–201).
- Avoidance is shaped by built-in defense. Bolles's species-specific defense reactions (freezing, fleeing, fighting) dominate early learning, so responses compatible with them are learned easily; the predatory-imminence continuum links perceived danger (pre-encounter, post-encounter, circa-strike) to different defense modes, which also shape the CRs elicited by warning signals (pp. 201–204).

**Key terms**: avoidance conditioning, escape, discriminated/signaled avoidance, avoidance trial, escape trial, shuttle box, one-way avoidance, two-way avoidance, nondiscriminated/free-operant (Sidman) avoidance, shock–shock (S–S) interval, response–shock (R–S) interval, two-factor theory, negative reinforcement, escape-from-fear experiments, temporal cues, safety signal, proprioceptive cues, response blocking, species-specific defense reaction (SSDR), predatory imminence continuum, pre-encounter, post-encounter, circa-strike.

**Notable studies / people**: Mowrer & Lamoreaux (1942) posed the "nothing reinforces" question (p. 190); Bechterev (1913) (p. 191); Sidman (1953) (p. 193); Mowrer (1947) and N. E. Miller (1951) on two-factor theory (p. 195); Mineka (1979) and Lovibond et al. (2008) on fear falling as avoidance is learned (p. 197); Anger (1963) on temporal cues (p. 198); Dinsmoor (2001) on safety signals (p. 199); Solomon et al. (1953) on avoidance persisting after shock stops (p. 200); Bolles (1970) on SSDRs (p. 202); Fanselow (1994) on predatory imminence (p. 203).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Avoidance of anxiety-provoking tasks (procrastination, test avoidance) is maintained by relief and safety cues even when the feared event no longer occurs; effective change involves staying in contact with the cue and noticing the feared outcome fail to appear, not merely removing the threat (pp. 196, 200–201).
- Safety behaviors and "crutches" can prevent learners from discovering the situation is safe, so fade them deliberately during exposure-style practice (p. 201).
- Teach new avoidance-type responses (e.g., escape-from-error behaviors) in ways compatible with learners' natural defensive reactions and use an easy, clearly safe route first (one-way before two-way logic) (pp. 192–193, 202).

## Ch 13 — Stimulus Control of Behavior (pp. 207–224)

**Core ideas**
- Stimulus control means behavior varies with antecedent stimuli, and appropriate behavior depends on it (hugging is rewarded in some settings and punished in others). Two questions follow: how to measure control, and what determines it (pp. 207–208).
- Control by a stimulus is shown by differential responding when that stimulus is varied. To find which feature matters (e.g., color versus position of a traffic light) vary one dimension while holding others constant, and individuals may differ in which feature controls them (pp. 208–209).
- A stimulus generalization gradient shows how responding changes across values of a dimension; a steep gradient means tight, precise control, a flat one means weak control. Generalization and discrimination are complementary (pp. 209–211).
- Pavlov explained generalization as automatic spread of cortical activation from similar stimuli; Lashley and Wade argued it reflects a failure to learn to tell stimuli apart, so training can limit it (pp. 211–212).
- Stimulus control is limited by sensory capacity, orientation of the sense organ toward the stimulus, and stimulus intensity, with strong cues overshadowing weaker ones. Motivational state biases which cues matter: hungry pigeons attend to visual cues, fearful or avoidance-motivated ones to auditory cues (pp. 212–214).
- Differential reinforcement is the main route to control. Pavlovian or instrumental discrimination training pairs outcomes with an S+ but not an S− (SD and SΔ), and responding first generalizes to S− then sharpens. Multiple schedules arrange different reinforcement schedules in the presence of different signals, so the pattern changes with the signal (as when listening to different instructors) (pp. 214–217).
- Jenkins and Harrison's pigeons trained to discriminate tone from no tone showed a much steeper generalization gradient to untested tone frequencies than undiscriminated controls, so discrimination training raises precision beyond the trained stimuli (pp. 217–219).
- Interdimensional discrimination (stimuli differ in many ways) is easy but yields little control by any single feature; intradimensional discrimination (stimuli differ in one respect, as with the letters E and F) produces precise control and underlies reading and other expert skills, which sensitizes the learner to differences previously unnoticed (pp. 219–221).
- Perceptual learning improves discrimination through mere familiarization (habituating to common elements so distinctive ones stand out), as in learning to taste differences between wines (p. 221). The opposite goal, acquired equivalence, treats different stimuli the same: by giving the same consequence or response to varied examples (pigeons classifying pictures containing water, children saying "fruit"), equivalence classes form, which is basic to concept learning and vocabulary (pp. 222–223).

**Key terms**: stimulus control, differential responding, stimulus dimension, stimulus generalization, generalization gradient, stimulus discrimination, spread of effect, sensory capacity, sensory orientation, stimulus intensity/salience, overshadowing, stimulus filter, discrimination training, S+ (SD), S− (SΔ), multiple schedules, interdimensional discrimination, intradimensional discrimination, perceptual learning, acquired distinctiveness, acquired equivalence, stimulus equivalence class, perceptual concept learning.

**Notable studies / people**: Lashley & Wade (1946) on generalization as learned (p. 212); Foree & LoLordo (1973) on motivation and cue modality (p. 213); Jenkins & Harrison (1960, 1962) on discrimination training and gradient steepness (pp. 217–220); Herrnstein et al. (1976) on pigeon water-concept classification from hundreds of photographs (p. 222); Wasserman (2016) on perceptual concept learning (p. 222); Hall (2001) on perceptual learning (p. 221); Urcuioli (2013) on common responses creating classes (p. 223); Barnes-Holmes et al. (2018) on equivalence and language (p. 223).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- To make a learner sensitive to a fine difference, set up contrasting pairs that differ on only one feature and give different feedback for each; this builds precise control and explains why minimal-pair or contrasting-case practice works (pp. 220–221).
- Use varied examples (different fonts, contexts, examples of one concept) mapped to the same label or response so the concept generalizes beyond the training instances, and test with new unseen exemplars (pp. 222–223).
- Include meaningful exposure to many near-similar items before demanding judgments: familiarization alone can sharpen perception of subtle differences (pp. 221–222). Also remember performance is cue-dependent, so practice under conditions resembling those of eventual use (pp. 207–208, 217).

## Ch 14 — Memory Mechanisms (pp. 225–244)

**Core ideas**
- Learning and memory are inseparable. The difference lies in focus: learning studies vary the conditions of acquisition and hold retention and retrieval constant, while memory studies hold acquisition constant and vary the retention interval and retrieval conditions. Information must be acquired, retained and retrieved (pp. 225–226).
- Matching-to-sample is the workhorse memory task: a sample is shown, a retention interval follows, and the choice matching the sample is reinforced. Start with simultaneous matching (sample still visible) to teach the task, then add delay; sample identity and correct position must vary across trials so that success cannot come from a fixed habit (pp. 227–229).
- Memory types: episodic (specific events: what, when, where), semantic (general facts), and procedural (automatic habits). Animals show the what–where–when structure of episodic memory without proof of conscious recollection (pp. 229–230).
- Working memory holds information needed only for the current trial or task, whereas reference memory holds task features that stay constant across trials and lasts longer (pp. 230–231).
- Memory is not just passive trace decay. Directed-forgetting studies show that remember versus forget cues change later retention (also in pigeons), and training with longer delays produced pigeons whose memory no longer declined over longer test delays, so retention span is a trainable skill (pp. 231–233).
- Working memory may be retrospective (holding the past sample) or prospective (holding the planned future choice or action); symbolic matching-to-sample separates them and pigeons often use prospective coding, with the strategy chosen by task efficiency (pp. 233–235).
- True forgetting is hard to prove; failures more often reflect poor encoding, interference or retrieval failure. Proactive interference (earlier material disrupts later) increases when trials are massed and reflects remembering too much, while retroactive interference (later material disrupts earlier) seems to impair retrieval or rehearsal (pp. 235–237).
- Retrieval failure is shown by reminder cues restoring performance; even incidental background cues present during training (the crib liner in Borovsky and Rovee-Collier's infant mobile study) can retrieve the memory 24 hours later, and retrieval cues can reverse extinction, latent inhibition, overshadowing and blocking effects (pp. 237–239).
- Consolidation turns experience into lasting memory over time at cellular, synaptic and circuit levels, with a window of vulnerability shortly after learning. Contemporary work shows that retrieved memories become labile and are reconsolidated, so repeated recall can distort them (false memories) but also allows useful updating, particularly when the reactivated memory meets prediction error or new information (pp. 239–241).

**Key terms**: acquisition, retention, retrieval, matching-to-sample, simultaneous matching, delayed matching, retention interval, episodic memory, semantic memory, procedural memory, working memory, reference memory, trace decay, directed forgetting (remember/forget cues), retrospective memory, prospective memory, symbolic matching, proactive interference, retroactive interference, retrieval cue, consolidation, consolidation window, reconsolidation, memory updating.

**Notable studies / people**: Grant (1976) on delay and memory difficulty (p. 228); Sargisson & White (2001) on training with longer delays (pp. 232–233); Milmine et al. (2008) on directed forgetting in pigeons (p. 232); Roitblat (1980) and Santi & Roberts (1985) on prospective coding in symbolic matching (p. 235); Borovsky & Rovee-Collier (1990) on playpen-liner retrieval cues in 6-month-old infants (pp. 237–239); Edhouse & White (1988) on proactive interference from massed trials (p. 237); Clayton and colleagues (2007) and Crystal (2021) on animal episodic-like memory (p. 230); McGaugh (2000) on consolidation disruption (p. 239); Nader & Hardt (2009) and Lee et al. (2017) on reconsolidation (p. 240).

**Use for teaching and studying** (own inference)
- Retention and retrieval are separate stages: if a learner "forgot," check retrieval before re-teaching, and supply or rehearse reminder cues that were present at learning; plan practice under varied cue conditions so that retrieval cues are not tied to one setting (pp. 236–239).
- Mark what must be remembered (the R cue) and expect to be tested: telling learners what will be needed later improves retention, and training with longer delays builds longer retention (pp. 232–233).
- Avoid massing confusable items in immediate succession, which breeds proactive interference, and avoid interference during delays right after learning; use retrieval as an occasion to correct and update, remembering that each recall can modify the memory (pp. 237, 240–241).
- Scaffold tasks as the matching procedure does: start with the sample visible (support), then introduce delay, and vary examples so that success cannot come from a fixed pattern (pp. 228–229).

## Cross-cutting takeaways for teaching and learning

1. **Immediate, specific feedback beats delayed feedback; bridge unavoidable delays.** Reinforcement must follow the target response at once, or other responses intervene; conditioned reinforcers (tokens) and marking stimuli rescue delayed outcomes (Ch 7, pp. 110–111; same logic for punishment, Ch 11, p. 178).
2. **Teach complex skills by shaping.** Define the target, assess the starting repertoire, and reinforce successive approximations, building components (pedal, steer, balance) in order (Ch 7, pp. 107–109).
3. **Learning is driven by prediction error, so make learners predict.** Cues already known to predict an outcome block learning about redundant cues; surprise drives learning (Ch 5, pp. 69–70; Ch 6, pp. 89–91).
4. **Performance is not learning; test indirectly and with transfer.** Learning can be silent, and value-change (devaluation) tests reveal what is actually represented (Ch 1, pp. 6–7; Ch 6, pp. 85–86; Ch 7, pp. 112–113).
5. **Context binds what is learned, especially new "unlearning"-type learning.** Extinction is context-specific and can renew, spontaneously recover or be reinstated; vary contexts, space trials, and use reminder cues to make change durable (Ch 10, pp. 159–164).
6. **Schedules shape effort patterns, and intermittent reward builds persistence.** Ratio schedules drive high rates, interval schedules produce scalloped cramming, and partial reinforcement makes behavior resistant to extinction (Ch 8, pp. 124–130; Ch 10, pp. 167–170).
7. **Reinforcement works within the learner's whole activity budget.** Premack-style contingencies and response deprivation use preferred activities as rewards; substitutes available to the learner can defeat a contingency (Ch 9, pp. 144–152; Ch 8, p. 133).
8. **Contrastive, minimal-difference practice builds fine discrimination; varied exemplars build concepts.** Intradimensional discrimination creates precision, and the same label across varied examples builds equivalence classes (Ch 13, pp. 219–223).
9. **Familiarization alone sharpens perception.** Exposure to many similar items (perceptual learning) improves fine distinctions without explicit feedback (Ch 13, pp. 221–222).
10. **Delay discounting links to self-control; lengthen delays gradually.** Training with gradually increasing delays and with inhibition, time estimation and future thinking raises preference for larger-later rewards (Ch 8, pp. 134–135).
11. **Punishment only works when immediate, consistent, and paired with reinforced alternatives; anger-driven punishment violates all of this.** Time-out and DRO are systematic alternatives that depend on real reinforcement loss or delivery (Ch 11, pp. 176–186).
12. **Memory failure is usually retrieval or interference rather than decay.** Reminder cues, incidental background cues and avoiding massed confusable items matter (Ch 14, pp. 235–239).
13. **Retention is trainable and cue-controlled.** Remember/forget cues and longer training delays alter retention; telling learners what will be tested helps (Ch 14, pp. 232–233).
14. **Retrieval changes memories.** Reactivated memories are modifiable, supporting both useful updating and distortion; surprise (prediction error) is the trigger for updating (Ch 14, pp. 240–241; Ch 6, pp. 89–91).
15. **Habits arise from extensive repetition and run on cues without thinking about goals.** Early learning is goal-directed; to maintain flexibility, vary conditions and keep outcomes meaningful (Ch 7, pp. 112–114).
