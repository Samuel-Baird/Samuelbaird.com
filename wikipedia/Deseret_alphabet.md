The **Deseret alphabet** ([/ˌdɛzəˈrɛt/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:En-us-Deseret.ogg "File:En-us-Deseret.ogg"); Deseret: 𐐔𐐯𐑅𐐨𐑉𐐯𐐻 or 𐐔𐐯𐑆𐐲𐑉𐐯𐐻) is a [phonemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme "Phoneme") [English-language spelling reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-language_spelling_reform "English-language spelling reform") developed between 1847 and 1854 by the [board of regents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_regents "Board of regents") of the [University of Deseret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Deseret "University of Deseret") under the leadership of [Brigham Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young "Brigham Young"), the second [president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Church_(LDS_Church) "President of the Church (LDS Church)") of [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints "The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints") (LDS Church). [George D. Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_D._Watt "George D. Watt") is reported to have been the most actively involved in the development of the script's novel characters, which were used to replace those of the 1847 version of [Isaac Pitman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Pitman "Isaac Pitman")'s [English phonotypic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Phonotypic_Alphabet "English Phonotypic Alphabet"). He was also the "New Alphabet's" first serious user. The script gets its name from the word [*deseret*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deseret_(Book_of_Mormon) "Deseret (Book of Mormon)"), a [term that occurs only once](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hapax_legomenon "Hapax legomenon") in the [Book of Mormon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Mormon "Book of Mormon"), which is said to mean "[honeybee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeybee "Honeybee")" in the only verse it is used in. This reformation of English orthography was believed by its creators to be a first step to the ultimate restoration of [Adamic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamic_language "Adamic language").

The Deseret alphabet was an outgrowth of the [Restorationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restorationism "Restorationism") [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_(ethics) "Ideal (ethics)") and [utopianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopianism "Utopianism") of Young and the early LDS Church. The "New Alphabet" was intended to correct "the corruptions and perversions of language which was originally pure", and to meet the urgent need for a language to "answer the demands of a constant intercommunication between several thousand languages". One "fitted to meet the great emergency of the great gathering and great work of teaching the law of the Lord to all people."

Young and the [Mormon pioneers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon_pioneers "Mormon pioneers") believed "all aspects of life" were in need of reform for the imminent [Millennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming_in_Mormonism#The_Millennium "Second Coming in Mormonism"), and the Deseret alphabet was just one of many ways in which they sought to bring about a complete "[transformation in society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_change "Social change")", in anticipation of the [Second Coming of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming_of_Jesus "Second Coming of Jesus").

In public statements, Young claimed the alphabet would replace the traditional [Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet "Latin alphabet") with an alternative, more [phonetically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonetics "Phonetics") accurate alphabet for the [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language"). This would offer immigrants an opportunity to learn to read and write English, the [orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthography "Orthography") of which, he said, is often less phonetically consistent than those of many other languages. Young also proposed teaching the alphabet in the school system, stating "It will be the means of introducing uniformity in our orthography, and the years that are now required to learn to read and spell can be devoted to other studies."

Between 1854 and 1869, the alphabet was used in scriptural newspaper passages, selected church records, a few diaries, and some correspondence. Occasional street signs and posters used the new letters. In 1860 a $5 gold coin was embossed 𐐐𐐬𐑊𐐨𐑌𐐮𐑅 𐐻𐐭 𐑄 𐐢𐐫𐑉𐐼 (Holiness to the Lord). In 1868–9, after much difficulty creating suitable fonts, four books were printed: two school primers, the full [Book of Mormon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Mormon "Book of Mormon"), and a first portion of it, intended as a third school reader.

Despite repeated and costly promotion by the early LDS Church, the alphabet never enjoyed widespread use, and it has been regarded by historians as a failure. However, in recent years, aided by [digital typography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_typography "Digital typography"), the Deseret alphabet has been revived as a cultural [heirloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heirloom "Heirloom").

## History

### Creation (1847–1854)

The Deseret alphabet was a project of the [Mormon pioneers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon_pioneers "Mormon pioneers"), a group of early followers of [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints "The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints") (LDS Church) who, motivated by [revelations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation "Revelation") of a unique [premillennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premillennialism "Premillennialism") [eschatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschatology "Eschatology"), had set about building a unique [theocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theocracy "Theocracy") in the Utah desert, which was then still claimed by [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico "Mexico"), after the death of the church's founder, the [prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet "Prophet") [Joseph Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Smith "Joseph Smith"). They were to build a "city of Zion" where converts would gather in preparation for the Second Coming of Christ. As part of that [Gathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gathering_of_Israel "Gathering of Israel"), in 1848, Church leaders urged converts in Europe to "emigrate as speedily as possible" to the Great Basin. There, in the ["Kingdom of God"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_God_(Latter_Day_Saints) "Kingdom of God (Latter Day Saints)"), under fused [theo-democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theo-democratic "Theo-democratic") leadership, they would be safe from the fall of the [apostate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostasy "Apostasy") world of so-called "Babylon".

March 6, 1849, Church authorities organized the "free and independent government" called the [State of Deseret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Deseret "State of Deseret"), while retaining the [Council of Fifty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Fifty "Council of Fifty"). In that historical context, which has been called "The Forgotten Kingdom", there was a "compete identity of religious and temporal purpose throughout the history of the Alphabet." This theo-linguistic fusion has been noted by multiple historians.

Young wrote of the reform that "with a very few additions, it is believed, it would represent every sound used in the construction of any known language; and, in fact, a step and partial return to a pure language which has been promised unto us in the latter days", which meant the pure [Adamic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamic_language "Adamic language") spoken before the [Tower of Babel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_Babel "Tower of Babel"). The Deseret Typographical Association called the alphabet "a forerunner in that series of developments which shall prepare mankind for the reception of pure language". Brigham Young, Church President and Prophet, the "driving force" for the reform, looked forward to the time "when a man is full of light of eternity", and stated, "I shall yet see the time that I can converse with this people without opening my mouth."

The Deseret alphabet was developed primarily by a committee made up of the board of regents of the [University of Deseret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Deseret "University of Deseret"), members of which included LDS Church leaders Brigham Young, [Parley P. Pratt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parley_P._Pratt "Parley P. Pratt"), [Heber C. Kimball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heber_C._Kimball "Heber C. Kimball"), and several of the other [Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostle_(Latter_Day_Saints) "Apostle (Latter Day Saints)"). According to [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University "Brigham Young University") professor Richard G. Moore, most scholars believe that [George D. Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_D._Watt "George D. Watt")'s contribution to the actual form the alphabet took, its unique [glyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyph "Glyph"), was the greatest; he furthermore "plant[ed] the idea of [spelling reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spelling_reform "Spelling reform") in Brigham Young's mind" through a [phonography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitman_shorthand "Pitman shorthand") class he gave after the [death of Joseph Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Joseph_Smith "Death of Joseph Smith") which Young attended. [William W. Phelps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._W._Phelps_(Mormon) "W. W. Phelps (Mormon)") helped "work out the letters" along with Pratt.

The Deseret alphabet was based on the 1847 version of [Isaac Pitman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Pitman "Isaac Pitman")'s *[English Phonotypic Alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Phonotypic_Alphabet "English Phonotypic Alphabet")*, and in fact, Pitman's alphabet was nearly chosen by the Board of Regents as their preferred spelling reform.

Before they decided on the Deseret alphabet, the attention of the board of regents was mostly focused on [Pitman style alphabets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Phonotypic_Alphabet "English Phonotypic Alphabet"), and in April 1847 Brigham Young nearly purchased 200 pounds (91 kg) of [lead type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sort_(typesetting) "Sort (typesetting)") to print books using Pitman's orthography. The University of Deseret was incorporated on 28 February 1850; less than three weeks later, on 20 March, the new board of regents began to discuss spelling reform.

On 29 November 1853, the committee was ready to approve a slightly modified version of the Pitman orthography, when [Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostle_(Latter_Day_Saints) "Apostle (Latter Day Saints)") [Willard Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Richards "Willard Richards"), [Second Counselor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Presidency_(LDS_Church) "First Presidency (LDS Church)") to Young, who had been deathly ill and missed the debate before the vote, saw the proposed alphabet, which spelled the word "phonetic" as "fɷnetic". Richards was quick to condemn it, saying to the committee: "We want a new kind of alphabet...those characters...seem like putting [new wine into old bottles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_wine_into_old_bottles "New wine into old bottles")...I am inclined to think...we shall...throw away all characters that bear much resemblance to the English characters, and introduce an alphabet that is original...an alphabet entirely different from any alphabet in use."

These words persuaded Brigham Young and the rest of the committee, and Watt then endeavored to create an original alphabet. Less than two months later, on 19 January 1854, the board of regents finally approved the first 38-letter Deseret alphabet. One legacy of Pitman's orthography survived, though: the idea that [one letter should equal one sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonemic_orthography "Phonemic orthography").

### Use by the Mormon pioneers (1854–1869)

Upon the alphabet's acceptance, its first user was its principal architect, George D. Watt, who began writing the meeting minutes of the early [Bishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_(Latter_Day_Saints) "Bishop (Latter Day Saints)") in a cursive form of it in 1854. Almost immediately after its publication, church members began experimenting with it, and by 1855 travel writers Jules Remy and [Julius Brenchley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Brenchley "Julius Brenchley") published a chart of the new alphabet which differed heavily from the 1854 version. Some early Mormons, such as [Thales Hastings Haskell](/w/index.php?title=Thales_Hastings_Haskell&action=edit&redlink=1 "Thales Hastings Haskell (page does not exist)"), began writing their personal journals in the new alphabet. Remy further reported that during his time in [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City "Salt Lake City"), he saw signs on the street and above shops using the new alphabet.

After its approval by the board of regents, Brigham Young testified before the [Utah territorial legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_Legislature "Utah Legislature") that the new alphabet should "be thoroughly and extensively taught in all the schools". Some teaching in Utah schools did take place: John B. Milner taught the alphabet in [Provo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provo,_Utah "Provo, Utah"), [Lehi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lehi,_Utah "Lehi, Utah"), [American Fork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Fork,_Utah "American Fork, Utah"), and [Pleasant Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleasant_Grove,_Utah "Pleasant Grove, Utah"), while evening classes were taught in Salt Lake City and [Farmington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmington,_Utah "Farmington, Utah").

After several months' practice writing with the new alphabet, Watt wrote to Brigham Young that he was unhappy with it, and proposed a complete overhaul, which was never followed up on.

Word of the new alphabet soon spread outside Utah, and most press reports in non-Mormon papers were critical. Other writers, however, acquainted with other phonotypic and stenographic alphabets, ranged from neutral descriptions of the new alphabet to praise.

Until this point, all the printed material (mostly just charts of the alphabet and its standard orthography equivalents) had been produced with large [wooden type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_type "Wood type"), which was not suitable for printing at small sizes. Because the alphabet was wholly unique, no font existed, so in 1857 the board of regents appointed [Erastus Snow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erastus_Snow "Erastus Snow") to procure metal type from [St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis "St. Louis")–based [font foundry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Font_foundry "Font foundry") Ladew & Peer. However, in May 1857 the [Utah War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_War "Utah War") began, and Snow left St. Louis to support the Mormon pioneers. During the war, Ladew & Peer kept working on the type, and the [punches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punchcutting "Punchcutting") and [matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_(printing) "Matrix (printing)") were delivered in the winter of 1858. The first use of the new type was to make a business card for [George A. Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_A._Smith "George A. Smith"), an early [Mormon historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon_historian "Mormon historian").

The [Sermon on the Mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon_on_the_Mount "Sermon on the Mount") as it appears in the 16 February 1859 edition of the *[Deseret News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deseret_News "Deseret News")*

In 1859, with the new type in hand, the *Deseret News* began printing with it. It would print one piece per issue in the new alphabet, usually a quotation from *[The Book of Mormon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_Mormon "The Book of Mormon")* or the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament"). However, this only lasted for one year, after which the practice stopped; it would start again in May 1864 and stop permanently at the end of that year.

The covers of two [primers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primer_(textbook) "Primer (textbook)") published in the Deseret alphabet during the life of Brigham Young, the *Deseret First Book* and the *Deseret Second Book*.

Their inscriptions read:

𐐜 𐐔𐐇𐐝𐐀𐐡𐐇𐐓 𐐙𐐊𐐡𐐝𐐓/𐐝𐐇𐐗𐐊𐐤𐐔 𐐒𐐋𐐗 𐐒𐐌 𐐜 𐐡𐐀𐐖𐐇𐐤𐐓𐐝 𐐱𐑂 𐑄 𐐔𐐇𐐝𐐀𐐡𐐇𐐓 𐐏𐐆𐐅𐐤𐐆𐐚𐐊𐐡𐐝𐐆𐐓𐐆 1868.

THE DESERET FIRST/SECOND BOOK BY THE REGENTS of the DESERET UNIVERSITY 1868.

[Benn Pitman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benn_Pitman "Benn Pitman"), the brother of Isaac Pitman, was also interested in spelling reform, and by 1864 had published his own orthography, which the board of regents considered adopting. However, they ultimately decided not to and used the opportunity to re-affirm their commitment to the Deseret alphabet.

Brigham Young blamed the failure of this first attempt at reform on the ugliness of the type developed by Ladew & Peer, and so he commissioned Russell's American Steam Printing House, a [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City") based font foundry, to design more pleasing type. The result was the [Bodoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodoni "Bodoni")-esque font (below) that was used to print all of the books in this period. In an 1868 article, the *Deseret News* wrote that "the characters, to a person unaccustomed to them, may look strange, [but] to the eye to which they are familiar they are beautiful."

At least four books were published in the new alphabet, all transcribed by [Orson Pratt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orson_Pratt "Orson Pratt") and all using the Russell's House font: *The First Deseret Alphabet Reader* (1868), *The Second Deseret Alphabet Reader* (1868), *The Book of Mormon* (1869), and a *Book of Mormon* excerpt called *[First Nephi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Book_of_Nephi "First Book of Nephi")–[Omni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Omni "Book of Omni")* (1869).

Considerable non-printed material in the Deseret alphabet was made, including a replica headstone in [Cedar City, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cedar_City,_Utah "Cedar City, Utah"), some coinage, letters, diaries, and meeting minutes. One of the more curious items found in the Deseret alphabet is an English-[Hopi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopi_language "Hopi language") dictionary prepared by two Mormon missionaries. The handwritten document sat in the [LDS Church Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_History_Library "Church History Library"), largely ignored until 2014 when [writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system "Writing system") researcher and computer scientist Kenneth R. Beesley re-discovered it and transcribed it into standard written English.

### Decline (1869–1877)

The final book the Mormon pioneers printed in the Deseret alphabet: a three part *Book of Mormon*. On left, the cover of volume one; on right, the Deseret alphabet chart in the book.

Despite years of heavy promotion, the Deseret alphabet was never widely adopted. This reluctance was partly due to prohibitive costs; the project had already cost the early church $20,000, with $6,000 going to Pratt as remuneration for his transcription effort and most of the rest going to cutting metal type featuring the new alphabet and printing costs. In 1859, Orson Pratt estimated that the cost of supplying all [Utah Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_Territory "Utah Territory") [schoolchildren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_education_in_the_United_States#Federal_era "History of education in the United States") with suitable textbooks would be over $5,000,000.

An 1876 [campaign ticket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticket_(election) "Ticket (election)") for the [People's Party of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People%27s_Party_of_Utah "People's Party of Utah"). The Deseret type is recycled to make a border. The "words" in the border are [gibberish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibberish "Gibberish").

According to Beesley, many have written that interest in the Deseret alphabet died with Brigham Young. This, however, is not true; the alphabet was already regarded as a failure during Young's time. Only 500 copies of the full *Book of Mormon* translated into the Deseret alphabet sold for $2 each, and even Young realized that the venture was too expensive and even the most devout Mormons could not be convinced to purchase and study the Deseret edition books over the books in the traditional orthography. In the winter of 1870, just one year after their publication, advertisements for the Deseret alphabet books were quietly removed from the *Deseret News*.

Contemporary writers noted that thousands of copies of the 15¢ and 20¢ Deseret primers went unsold, and historian Roby Wentz speculated that the LDS Church at that time had a "cache" of the primers in mint condition, which it was slowly selling off; according to him, one such primer sold for $250 in 1978.

The Mormons had planned to use the profits from sale of the earlier books to fund printing of more books, and in anticipation Orson Pratt had already transcribed the complete [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible"), *[Doctrine and Covenants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine_and_Covenants "Doctrine and Covenants")*, and [John Jaques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Jaques_(Mormon) "John Jaques (Mormon)")'s *Catechism for Children*. Pratt had also prepared an apparent sequel to the primers, the *Deseret Phonetic Speller*. After the sales failure, however, none of these books were ever published and were thought lost until being rediscovered in a storage area of the LDS Church Archives in Salt Lake City in May 1967.

Ralph Vigoda, a reporter for *[The Philadelphia Inquirer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Philadelphia_Inquirer "The Philadelphia Inquirer")*, has speculated that the completion of the [Transcontinental railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcontinental_railroad "Transcontinental railroad") may have contributed to the alphabet's downfall: non-Mormons, not loyal to Brigham Young, became a large part of the city, and without the religious motivation it would be difficult indeed to get them to learn a new alphabet. In a retrospective piece, historian A. J. Simmonds claims that the new railroad doomed the alphabet. According to him, easy access to "the whole literature of the English speaking world" rendered the alphabet useless.

In July 1877, Young tried one more time at a spelling reform, ordering lead type designed for the orthography of [Benn Pitman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benn_Pitman "Benn Pitman") (Isaac's brother) with the intention of printing an edition of the *Book of Mormon* and *Doctrine and Covenants* using it. Most of the type had arrived by August, but with Young's death, the translation was never undertaken and the type never used. Young's death thus marked the end of the Mormon experimentation with English spelling reforms.

### Rediscovery in the computer era

Three questions ("Where is my room?", "Where is the beach?" and "Where is the bar?") in a Deseret digital computer typeface

Modern [digital typography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_typography "Digital typography") has reduced the costs of typesetting substantially, especially for small print runs. [Freely licensed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_content "Free content") Deseret alphabet fonts can be used at no additional cost.

Film director [Trent Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trent_Harris_(director) "Trent Harris (director)") used the Deseret alphabet in his 1994 satire of Mormon theology, *[Plan 10 from Outer Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_10_from_Outer_Space "Plan 10 from Outer Space")*, where it features as an alien language used on a mysterious "Plaque of [Kolob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolob "Kolob")".

During the 1996 Utah Centennial celebration, an [activity book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_book "Activity book") for children was distributed, within which one of the activities was for a child to write their own name in the alphabet. The book says that a child who does this will be "the first kid in 100 years to write [their] name in the Deseret alphabet!"

Also in 1996, *Buffalo River Press* published a reprint of the *Deseret First Book*, of which only 10,000 were originally printed. The entire *Book of Mormon* in the Deseret alphabet has been likewise reprinted, as only 500 copies from the original print run exist, and they can sell on eBay for ≈$7,500 (as of 2004). In 1997, John Jenkins [uploaded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_publishing "Electronic publishing") a free three part [PDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portable_Document_Format "Portable Document Format") of the so-called "triple combination", that is, a combined *Book of Mormon*, *Doctrine and Covenants* and *[Pearl of Great Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_of_Great_Price_(Mormonism) "Pearl of Great Price (Mormonism)")*.

John Jenkins has gone on to publish many classic pieces of [English literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_literature "English literature") in the Deseret alphabet, such as *[Alice in Wonderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_in_Wonderland "Alice in Wonderland"),* *[Pride and Prejudice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pride_and_Prejudice "Pride and Prejudice")*, and *[The Wonderful Wizard of Oz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wonderful_Wizard_of_Oz "The Wonderful Wizard of Oz").*

Owing to the character set's inclusion in Unicode, most of the original books and many of the original manuscripts have been transcribed into [plain text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_text "Plain text"), and, when this is not possible due to discrepancies between the Unicode reference glyphs and the documents, [LaTeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaTeX "LaTeX").

#### Fonts

The phrase "𐐆𐑌𐑁𐐲𐑉𐑋𐐩𐑇𐐲𐑌 𐐶𐐪𐑌𐐻𐑅 𐐻𐐭 𐐺 𐑁𐑉𐐨" ([Information wants to be free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_wants_to_be_free "Information wants to be free")) in five Deseret fonts. From top, Noto Sans Deseret, QueenBee Star, TuBeeRound, Times Bee and Analecta.

The first digital font for the Deseret alphabet, called "Deseret", was designed by Greg Kearney as part of work he was doing for the [LDS Church History Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_History_Library "Church History Library") in 1991; the font was used in an [exhibit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhibit_design "Exhibit design") that year. In August 1995, a cleaned up, digitized version of the font in use in the *Deseret Second Book* was created by Salt Lake City graphic designer Edward Bateman, who made the font in [Fontographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fontographer "Fontographer") while working on *[Plan 10 from Outer Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_10_from_Outer_Space "Plan 10 from Outer Space")*.

Kenneth R. Beesley created a [Metafont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafont "Metafont") (and thus, LaTeX-compatible) font called [desalph](https://github.com/ctrlcctrlv/desalph) in 2002.

All computers running [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft "Microsoft")'s [Windows 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_7 "Windows 7") operating system or newer can display the entire Deseret alphabet Unicode range as the glyphs are included in the [Segoe UI Symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segoe#Variations "Segoe") font.

Besides maintaining a Deseret [input method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input_method "Input method") for Windows, Joshua Erickson, a [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA "UCLA") alumnus, also maintains a large collection of [freeware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeware "Freeware") Unicode fonts for the alphabet, which he collectively terms the "Bee Fonts".

There also exist [free software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_software "Free software") fonts for the Deseret alphabet. [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google "Google"), through its [Noto Sans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noto_Sans "Noto Sans") project, the aim of which is "to support all languages with a harmonious look and feel", has also released a Deseret font under the name "Noto Sans Deseret". George Douros maintained a [public domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_domain "Public domain") font called "Analecta" until 2022 as part of his [Unicode Fonts for Ancient Scripts](/w/index.php?title=Unicode_Fonts_for_Ancient_Scripts&action=edit&redlink=1 "Unicode Fonts for Ancient Scripts (page does not exist)") project, which supports the [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_language "Coptic language"), [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_language "Gothic language"), and Deseret scripts and is still available on [archive.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archive.org "Archive.org"). Deseret glyphs are also available in the popular pan-Unicode fonts [Code2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code2000 "Code2000") and [Everson Mono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everson_Mono "Everson Mono") (as of version 5.1.5).

A degree of free spelling is allowed to accommodate dialectal differences in English. For example, in the Deseret edition of *The Book of Mormon*, the word "wherefore" is written as 𐐸𐐶𐐩𐑉𐑁𐐬𐑉 ([/hwɛərfoʊr/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")), which means that the translator of the book did not exhibit the [wine–whine merger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine%E2%80%93whine_merger "Wine–whine merger"). Those who do exhibit the merger might instead prefer the spelling 𐐶𐐯𐑉𐑁𐐬𐑉 to match the pronunciation ([/wɛrfoʊr/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")), or, depending on [dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_dialects_of_the_English_language#United_States "List of dialects of the English language"), perhaps 𐐶𐐯𐑉𐑁𐐫𐑉 ([/wɛrfɔːr/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")).

The alphabet was designed to be able to write all of the vowels used in the dialect spoken in 19th century Utah. The vowel inventory has also been attributed to the fact that, unlike other [American pioneers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_pioneer "American pioneer"), the Mormon pioneers were from [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England "New England") as opposed to the [American South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_United_States "Southern United States"). As such, many of the vowels in the Deseret alphabet have since [merged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_change "Phonological change") in the modern era: they are no longer distinguished in some dialects of English, particularly dialects of US English, though are still present in others, such as many varieties of British English.

Speakers who exhibit the [father–bother merger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Father%E2%80%93bother_merger "Father–bother merger") no longer distinguish [/ɑː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") (𐐪) and [/ɒ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") (𐐱), and so both "father" and "bother" would be written with 𐐪: as 𐑁𐐪𐑄𐐲𐑉 and 𐐺𐐪𐑄𐐲𐑉 as opposed to 𐑁𐐪𐑄𐐲𐑉 and 𐐺𐐱𐑄𐐲𐑉. For those with the [cot–caught merger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cot%E2%80%93caught_merger "Cot–caught merger"), [/ɔː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") (𐐫) and [/ɒ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") (𐐱) are no longer distinguished: both "cot" and "caught" are thus written by them as 𐐿𐐱𐐻 ([/kɒt/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")) in the case of [North American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_English "North American English"), and as 𐐿𐐫𐐻 ([/kɔːt/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")) in the case of [Scottish English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_English "Scottish English"). For those exhibiting both mergers, both would be written 𐐿𐐪𐐻 ([/kɑːt/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")).

### Versions

There have been several published versions of the alphabet. Most versions (including the versions used in *The Deseret First Book*, *The Deseret Second Book*, *The Deseret News* and *The Book of Mormon*) had only 38 letters, but some versions contained two [ligatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typographic_ligature "Typographic ligature"), 𐐧 (ew) and 𐐦 (oi). In place of 𐐮𐐭 or 𐐷𐐭, 𐑏 was to be used; in place of 𐐱𐐮, 𐑎.

In the 23 February 1859 edition of the *Deseret News*, the editors announced their approval of the two new letters and eventual intention to use them in the newsletter. However, due to the [hot metal typesetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_metal_typesetting "Hot metal typesetting") technology in use at the time, casting the new letters for use would have been a considerable expense, so it was never realized.

### Representation of [ə]

The Deseret alphabet does not have a distinct symbol for the [mid central vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid_central_vowel "Mid central vowel") ([[ə](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C6%8F "Ə")], "schwa"). The lack of a schwa has been cited as the biggest "[phonological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological "Phonological") flaw" in the alphabet.

Shelton using his [schwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwa "Schwa") to handwrite the word "broken" (𐐺𐑉𐐬𐐿ı𐑌) ([/ˈbroʊkən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")). The standard way to render this word is 𐐺𐑉𐐬𐐿𐑌 ([/ˈbroʊkn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")).

Because of the lack of a schwa, the author must write the sound that would be used if the syllable was stressed. For example, the word *enough* is commonly pronounced [/əˈnʌf/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), but when it is stressed (as in a declaration of irritation) it is pronounced [/iˈnʌf/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"). The Deseret spelling of the word, 𐐨𐑌𐐲𐑁, reflects that stressed pronunciation. If [[ə](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C6%8F "Ə")] does not have an inherent stressed value in a word, as is often the case before [/r/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), then it is written as 𐐲.

[Marion Jackson Shelton](/w/index.php?title=Marion_Jackson_Shelton&action=edit&redlink=1 "Marion Jackson Shelton (page does not exist)"), an early [Mormon missionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon_missionary "Mormon missionary"), proposed the addition of a new glyph to represent the schwa, a simple vertical line of the same height as other Deseret characters with a similar appearance to the [Turkish dotless i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_dotless_i "Turkish dotless i") (ı). The addition of this glyph did not catch on among his contemporaries, however, and no document outside of ones penned by Shelton makes use of it. Shelton used the new glyph in an 1860 letter to Brigham Young reporting on a recently completed mission to the [Paiute people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Paiute "Southern Paiute").

### Syllabic values

Each letter in the Deseret alphabet has a name, and when a letter is written on its own it has the value of that name. This allows some short words to be written with a single letter, and is called a letter's "syllabic value". The most common word in English, *the*, is written simply 𐑄, as the letter's name is [/ðiː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") and that is the stressed pronunciation of the word. The consonants with syllabic values are 𐐶 (woo), 𐐷 (yee), 𐐸 (ha), 𐐹 (pee), 𐐺 (be/bee), 𐐻 (tee/tea), 𐐽 (qi), 𐐾 (jee), 𐑀 (gay), and 𐑄 (the/thee).

Syllabic values do not apply within words, although this was formerly the case. In early documents, Watt writes "people" as 𐐹𐐹𐑊 with the expectation that readers will interpret the first 𐐹 as [/piː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), but the second 𐐹 as [/p/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"). This contextual value switching was soon done away with, so in later documents, while "bee" is written 𐐺, "bees" is written 𐐺𐐨𐑆.

In 40-letter versions of the alphabet which include the letter 𐐧 (ew) which represents [/juː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), the letter 𐐧 when standing alone can be used to represent the word "you".

## Handwriting

There were two main handwritten forms of the Deseret alphabet: a [cursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive "Cursive") version and a [printed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_letters "Block letters") version. Over the lifetime of the alphabet, the cursive form fell out of favor among most users of the alphabet and by 1856 no more cursive documents exist. Its impact on the [glyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyph "Glyph") can however still be plainly seen in the loops of certain characters such as 𐑅, 𐑀 and 𐐼. The earliest surviving versions of the Deseret alphabet, from 1853 (one year before its January 1854 approval), have printed and cursive forms side-by-side, suggesting that a cursive form was part of the plan from the very beginning.

### Cursive

The cursive form of the Deseret alphabet was mainly used by two people: George D. Watt, and James Henry Martineau. Watt, a [stenographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stenographer "Stenographer"), recorded several bishops meetings and wrote other personal documents in this cursive style. A chart of the cursive form appears below. The blue glyphs represent how to write each character, while the top row of printed glyphs shows the corresponding Unicode reference glyph.

The cursive style has many unorthodox characteristics uncommon to [alphabetic writing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet "Alphabet"). [Vowels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel "Vowel") can be dropped if the writer is in a hurry and feels the word is obvious as in an [abjad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abjad "Abjad"), letters can be written above or below the base line depending on what precedes them, and 𐐮 is placed on letters after they are already written as in an [abugida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abugida "Abugida"). Furthermore, unlike the typeset alphabet, the cursive alphabet has no letter case. These characteristics could have arisen because Watt was a local expert in [Pitman shorthand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitman_shorthand "Pitman shorthand"), which is written in a similar way.

### Block letters

George D. Watt found his own alphabet cumbersome to write and abandoned it. As he wrote to Brigham Young on 21 August 1854:

> Dear Bro. I herein submit for your examination the result of much thought and extensive practice on the new alphabet since the Board of Regents last met. **I candidly confess that I never did like the present construction of the alphabet.** I was not left as free as I could have wished to be in the construction of it. [...] I am now thoroughly convinced that it is not the most expeditious method of writing and printing, but on the contrary it retards the hand in its onward course.

His new alphabet closely resembled an 1853 publication of Isaac Pitman, containing only 33 letters. However, at this point, Young was still enamored with the original Deseret alphabet, and so he rejected the proposal and Watt continued to publicly promote the alphabet as part of his job despite his reservations.

## Design criticism

Text from the [Book of Alma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Alma "Book of Alma")

Old Turkic inscription

The Deseret alphabet was purposely designed so as to not have [ascenders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascender_(typography) "Ascender (typography)") and [descenders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descender "Descender"). This was envisioned as a practical benefit for the alphabet in an era of metal type: after many uses, the edges of [type sorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sort_(typesetting) "Sort (typesetting)") become dull, and narrow ascenders and descenders are most prone to this effect.

While well intentioned, this lack has been described as a "catastrophic" mistake that makes type look "monotonous" and makes all words look alike. Some have joked that this aesthetic quality could cause the new alphabet to be mistaken from afar for a [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey") [tax list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ledger "Ledger").

The Mormon pioneers were apparently aware of the problems caused by its monotony:

> President Young has decided that [the letters] are not so well adapted for the purpose designed as it was hoped they would be. There being no shanks (ascenders or descenders) to the letters, all being very even, they are trying to the eye, because of their uniformity.

— article in the *Juvenile Instructor*, 2 October 1875

Other criticism of the design was harsher still. In an 18 December 1857 editorial in the *Boston Globe*, the alphabet was described as being "so arranged and named as to cause the greatest possible annoyance to outsiders" and the design of the letters as "incomprehensible as [...] the [hieroglyphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_hieroglyphs "Egyptian hieroglyphs") of the [...] [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt")." On 4 March 1872, *The New York Times* called the alphabet "rude, awkward and cumbersome".

Some modern computer fonts and printed books have attempted to correct this perceived fault: in the books in John Jenkins' *Deseret Alphabet Classics* series, the font used adds a descender to 𐑉 and 𐐻 and an ascender to 𐐼 and 𐑇 among other tweaks.

## Other motives

Officially, the Deseret alphabet was created to simplify the spelling of English words for the benefit of children and [English as a second language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_as_a_second_language "English as a second language") learners. Some of the alphabet's contemporaries, however, posited an alternative motivation for its development: increasing the [isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolationism "Isolationism") of the early Mormons.

### Restricting access to religious texts and internal communications

The charge that the Deseret alphabet's main purpose was to keep outsiders in the dark was brought almost immediately, as evidenced by the following 1858 *[Lyttelton Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyttelton_Times "Lyttelton Times")* reprint of an unnamed "New York newspaper":

> Mormon Secretiveness.—The new "Deseret Alphabet" is completed, and a fount of *[pica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_point-size_names "Traditional point-size names")* type has been cast in St. Louis. [Specimens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_specimen "Type specimen") of the type are published in the St. Louis papers, but they are unproducible in types that common people use [...] The [ukases](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/en:ukase "wikt:en:ukase") of Brother Brigham will hereafter be a sealed letter, literally, to Gentile eyes.

Having obtained a copy of the *Deseret News* in 1859, the *Richmond Dispatch* disparaged it on April 25, writing "The *Deseret News* is filled with a lot of hieroglyphs. It seems to be [an alphabet] which the Mormons alone are to be taught."

However, some modern historians doubt the veracity of this theory. For one, notes Kenneth R. Beesley, the *Deseret News* and every book published in the alphabet prominently features the key to the alphabet, and anyone without a key could have gotten a copy of *A Journey to Great-Salt-Lake City*, or traveled to Salt Lake City themselves and bought one. Contemporary scholars [Richard F. Burton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_F._Burton "Richard F. Burton") and [Jules Remy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Remy "Jules Remy") also dismissed the secrecy argument, in 1860 and 1855 respectively.

### Limitations on exposure to external publications among early Latter-day Saints

With the impending completion of the Transcontinental Railroad, Mormons would have easy, cheap access to publications from the east, including [yellowbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow-back "Yellow-back"), [penny dreadfuls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_dreadful "Penny dreadful"), [pulp magazines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_magazine "Pulp magazine"), and other, often scandalous or "dirty" publications, that were rising to prominence in the 19th century. In an article about the benefits of the alphabet, the *Deseret News* proudly wrote:

> If our community were situated as others are, it might be [Quixotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Quixote "Don Quixote") to attempt the introduction of this reform among us with the hope of carrying it into practical operation. But our position is unique, we are united. [...] Some have an idea that if a child be educated in the system of spelling and writing by sound it will be a detriment to it in learning the present system. [...] **If they could find no better reading than much of the miserable trash that now obtains extensive circulation, it would be better if they never learned to read the present orthography.** In such a case ignorance would be blissful. [...] The greatest evils which now flourish and under which Christendom groans are directly traceable to the licentiousness of the press.

— "The Deseret Alphabet—Its Advantages", 19 August 1868, *Deseret News*

In another article, the *Deseret News* cited an example of the kind of literature Mormons would benefit from not being able to read: *[The Police Gazette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Police_Gazette "The Police Gazette")*. Historians A. J. Simmonds and Roby Wentz contend that while this may have been a tertiary goal of the alphabet, a sort of "happy accident", the main purpose of it was simple orthographic reform. Simmonds notes that the teaching of English to foreigners was not a mere hypothetical to mask isolationist tendencies: 35% of the [Utah Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_Territory "Utah Territory")'s population at the time was [Scandinavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia "Scandinavia"), with [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"), [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language "Italian language") and [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language "Welsh language") speaking people also making up a considerable percentage of inhabitants; therefore, communication between the recently [baptized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism_in_Mormonism "Baptism in Mormonism") and the community was a real problem.

## Encodings

Between 1855 and 1859, the way most people wrote the letters 𐑏 and 𐑎 changed, causing encoding problems when attempting to transcribe documents using the latter glyphs with Unicode.

The Deseret alphabet (U+10400–U+1044F) was added to the [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode "Unicode") Standard in March 2001 with the release of version 3.1, after a request by John H. Jenkins of [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc. "Apple Inc."), making it one of the first scripts to be added outside of the [Basic Multilingual Plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Multilingual_Plane "Basic Multilingual Plane"). The letters 𐐧 (ew) and 𐐦 (oi) were added to the Unicode Standard in April 2003 with the release of version 4.0.

According to Kenneth R. Beesley, who submitted the proposal to expand the encoding, "Unicode fonts based on the current heterogeneous collection of [glyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyph "Glyph") will be useless for any practical [typesetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typesetting "Typesetting") of 40-letter Deseret Alphabet documents." This is because the Unicode Consortium chose to use glyphs from 1855 as the reference glyphs, while by 1859 those glyphs were already outmoded and replaced with newer glyphs. Beesley thus recommends using [LaTeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaTeX "LaTeX") along with his [Metafont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafont "Metafont") desalph font to typeset Deseret text, but [fonts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Font "Font") which use the alternate glyphs for the two codepoints in question would also work for transcription of 40-letter Deseret texts written during and after 1859.
