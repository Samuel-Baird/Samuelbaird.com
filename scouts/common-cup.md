# Scout: Historical LDS sacrament COMMON CUPS (shared vessels)

## OBJECTIVE
Each run, search current marketplace and auction listings for historical
Latter-day Saint sacrament COMMON CUPS — the single shared drinking vessels
used in LDS worship before the Church moved to individual cups (a shift that
began in 1911 and was largely complete by the 1910s–1920s). Report only NEW
listings found since the last run; skip anything already reported.

## WHAT I WANT (shared common cups only)
- Communal/shared vessels a whole congregation drank from, one cup passed along
  the pew.
- Material: ceramic, earthenware, stoneware, pottery, pewter, tin, brass,
  silver, or silver-plated. METAL OR CERAMIC ONLY.
- Likely forms: goblet, chalice, mug, tankard, handled cup, or simple
  domestic cup pressed into sacramental use.
- Period: roughly 1830s–1920s.
- Bonus markings: ward name, branch name, "Sacrament," or pioneer-era
  provenance.

## WHAT TO REJECT — DO NOT REPORT
- ANY individual/single-serving glass sacrament cups (the small ~1"–1.5" glass
  cups introduced in 1911 and after). These are the OPPOSITE of what I want.
  Reject on sight, even if listed as "antique," "rare," "Mormon," or "Utah."
- Individual disposable plastic/paper sacrament cups.
- Modern Catholic/Protestant church-supply chalices sold new.
- Generic antique cups with no LDS connection.
- Items claimed LDS with no supporting evidence.
- Reproductions and poorly photographed items.

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT FOR EVALUATION
The common-cup practice was inherited from Protestant communion tradition and
was standard LDS practice through the 19th century; the switch to individual
cups was driven partly by public-health concerns in the influenza era.
Ward-marked or engraved pieces are extremely rare — most vessels were ordinary
domestic tableware pressed into sacramental use, so don't expect ornate
chalices, and weight provenance heavily. Provenance documentation (family
journals, ward histories, photographs) dramatically raises credibility. Also
scan obituaries for past owners who may have held such a cup.
